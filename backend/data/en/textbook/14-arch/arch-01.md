#CHAPTER 1 | The Computer and Levels of Abstraction
Computer Architecture. Chapter 1. The Computer and Levels of Abstraction. Over the last half century, microprocessors have changed the world. Today a laptop is more powerful than a machine that once filled an entire room, and a modern car contains dozens of processors. This book will show what is inside. We will start with the physics of the transistor, build gates out of transistors, assemble memory, an adder, and an arithmetic unit out of gates, and then a whole processor that runs assembly programs. We will go from the bottom up, and every step can be checked by hand on the board.\n\nThe main difficulty is that a modern processor consists of billions of transistors. One transistor is easy to understand, a thousand is already hard, and nobody can hold a billion in their head. An engineer does not try to write down the equations of motion of every electron in every transistor. Instead, they manage complexity: they pick a suitable level of description and deliberately forget the details that do not matter at that level. This skill is exactly what separates a professional from an amateur.\n\nThe technique is called abstraction: we leave out everything that is not essential for the task at hand. A politician running for president does not need to see individual neighborhoods; a map by district is enough. A census bureau, on the other hand, counts the residents of every town and works at the very lowest level. Both need the truth, but at different scales. It is the same in a computer: every specialist has their own level, and they do not have to dig deeper as long as everything works.\n\nFor an electronic computer we usually distinguish several levels of abstraction. At the bottom is physics, which studies the motion of electrons. Above it are semiconductor devices: transistors and diodes. Then come analog circuits, where voltage varies continuously, and digital circuits, where only two levels remain. Next is logic: gates, adders, memory. Above that sit microarchitecture, then architecture, the operating system, and finally application programs. Our course covers the path from digital circuits up to architecture.\n\nThe difference between architecture and microarchitecture is important. Architecture describes the computer through the eyes of a programmer: which instructions and registers exist. Microarchitecture is how the blocks that execute those instructions are built from gates. One architecture can be implemented in different ways, with different trade-offs between price, speed, and power consumption. The Core i7, the 80486, and the Athlon all understand the same x86 instructions, but inside they are built completely differently.\n\nThe second tool for managing complexity is design discipline: the designer deliberately restricts the choices available in order to be more productive at a higher level. A classic example is interchangeable parts. Muskets used to be made by hand, and every part was fitted individually. When a standard set of parts with tight tolerances was introduced, muskets could be assembled and repaired much faster. In electronics the same discipline means using digital circuits wherever possible: they are simpler, and systems built from them outperform analog ones.\n\nBesides abstraction and discipline, there are three principles: hierarchy, modularity, and regularity. Hierarchy: a system is divided into modules, and each module into smaller parts, until the behavior of each part is easy to understand. Modularity: a module has a clearly defined function and interface, and when connected to others it causes no side effects. Regularity: we use the same standard blocks many times instead of inventing something new each time. We will meet these three principles at every step.\n\nDigital abstraction means that information is stored not in a continuous quantity but in variables with a finite set of values. One of the first digital machines was Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, in which each gear could sit in one of ten positions. Electronic computers use only two values: high voltage is one, low voltage is zero. The amount of information in a variable with N states equals the base-two logarithm of N. A binary variable carries exactly one bit.\n\nDigital abstraction frees the designer from physics. They do not care what represents the zeros and ones: voltage, rotating gears, or the level of a liquid. A programmer, too, can write code without knowing how the hardware works. But knowing the hardware gives you more: you can tailor code to a particular processor, understand where limitations come from, and see why the same program runs at different speeds on different machines. That is why it is worth knowing at least the neighboring levels of abstraction.\n\nHow the course is organized. Chapters one through three are the introduction, the physics of the transistor, and standards. Chapters four and five cover how numbers and text are written in bits. From chapter six through nine we build digital logic: gates, multiplexers, latches, and adders. Chapters ten through fourteen cover registers, the arithmetic unit, memory, and the whole processor. Chapters fifteen through seventeen are about assembly programming. The final chapters explain how the cache, the pipeline, and parallel systems work. Every chapter has slides, questions, and practice.
