#CHAPTER 11 | The Arithmetic Logic Unit
Computer Architecture. Chapter 11. The Arithmetic Logic Unit. The arithmetic logic unit, the ALU, is the calculator of the processor. It receives two operands and an operation code, performs the required action, and outputs the result together with several flags that describe that result. Everything a processor can do: add, subtract, perform bitwise operations, shift numbers and compare, is ultimately computed by the ALU. In this chapter we will build a two-bit ALU piece by piece and see where branch conditions come from.\n\nThe simplest ALU knows just two operations: AND and OR. The secret is that both operations are computed at the same time, always, and a multiplexer chooses which of the results to send to the output. The operation code, op, is the selector of the multiplexer. This organization, compute everything and pick what you need, is typical for processors: the extra computations cost nothing in time, because the gates work in parallel, while the choice is made quickly and simply.\n\nLet us add operations. A two-bit code selects one of four logic operations: zero-zero is AND, zero-one is OR, one-zero is exclusive OR, one-one is NOT of A, and B is ignored. Now we need a four-to-one multiplexer. The operations are bitwise: each bit of the result depends only on the corresponding bits of the operands. So for a multi-bit word the circuit is simply repeated for each bit, the principle of regularity in action.\n\nThe arithmetic block is the adder-subtractor from chapter nine. Mode zero gives A plus B, mode one gives A minus B, that is, A plus not B plus one. The carry-out shows that the sum did not fit. In subtraction it means the opposite: a one on the output says that there was no borrow, that is, A is not less than B. Two special actions are added to this block, increment and decrement, adding and subtracting one with wraparound: three plus one gives zero, and zero minus one gives three.\n\nThe third block is the shifter. A shift left by one position multiplies the number by two, and a shift right divides by two. The bit that falls off the edge of the word during the shift is not lost without a trace: it goes to the carry output. A right shift of the two-bit number zero-one gives zero-zero and puts a one into the carry, the very one that slipped off the edge. Shifts will come in handy when we multiply by powers of two and work with individual bits.\n\nNow let us assemble one bit slice of the ALU. It has inputs A, B and the carry-in, as well as a two-bit operation code: add, subtract, AND, OR. Inside is one full adder with an XOR gate on the B input, which inverts B during subtraction, and next to it are AND and OR gates and a multiplexer that picks one of the results. The outputs are the result bit and the carry. For logic operations the carry is zero. Such a slice is replicated, and we get an ALU of any width.\n\nTwo such slices, connected by a carry chain, make a two-bit ALU. A two-bit code selects the operation: add, subtract, AND, OR. The result is computed modulo four, that is, the top carry is discarded. Take A equal to two and B equal to one. Addition gives three, subtraction gives one, AND gives zero, OR gives three. All four results are computed in parallel, and the multiplexer delivers the one needed. You will be able to build such an ALU piece by piece in the practice section of this chapter.\n\nBesides the result, the ALU outputs flags. The Z flag means the result is zero. The N flag means the result is negative: it is the top bit in two's complement. The C flag is the carry out of the top bit. The V flag is overflow: for signed numbers the result did not fit into the width. Sometimes there is also a parity flag: the number of ones in the result is even. Flags are not needed for the computations themselves, but they are exactly what lets a program make decisions.\n\nHow does a processor compare numbers? It subtracts them and looks at the flags. If the result is zero, the numbers are equal. If, for unsigned numbers, a borrow occurred in the subtraction, that is, the carry is zero, the first number is less than the second. For signed numbers the comparison is slightly subtler: we must account for overflow, and less than means that the sign of the result and the overflow differ. The subtraction itself writes its result nowhere: only the flags are needed. Such an instruction is called a compare.\n\nFinally, the flags produce the branch condition. A two-bit condition code selects which check to perform: equal is the zero flag, not equal is its negation, less than is the exclusive OR of the sign and overflow, less than or equal is the same plus the zero flag. The output says whether the condition holds. This signal goes into the program counter circuit and decides whether to make the jump. This is how arithmetic turns into control: the program gains the ability to branch.
