#CHAPTER 4 | Numbers in a computer
Computer Architecture. Chapter 4. Numbers in a computer. In the first chapters we found out that all information inside the machine is bits, zeros and ones. Now let us see how numbers are built from bits: unsigned integers, negative numbers and fractions. A great deal depends on this. Why does the sum of two positive numbers suddenly turn out negative? Why does zero point one plus zero point two not equal zero point three? Why is the same file read differently on different machines? Today we will go through positional number systems, binary and hexadecimal notation, two's complement for negative numbers, bitwise operations, the floating-point format and byte order.\n\nWe are used to the decimal system: a number has digit positions, and each digit is multiplied by a power of ten. The binary system works the same way, only the base is two, and there are just two digits. The number thirteen in binary looks like one, one, zero, one: eight plus four plus zero plus one. To convert a number to binary, you just keep dividing it by two and writing down the remainders: they give you the digits from the lowest to the highest. The reverse conversion is a sum of powers of two.\n\nBinary numbers are long and awkward for people, so programmers use the hexadecimal system. Its base is sixteen, with digits from zero to nine and letters from A to F. The main advantage: one hexadecimal digit corresponds to exactly four bits, a nibble. So a byte is written with two digits, and converting between binary and hexadecimal needs no calculation, just groups of four bits. That is why memory addresses, web colors and file contents are usually shown in hexadecimal. A color with a red value of two hundred fifty-five, green one hundred thirty-six and blue zero is written as hash F F eight eight zero zero.\n\nNumbers in a processor have a fixed length: eight, sixteen, thirty-two or sixty-four bits. An unsigned eight-bit number can take values from zero to two hundred fifty-five. What happens if you add ten to two hundred fifty? The result does not fit into eight bits, the top bit is lost, and you get four. This is overflow, and it is not a bug, it is arithmetic modulo two to the power of the width. If you subtract one from zero, you get two hundred fifty-five. That is how counters wrap around, like the hands of a clock reaching the end.\n\nHow do we write a negative number? The most obvious way is to give one bit to the sign and leave the rest for the magnitude. This is sign-magnitude. It is easy to understand, but it has two drawbacks. First, you get two zeros: plus zero and minus zero, and the processor would have to compare both. Second, addition needs different circuits depending on the signs: add, subtract, compare magnitudes. Engineers wanted the adder from the first chapter to add any numbers without branching. For that, a different representation was invented, and all modern processors use it.\n\nIt is called two's complement. The idea: the negative number minus x is written as two to the power of the width minus x. A practical way to get it: invert all the bits of the number and add one. So minus five in eight bits turns into one one one one one zero one one, which is two hundred fifty-one. The top bit still shows the sign, but now it enters the number with a negative weight. The range of eight bits goes from minus one hundred twenty-eight to plus one hundred twenty-seven. There is only one zero, and negative numbers have one more value than positive ones.\n\nThe main beauty of two's complement is that addition is the same for signed and unsigned numbers. The same adder adds the bits, and we only interpret the result differently. Subtraction turns into addition of a negative number: invert the second number, add one, and add. True, signed overflow remains: if you add one hundred and one hundred in eight bits, you get two hundred, which as a signed number is minus fifty-six. The sign of such overflow: two operands of the same sign, and a result of the opposite one. The processor remembers it in a special flag.\n\nA separate set of operations works with bits directly. Bitwise AND with a mask keeps the bits you need, bitwise OR turns bits on, XOR toggles them, and NOT inverts them. This is how flags work: one number holds a set of independent switches, and a mask with a one in the right position lets you set, clear, toggle or test a particular bit. Drivers, network protocols, file permissions and graphics libraries use these tricks all the time, because they are cheap in memory and execute instantly.\n\nShifts move bits along the number. Shifting left by one position multiplies the number by two, and shifting left by three multiplies it by eight. Shifting right divides by a power of two, discarding the remainder. For signed numbers we distinguish a logical shift, where zeros appear on the left, from an arithmetic shift, where the sign bit is copied: so minus eight after a right shift becomes minus four, and not a big positive number. Compilers happily replace multiplication and division by powers of two with shifts, because a shift is simpler and faster for the processor than multiplication.\n\nAnd what about fractions? You can store a fixed-point number: an integer that we have agreed to count in fractions, for example in units of one two-hundred-fifty-sixth. This is how embedded systems work, and money stored in cents. But more often floating-point numbers are used, following the IEEE seven fifty-four standard. A number is split into three fields: sign, exponent and mantissa. The value equals the sign times one point mantissa times two to the power of the exponent. In the thirty-two-bit format, one bit is given to the sign, eight to the exponent and twenty-three to the mantissa, and the exponent is stored with a bias of one hundred twenty-seven.\n\nFloating point gives a huge range, but its numbers are approximate. Zero point one in binary is an infinite fraction, like one third in decimal, so it is stored with an error. The sum of zero point one and zero point two turns out slightly greater than zero point three, and an equality comparison gives false. Such numbers are compared with a tolerance. There are also special values: infinity, which appears on overflow, and not-a-number, which results, for example, from subtracting infinity from infinity and is not equal even to itself. Large integers also lose precision: beyond two to the power of fifty-three, neighboring numbers can no longer be told apart.\n\nFinally, byte order. A thirty-two-bit number takes four bytes in memory, and we have to decide which of them comes first. The order from most significant to least significant is called big-endian, and it is used in network protocols. The order from least significant to most significant is called little-endian, and that is how Intel processors and most ARM processors work. The hexadecimal number twelve, thirty-four, fifty-six, seventy-eight lies in memory exactly like that in the first case, and backwards in the second. When data goes over the network or into a file, it is important to agree on the order in advance.\n\nLet us sum up. Any number is stored in a fixed number of bits, so arithmetic is actually done modulo something. Hexadecimal notation is convenient because one digit equals four bits. Negative numbers are written in two's complement: invert and add one, and then a single adder works for everyone. Bitwise operations and shifts are the basis of flags and fast computation. Fractional numbers are stored approximately in the floating-point format, and byte order must be agreed upon. In the next chapter we will talk about how characters are made from numbers and how to protect data from errors.
