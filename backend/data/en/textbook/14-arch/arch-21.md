#CHAPTER 21 | I/O, buses, and disks
Computer Architecture. Chapter 21. I/O, buses, and disks. So far we have studied the processor and memory, but a computer without the outside world is useless: it needs a keyboard and a screen, a network, and above all persistent storage for data and programs. I/O devices are thousands and millions of times slower than the processor, and the whole architecture of input and output is devoted to making sure they don't slow down the fast parts of the machine. Today we'll look at buses and their bandwidth, accessing devices through memory addresses, polling and interrupts, direct memory access, the design of hard drives and solid-state drives, disk request scheduling, redundant disk arrays, and the role of buffering.\n\nDevices are connected to the processor and memory by buses: sets of wires with shared rules of exchange. The address bus says who we are talking to, the data bus carries the values, and the control lines set the direction and the timing. The main characteristic of a bus is its bandwidth: the width in bytes multiplied by the number of transfers per second. A sixty-four-bit memory bus at three thousand two hundred million transfers per second gives twenty-five point six gigabytes per second. Modern buses are not shared wires for everybody, but serial links like PCI Express, where each device is connected by its own lanes.\n\nHow does the processor talk to a device? The most common way is called memory-mapped I/O. Every device has a few registers: data, status, and control. These registers get addresses in the same address space as main memory. Ordinary read and write instructions at these addresses go not to memory but to the device: writing a byte to the data register of a serial port sends a character out. Older processors, for example the x86, also had a separate port space and special input and output instructions, but the idea is the same.\n\nA device is not always ready: you have to wait until a key is pressed, until a packet arrives, until the disk spins around. The simplest way to wait is called polling. The processor reads the status register in a loop and checks the ready bit. This is very easy to implement, and the reaction time is minimal. But while the loop spins, the processor does nothing useful: if the device responds after a thousand cycles, a thousand cycles are wasted. Polling is fine for rare and very fast events, but when the wait is long, we need a different mechanism.\n\nThis mechanism is called an interrupt. The device itself signals the processor when it is ready. The processor finishes the current instruction, saves its state, that is, the program counter and flags, and uses the interrupt number to find the handler's address in the vector table. The handler does its work and returns control with a return-from-interrupt instruction, after which the interrupted program carries on as if nothing had happened. The timer, the keyboard, the network card, the disk, and even processor errors, such as a page fault, all use this single scheme. The vector table lets the kernel plug in its own handlers.\n\nInterrupts are not free. Saving the state, switching modes, running the handler, and returning cost thousands of cycles, and the caches and the pipeline get disturbed in the process. If events arrive rarely, interrupts are better than polling. But if a network card receives hundreds of thousands of packets per second, almost all of the processor's time would go to interrupts. So high-load drivers switch to a hybrid: while traffic is low, they work by interrupts, and as the load grows, they turn interrupts off and poll the device in batches. A direct comparison of the cost of the two schemes shows the point where one becomes better than the other.\n\nCopying large blocks of data with the processor is inefficient: it has to read every byte from the device and write it to memory. For this, direct memory access, DMA, was invented. The processor just gives the DMA controller the address in memory, the block size, and the direction, and goes off to do other things. The controller transfers the data over the bus by itself and sends a single interrupt when finished. The setup overhead is hundreds of cycles regardless of the block size, while copying a few thousand bytes with the processor would take many thousands of cycles. All disks, network cards, and graphics cards work through DMA.\n\nLet's look at how a hard drive is built. It is a stack of spinning platters and a head that moves along the radius. To read data, the head has to be moved to the right track; this is the seek time, on average about eight milliseconds. Then we wait for the right sector to come under it: on average half a revolution. At seven thousand two hundred revolutions per minute that is four point one seven milliseconds. The data transfer itself takes a negligible amount of time. In total, one random access costs about twelve milliseconds, so the disk performs fewer than a hundred random operations per second. Sequential reading is faster by several orders of magnitude.\n\nSince moving the head is so expensive, disk requests are ordered. The simplest policy serves them in order of arrival, but the head dashes back and forth. The shortest-seek policy picks the request closest to the current position, which reduces movement but may forget about distant requests for a long time. The elevator algorithm scans: it moves in one direction, serving all requests along the way, and then turns around. A classic example, with requests to tracks ninety-eight, one hundred eighty-three, thirty-seven, and others, starting at position fifty-three, shows this: arrival order gives six hundred forty tracks of travel, the closest request gives two hundred thirty-six.\n\nA solid-state drive has no moving parts: data is stored in flash memory cells. Its peculiarity is that you can write a page, but you can erase only a whole block of hundreds of pages, and the number of erasures is limited. If you updated a page in place, you would have to rewrite the entire block. So the controller keeps a mapping table and writes changed data to a new free spot, marking the old one as stale, and later collects the garbage and erases whole blocks. Thanks to this, random access takes tens of microseconds, hundreds of times faster than a disk, and spreading erasures evenly prolongs the life of the drive.\n\nDisks fail, and data must not be lost. A RAID array combines several disks. A mirror keeps two copies on different disks. A parity scheme, for example RAID five, stores data in blocks across several disks, and on one more disk a check block equal to the XOR of the others. If a disk fails, its contents are restored by XORing all the remaining blocks, because the XOR of any value with itself is zero. Such an array survives the loss of one disk at the cost of one disk of capacity. But RAID is not a replacement for backups: it won't save you from a user mistake or a virus.\n\nFinally, exchange with devices is always done in blocks and through buffers. A system call is expensive, so writing ten thousand bytes one at a time would turn into ten thousand calls, while in blocks of four kilobytes it takes just three. The operating system keeps a page cache of the disk in memory, merges and orders requests, and the driver hides the differences between devices behind a single interface: in Unix, almost everything looks like a file that can be read and written. Language libraries also buffer output, which is why text sometimes appears on the screen with a delay, until the buffer is flushed.\n\nLet's sum up. Buses connect the processor, memory, and devices, and their speed is determined by width and frequency. Devices are accessible through registers in the address space. Polling is simple but wasteful, interrupts free the processor but are costly, and under high load they are replaced by a hybrid. Direct memory access takes the copying off the processor. A hard drive is slow because of its mechanics, so requests are ordered, while a solid-state drive is faster thanks to flash memory and its controller. RAID protects against disk failure, and buffering reduces the number of costly operations. In the next chapter we'll move on to parallelism: multicore processors and cache coherence.
