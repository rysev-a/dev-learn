#CHAPTER 19 | Pipelining and performance
Computer Architecture. Chapter 19. Pipelining and performance. We know that a processor executes an instruction in several steps: fetch, decode, execute, memory access, and write-back of the result. But if we wait for an instruction to pass through all the steps before starting the next one, most of the circuitry sits idle. A pipeline lets the work overlap: while one instruction is executing, the next is already being decoded, and a third is being fetched from memory. Today we'll look at how a pipeline works and why it speeds up the processor, what difficulties come from dependencies and branches, how they are handled with bubbles, forwarding, and branch prediction, what superscalar execution is, how to estimate speedup with Amdahl's law, and why you can't measure speed by clock frequency alone.\n\nLet's begin with a formula known as the iron law of performance. The running time of a program equals the number of instructions, multiplied by the average number of cycles per instruction, divided by the clock frequency. Every speedup technique acts on one of these three factors. Compilers and good algorithms reduce the instruction count, the pipeline and the cache reduce the cycles per instruction, and manufacturing technology raises the frequency. Improving just one factor often makes another one worse: for example, adding pipeline stages lets you raise the frequency, but makes every mistake more expensive. So you always have to look at the product.\n\nAn analogy with a laundromat helps to understand the pipeline. You need to wash, dry, and iron several loads of laundry. If you wait for one load to go through all three stages before starting the next, each one takes three hours. But if, as soon as the first load moves to the dryer, you put the second one into the washing machine, then after the initial delay a finished load comes out every hour. The time for a single instruction hasn't gotten any shorter, but the throughput has grown. In the classic five-stage pipeline, n instructions need five plus n minus one cycles instead of five times n: for large n that is almost a fivefold speedup.\n\nIt's convenient to draw the process as a table: cycles go along the horizontal axis, instructions along the vertical one, and the cells hold the names of the stages. Each next instruction is shifted one cycle to the right of the previous one. You can see that from the fifth cycle on, five instructions are in the pipeline at once, one in each stage, and all five blocks of the processor are busy. An ideal pipeline finishes one instruction per cycle, so the CPI is equal to one. But the ideal is rarely achieved, because instructions depend on each other, and a program sometimes changes the order of execution. These situations are called hazards.\n\nThere are three kinds of hazards. A structural hazard occurs when two instructions want the same hardware at the same time, for example memory: that's why processors have separate caches for instructions and data. A data hazard occurs when an instruction needs the result of the previous one, which isn't ready yet. The most common case is called read after write: one instruction writes a register, and the next one reads it. A control hazard occurs at branches: the processor doesn't yet know which instruction to fetch next. Of the three kinds, data and control hazards call for the most complex solutions, so let's discuss them in more detail.\n\nThe simplest way to resolve a data hazard is to wait. The processor inserts bubbles, empty cycles, into the pipeline until the result appears in the register. In a five-stage pipeline without forwarding, an instruction that depends on the one right before it loses two cycles, and one that depends on the instruction two back loses one cycle. That's expensive: if almost every second instruction depends on the previous one, the pipeline turns into a slow queue. So forwarding was invented, also called bypassing. The result of an arithmetic operation is fed straight from the ALU output back to the ALU input for the next instruction, without waiting for it to be written to the register. With forwarding, arithmetic dependencies cost nothing.\n\nThere is one case that forwarding can't solve: a load from memory. The data becomes known only after the memory access stage, so an instruction that uses the loaded value right in the next slot still loses one cycle. This is called a load-use hazard. Compilers fight it by moving independent instructions between the load and its use: they fill the pause with useful work. If an instruction that can be placed in the gap is found, the program runs one cycle faster. That is why the quality of machine code depends heavily on knowing the pipeline, and compilers take into account the design of specific processors.\n\nBranches create a control hazard. Until a conditional instruction has computed its condition, the processor doesn't know where to take the next instruction from. Stopping and waiting is too expensive: branches make up every fifth instruction. So the processor guesses and keeps executing instructions based on the guess. If the guess is right, nothing is lost; if not, all the instructions started by mistake are thrown away, and the pipeline is refilled. The penalty equals the number of stages between fetch and the condition check, usually from three to twenty cycles. Suppose twenty percent of instructions are branches, ten percent of them are mispredicted, and the penalty is three cycles: the CPI grows from one to one point oh six.\n\nModern branch predictors are very accurate, but the principle behind them is simple. The simplest one remembers the last outcome of a branch and predicts the same thing again. A two-bit counter is better: it moves from strongly not taken to strongly taken, and changes its prediction only after two misses in a row. For a loop that almost always jumps back, a one-bit predictor is wrong twice per run, while a two-bit one is wrong only once. More advanced predictors take into account the history of several recent branches and reach accuracy of over ninety-five percent. Because of this, talk about the cost of branching in modern processors is about unpredictable conditions, not about the mere fact of a branch.\n\nCan we finish more than one instruction per cycle? Yes: superscalar processors have several execution units, and each cycle they launch up to four, six, or more independent instructions. To do this, there is a scheduler inside that picks the ready instructions, not necessarily in the order they were written: this is out-of-order execution. The results, however, are committed strictly in order, so that the program behaves as usual. The gain is limited by dependencies: if instructions are lined up in a chain, then no matter how many units there are, a chain of four instructions takes four cycles. This length is called the critical path.\n\nAmdahl's law describes how speeding up part of a program affects the overall speed. If the fraction of time that can be sped up is P, and that part is made S times faster, then the overall speedup equals one divided by the quantity one minus P plus P over S. If you speed up ninety percent of a program by a factor of ten, you get only five and a quarter, and even with an infinite speedup you get only ten. The remaining ten percent becomes the bottleneck. The law teaches us: first find the main share of the time, and then speed up exactly that, instead of fussing over small things.\n\nHow do we compare processors? Clock frequency and millions of instructions per second, MIPS, are misleading. A processor may execute more instructions per second but need twice as many instructions for the same task because its instruction set is simpler. A fair comparison is possible only by the running time of real programs: for this, people use benchmark suites, for example SPEC and Geekbench. Any measurement should be repeated, with caches warmed up and the variance taken into account, and conclusions should be drawn about specific workloads, not in general. Speed on one task is no guarantee of speed on another.\n\nLet's sum up. The time of a program is determined by the number of instructions, the cycles per instruction, and the frequency. A pipeline overlaps the stages of different instructions and brings the CPI close to one. Structural, data, and control hazards create bubbles; some of them are removed by forwarding and instruction reordering, while branch mispredictions are costly. Superscalar processors execute several instructions per cycle, but are limited by the critical path of dependencies. Amdahl's law shows that the speedup of a part of a program is limited by the remaining share. And processors should be compared by the running time of real tasks.
