#CHAPTER 2 | The Transistor and Digital Abstraction
Computer Architecture. Chapter 2. The Transistor and Digital Abstraction. Babbage's Analytical Engine was mechanical, and the first computers were built from relays and vacuum tubes. Modern ones run on transistors: they are cheap, small, and reliable. A transistor is a switch that is turned on and off by the voltage on a control terminal. In nineteen fifty-eight Jack Kilby built the first integrated circuit out of two transistors, and today about a billion fit on a square centimeter of silicon. Transistors are made all at once on an entire silicon wafer, so each one costs a fraction of a microcent.\n\nTransistors are made of silicon, an element found in sand and rock. A silicon atom has four valence electrons, and in the crystal lattice all of them are busy forming bonds with neighbors, so pure silicon conducts current poorly. But if you add a little impurity, the situation changes. Arsenic provides an extra free electron; that is an n-type impurity. Boron, on the contrary, creates a hole, a place where a neighbor's electron can move; that is a p-type impurity. By varying the amount of impurity, conductivity can be changed by orders of magnitude, which is why silicon is called a semiconductor.\n\nIf you join a p-type region to an n-type region, you get a diode. When the voltage on the anode is higher than on the cathode, the diode is open and current flows from the anode to the cathode, but not in the opposite direction. Another important device is the capacitor: two conductors separated by an insulator. It stores charge, and its capacitance equals the ratio of charge to voltage. Any conductor on a chip is a tiny capacitor. The larger the capacitance, the more time and energy it takes to charge it.\n\nNow the transistor itself. A MOSFET, a metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor, is a sandwich: a conducting gate on top, a thin layer of insulator, silicon oxide, beneath it, and a silicon substrate under that. At the sides, in the substrate, are two regions with the opposite type of impurity: the source and the drain. While the gate is at zero, there is no conducting path between the source and the drain. But if you apply enough voltage to the gate, the electric field pulls electrons under the oxide and forms a channel: the source connects to the drain, and current flows. The transistor is controlled by a field, hence the name field-effect.\n\nThere are two types of transistors. In an n-MOS transistor, current flows when the gate is at one, and in a p-MOS transistor it is the opposite: current flows when the gate is at zero. Unfortunately, they switch asymmetrically: n-MOS passes a zero well but a one poorly, so the drain ends up slightly below the supply voltage. P-MOS, on the other hand, passes a one well and a zero poorly. That is why CMOS, complementary technology, puts transistors of both types on one chip, and each does what it does best. Today almost all chips are made in CMOS.\n\nThe simplest CMOS circuit is the inverter. Between the supply and the output sits a p-MOS, between the output and ground sits an n-MOS, and their gates are shared and connected to the input. When the input is zero, the p-MOS is on and the n-MOS is off: the output is pulled up to the supply, that is, to one. When the input is one, it is the other way around: the n-MOS is open, and the output is pulled down to zero. One of the two transistors is always off, so in the steady state no current flows from the supply to ground.\n\nOther gates are built in the same way. In a NAND gate, two n-MOS transistors are in series: the output connects to ground only if both are on. And two p-MOS transistors are in parallel: one is enough to pull the output up to the supply. The rule of conduction complements applies: if the transistors in one network are in series, in the other they are in parallel. Then exactly one network is on at any moment. If both were open, we would get a short circuit, and if both were closed, the output would be connected to nothing.\n\nNow let us step outside the ideal picture. The lowest voltage in the system is called ground, and the highest is called the supply voltage. Transistors in the seventies ran on five volts; today the supply is about one volt. Voltage is a continuous quantity, and we have to decide which voltage counts as zero and which as one. For this we introduce logic levels. The driver guarantees that its output is a low voltage no higher than a certain threshold, or a high voltage no lower than a threshold. The receiver treats anything below its lower threshold as zero and anything above its upper threshold as one. Between them lies the forbidden zone.\n\nTo keep noise from corrupting the signal, we need a margin. The driver's output level for one must be higher than the receiver's input threshold for one, and the level for zero must be lower than the threshold for zero. The difference is called the noise margin. For five-volt CMOS the margin for zero is about one volt, and for one a little under seven tenths of a volt. The principle of static discipline requires: if the inputs are valid, the gate must produce valid outputs. If all gates obey this, we can forget the analog details and simply think in zeros and ones.\n\nThe last question is energy. Every gate and every wire has capacitance, and it has to be recharged at every switch. Dynamic power equals one half times the capacitance, times the square of the supply voltage, times the switching frequency. That is why lowering the voltage pays off so well: it enters as a square! There is also static power: even at rest a small leakage current flows through the transistors, and the power equals the leakage current times the voltage. Power consumption determines how long a phone lasts on its battery and how hot the processor gets.
