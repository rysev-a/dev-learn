#CHAPTER 10 | Registers and the Clock
Computer Architecture. Chapter 10. Registers and the Clock. In the last chapter we got the latch, and it turned out to have a serious drawback: while the enable is open, the latch is transparent, and values rush through it without stopping. In a large circuit, where hundreds of blocks pass data to each other, this creates races: nobody knows which of the signals will arrive first. The solution was invented long ago: synchronize everything with a common signal, the clock, and store data in elements that update only on a clock edge. Such elements are called flip-flops, and circuits built from them are called synchronous.\n\nThe clock is a periodic signal that alternately rises to one and falls to zero. The time of one full cycle is called the period, and the number of cycles per second is the frequency: it equals one divided by the period. A frequency of three gigahertz means three billion cycles per second, that is, a period of a little more than a third of a nanosecond. The most important moment of the clock is the edge, the transition from zero to one. At the moment of the edge, all the flip-flops in the system simultaneously capture the values from their inputs and hold them until the next edge.\n\nFirst let us see what happens if a latch is driven by the clock. Let the enable be the clock signal itself. While the clock is one, the latch is transparent and follows its input, and when the clock falls to zero, it remembers the last value. There is also the opposite polarity: the latch is transparent when the clock is zero. You will build both versions on the board. But such a latch still lets through any change on the input while the clock is high, and we would like to take the value only at the moment of the edge.\n\nFor that, two latches are connected in series. The first one, the master, is transparent while the clock is zero, and simply accepts the new value. The second one, the slave, is transparent when the clock is one. At the moment of the edge, the master closes and remembers what is on its input, and the slave opens and passes this value to the output. While the clock stays at one, the master is closed and does not react to changes of the input, and the slave is transparent but keeps receiving the same value. The result is an edge-triggered D flip-flop.\n\nA very simple and useful circuit appears if you connect the inverted output of a flip-flop to its own input. On every edge it switches to the opposite value. Such a circuit is called a divide-by-two: the output frequency is half the clock frequency. If the output of the first flip-flop is fed to the clock of the next one, we get a divide-by-four, and so on. This is the principle on which counters used to be built, and digital clocks divide the frequency of a quartz crystal down to one hertz with a whole chain of such flip-flops.\n\nFor a register to be able not only to store but also to accept a new value on command, a multiplexer is placed in front of the flip-flop input. When the write signal is zero, the flip-flop's own output is fed back to its input, and the register keeps the old value. When the write signal is one, new data arrives at the input and is remembered on the nearest edge. If we add an AND gate with an inverted clear signal, we get a synchronous reset: the zero is written on the edge, not instantly.\n\nFrom several such flip-flops with a common clock we build a multi-bit register: four flip-flops store a four-bit word. If the output of each flip-flop is connected to the input of the next, we get a shift register: on every edge, all the bits move one position. A single one gradually runs along the register. A shift left is equivalent to multiplying by two, and a shift right to dividing. Shift registers turn serial data into parallel and back, and serial ports, for example, are built on them.\n\nA counter is a register whose value increases by one on every clock cycle. Its output goes through an incrementer back to the input, and the register updates on the edge. By adding an enable, we get a counter that can be stopped. An interesting variant is the Johnson counter: a shift register into which the inverted output is fed back around the loop. A two-bit one goes through four states, and in general the number of states is twice the number of flip-flops. The program counter of a processor is built the same way.\n\nIf the output of an adder is returned to one of its inputs through a register, we get an accumulator. On each clock cycle it adds a new value to the accumulated sum. If we feed it the numbers three, four and two, the accumulator successively holds three, seven and nine. This is the loop that in programs looks like sum plus assignment, and the circuit made of a register and an adder lies at the heart of the arithmetic unit of any processor. It is on the accumulator that we will build the first processor.\n\nOne question remains: why can't we make the clock arbitrarily fast? Between two registers lies a combinational circuit, and the signal must have time to pass through it. The clock period must be no less than the sum of three times: the flip-flop's delay from the edge to the output, the delay of the logic itself, and the setup time that the flip-flop requires before the next edge. The longest path in the circuit sets the maximum frequency, so designers constantly break long paths up with registers. This idea will lead us to the pipeline.
