#CHAPTER 6 | Logic gates
Computer Architecture. Chapter 6. Logic gates. We found out that a transistor is a controllable switch, and zero and one are two voltage levels. Now we will build from switches the first circuits that can make decisions: signals come in, and a result comes out. We will learn to read a truth table, write it as a formula, and turn the formula into a circuit of gates. You will assemble these same circuits by hand on the board in this chapter's practice: from a plain wire and an inverter to a multiplexer.\n\nA logic gate is a circuit with one or more inputs and a single output. Both the inputs and the output are only zero or one. What appears on the output for each set of inputs is fully described by a truth table: it has one row for each combination. A circuit with one input has two rows, with two inputs four, with three inputs eight. Each new input doubles the table, which is why large circuits are described with formulas rather than by listing.\n\nThe simplest gate is NOT, also known as the inverter. One input, one output, and the output is always the opposite of the input: zero in, one out, and the other way around. In CMOS technology it is two transistors. The upper one, a P-channel, pulls the output up to the supply, the lower one, an N-channel, pulls it down to ground, and exactly one of them is always open. The formula of the inverter: Y equals A with a bar over it, that is, not A.\n\nThe AND gate gives a one only when there is a one on all inputs. It is like two switches connected one after another: current flows only if both are closed. The formula resembles multiplication: Y equals A times B. If at least one input is zero, the output is zero too. So AND works like a lock: a zero on one input shuts off the signal from the other.\n\nThe OR gate gives a one if there is a one on at least one input. These are two switches connected in parallel: closing either one is enough for the current to flow. The formula resembles addition: Y equals A plus B, except that one plus one stays one here. OR collects several causes together: the alarm goes off if any sensor is triggered.\n\nIf you put NOT after AND, you get NAND, and after OR, you get NOR. In CMOS these are the natural ones: NAND needs four transistors, while a plain AND takes six. NAND has a remarkable property: you can build any logic circuit from it alone. Connect both inputs together and you get NOT. Put another NAND, wired as NOT, after a NAND, and you get AND. That is why NAND is called the universal gate.\n\nHow do we turn a truth table into a circuit? We take each row where the output is one and write down the product of the inputs for it; inputs that are zero in that row are taken with negation. Then we add up all such products. This is called a sum of products, and this method always works. Exclusive OR has two rows with a one: A without B and B without A. The formula: Y equals A times not B, plus not A times B.\n\nA gate can be used as a controllable tap. The enable circuit: Y equals A times E. While E equals one, signal A passes to the output unchanged, and when E equals zero, the output freezes at zero. The inhibit circuit is the opposite: Y equals A times not E, and a one on E closes the tap. Such taps are everywhere: this is exactly how a processor decides which of the signals to let onto the shared bus.\n\nExclusive OR gives a one when the inputs differ, and its negation gives a one when the inputs match. So XNOR is a comparison of two bits for equality, and XOR is the sum of two bits modulo two: exactly the bit you get when adding without a carry. We will meet it in the adder. Another useful circuit is majority of three: the output is one if at least two of the inputs are ones. Such a circuit is called a majority circuit, and it is needed where you have to filter out a random error in one of three channels.\n\nA multiplexer chooses one of several inputs. The selector S says which of the signals, A or B, to pass to the output: Y equals not S times A, plus S times B. Multiplexers underlie memory, buses and the processor itself. The procedure for solving any such problem is the same: write out the truth table, mark the rows with a one, write the sum of products, simplify and build the circuit. And inputs that the output does not depend on do not need to be connected to the circuit.
