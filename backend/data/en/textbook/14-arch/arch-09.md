#CHAPTER 9 | Adders and Comparison
Computer Architecture. Chapter 9. Adders and Comparison. All the computations of a processor, up to multiplying matrices, ultimately come down to addition. Adding binary numbers is as simple as adding decimal ones in a column: we start from the lowest digit, add the digits, and when the sum does not fit, we write down the low digit and carry a one into the next position. In the decimal system a carry happens at a sum of ten, while in binary it happens already at one plus one. From gates we can build a circuit that performs this algorithm automatically.\n\nThe simplest adder is the half adder. It adds two bits and produces two bits: a sum and a carry. The sum is one when the inputs differ, which is exactly the exclusive OR. The carry is one only if both inputs are ones, which is an AND. One XOR gate and one AND gate, and that is the whole half adder. One plus one gives zero with a carry of one, which is the binary number two. But the half adder has no input for a carry from the previous position.\n\nThe full adder adds three bits: two addends and the incoming carry. Its sum is the exclusive OR of all three inputs, and its carry is one when at least two of the inputs are ones. It is built from two half adders and one OR gate: the first adds A and B, the second adds the carry, and the OR merges the two possible carries. You will build this circuit on the board in pieces: first the sum bit, then the carry, then everything together.\n\nA multi-bit number is added with a chain of full adders: the carry from the output of each position goes to the input of the next. This is the ripple-carry adder. Three bits need three adders. For example, three plus two: the lowest one gives a one with no carry, the middle one adds one and one and gives a zero with a carry, and the highest one receives that carry and gives a one. The result is five. The chain is simple and compact, but it has a price: the carry runs through all the positions one after another.\n\nA special case of addition is adding one, the increment. Here the second addend is always zero, and the carry comes from nowhere as a one. So the full adder can be simplified. The lowest bit is simply inverted: zero becomes one and vice versa. The next bit toggles only if the lowest bit was a one: that is the exclusive OR of two bits. This is how counters and the advancing of the next instruction address work, and the increment is exactly what you will have to build first.\n\nHow do we subtract? Two's complement helps reduce subtraction to addition: minus B is not B plus one. So A minus B equals A plus not B plus one. It is enough to put XOR gates in front of the second input of the adder, fed with a subtract signal: at zero, B passes as it is, and at one, it is inverted. And the same subtract signal goes to the carry input of the lowest position. The result is an adder-subtractor: one circuit performs both operations, and in the ALU of a processor it is the main building block.\n\nWhat happens if the result does not fit into the given number of bits? For unsigned numbers this is the carry out: fifteen plus one in four bits gives zero and a carry of one. For signed numbers in two's complement the carry no longer works: seven plus one gives minus eight, even though no carry occurred. Here we need an overflow flag: it equals one when the sign of the result does not match the signs of two addends that had the same sign. In the following chapters these flags will become the flags of the processor.\n\nSubtraction can also be built directly. The half subtractor takes two bits and produces a difference and a borrow: the difference is the exclusive OR, and the borrow is one when we subtract one from zero. The full subtractor adds the borrow from the previous position. Such circuits appear in textbooks, but in practice we subtract through an adder: it is more economical, because there is no need to build a separate subtraction circuit. Still, it is useful to build a half subtractor and a full subtractor to better understand how borrowing works.\n\nNext to arithmetic stands comparison. A one-bit comparator produces three signals: equal, greater, less. Equality is the negation of the exclusive OR, and A greater than B means A is one and B is zero, and symmetrically for less. Two-bit numbers are compared like this: first the high bits, and if they are equal, then the low bits. Another way is to subtract and look at the flags: zero means equal, and the sign shows which number is greater. This is exactly how branch conditions are checked in a processor.\n\nWhat remains is the question of speed. In a ripple-carry adder the carry has to run through all the positions, so the delay grows in proportion to the number of bits: for thirty-two bits that is very long. To speed things up, carry lookahead was invented: for each position we compute in advance whether it generates a carry by itself and whether it passes along an incoming one, and then all the carries are obtained in parallel through two levels of gates. Such circuits are more complex and larger, but noticeably faster. This is a trade of area for speed, and we will see it many times.
