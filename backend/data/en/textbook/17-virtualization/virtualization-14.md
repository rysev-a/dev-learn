#CHAPTER 14 | Security: Guest Escape, Side Channels and Confidential Computing
Virtualization. Chapter 14. Security: guest escape, side channels and confidential computing. We have built the boundary between machines and seen how to make it fast. Now the question is how strong it is. Security starts with a threat model: what are we protecting, and from whom. The guest must be protected from its neighbors, the host from the guest, and the cloud customer sometimes even from the provider itself. Each of these threats has its own attacks and its own defenses.\n\nThe most obvious threat is guest escape. An attacker inside a machine uses a bug in the hypervisor to get out and take control of the host. Most often such bugs are found in device emulation: a famous case from two thousand fifteen, where a bug in the emulation of a long-forgotten floppy disk controller opened a way out, even though nobody uses floppy disks in a modern machine. Virtual devices, video adapters and hypercall handlers are vulnerable too. Hence the rules: remove unneeded devices, lock down the monitor, keep everything updated, and write critical code in safe languages.\n\nEscapes also include attacks through devices. A passed-through device with direct memory access is a potential hole, which is why you need an IOMMU with correct tables. Problems also turn up in the guest drivers themselves and in device firmware, which the hypervisor is forced to trust. And if an attacker manages to replace the firmware of a device, they gain a foothold inside the trusted base. That is why serious clouds verify firmware, update it centrally, and limit the set of devices.\n\nThe second big group of threats is side channels. Two machines on one processor share caches, and from memory access times you can tell what the neighbor was doing. The attacker fills part of the cache with their own data, waits while the victim works, and then measures which entries have been evicted. This is called prime and probe. Sometimes it is enough to take turns flushing and reloading a shared page, as in flush and reload. This is how encryption keys were extracted between virtual machines under laboratory conditions. The defense: don't share cores and caches between untrusted tenants, and use code that does not depend on secrets.\n\nThe loudest side channels of two thousand eighteen are tied to speculative execution. Meltdown allowed reading kernel memory, Spectre made a program leak someone else's data through the cache, and Foreshadow, also known as L1TF, let a guest read data from the host's first-level cache. For virtualization they are especially dangerous: a neighbor on the same core could read the hypervisor's secrets. The answer was microcode updates, flushing the cache on entry to the guest, disabling hyper-threading between untrusted tenants, and new techniques in the kernel, and all of them cost speed.\n\nWe shouldn't forget the more down-to-earth threats either. A noisy neighbor or a malicious guest can exhaust a shared resource: memory, disk bandwidth, network throughput, interrupts. Defense takes quotas and fair sharing: limits in control groups, rate limits on the network and disk, protection against interrupt storms, separate threads for emulation. Denial of service doesn't steal data, but it can do just as much damage, especially when the service promises guaranteed performance.\n\nWhat if the customer doesn't trust even the provider? The answer is called confidential computing. The processor encrypts the virtual machine's memory with a key that the hypervisor cannot access. For AMD this is SEV with the SNP extension, for Intel it is TDX with trust domains, for Arm it is realms in the CCA architecture. SNP adds integrity as well: a special table forbids the hypervisor from silently remapping or replacing a guest page. The host still controls scheduling, but it can no longer read the guest's memory.\n\nHow can the customer make sure the machine is really protected? That is what remote attestation is for. The processor measures the machine's initial state, that is, it computes a hash of the loaded image, and signs a report with a key whose chain of trust leads to the manufacturer. The customer checks the signature, compares the measurement with the expected one, checks that the request is fresh, and only then hands the machine its keys and secrets. The method has limits too: the hypervisor can still deny service, side channels remain, and migration and debugging get harder.\n\nLet's gather it all into practical rules. Update the hypervisor, firmware and microcode. Keep a minimum of devices and lock down the virtual machine monitor. Don't share cores, hyper-threading and page merging between untrusted tenants. Limit resources and watch your neighbors. Encrypt disks and keep secrets in separate stores. For the most sensitive workloads, use confidential machines with attestation. Remember that security is not a product but layers, and every layer costs either speed, or density, or convenience.\n\nLet's sum up the whole book. We began with the question of how one computer can pretend to be many, and went the whole way: traps and hardware support in processors, nested page tables and their cost, input and output from emulation to passthrough, KVM and QEMU, images and migration, resource scheduling, containers and cloud micro machines, and finally security. Virtualization is not magic, it is careful work with boundaries. From here you can read the source code of KVM and Firecracker, build your own machines, and measure everything we talked about.
