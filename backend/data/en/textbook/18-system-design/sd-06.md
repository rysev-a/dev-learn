#CHAPTER 6 | Consistency, CAP, and Idempotency
Chapter 6. Consistency, CAP, and Idempotency. As long as the data lives in one place, the question of what counts as the truth never comes up. The moment we add a replica, a cache, or a shard, the same record lives in several places and can drift apart. A reader has the right to ask: will I see my own latest write, and other people's? And the network, meanwhile, is unreliable: messages get lost and repeated. This chapter is about what guarantees exist, what you pay for them, and how to build operations that are safe to repeat.\n\nThere are several guarantees, from strong to weak. Strict, or linearizability: the system behaves as if there were a single copy of the data, and every read sees the latest acknowledged write. Sequential: everyone sees the changes in the same order, possibly with a delay. Causal: if one event follows from another, they will be seen in the right order. Eventual consistency: if writes stop, all the copies will sooner or later converge, but in the meantime readers may see different things. The stronger the guarantee, the more it costs in latency and availability.\n\nThe CAP theorem talks about three properties: consistency, availability, and partition tolerance. A network partition is not a choice but a fact of life: links break. So the real choice is this: when nodes lose contact, the system either refuses to answer, so as not to hand out something wrong, and sacrifices availability, or answers with what it knows, and sacrifices consistency. This is not a pick-two-of-three forever, but a decision for the duration of the failure. A bank account leans toward consistency, a news feed toward availability.\n\nThe CAP theorem speaks only about failures, and those are rare. The PACELC model adds the second half: even when there is no partition, you still have to choose between latency and consistency. Confirmation from several replicas, especially in different regions, takes time, while reading from the nearest replica is faster but may be stale. So systems decide in advance which data needs strictness and which tolerates staleness. A profile and an account balance belong to different classes, and applying one rule to both makes no sense.\n\nFor several nodes to agree on a shared value, consensus algorithms are used. The best known are Raft and Paxos. They elect a leader, and a write is considered accepted once a majority of nodes have confirmed it. That is why a cluster of three nodes survives the loss of one, and a cluster of five survives two. Consensus is expensive: every write requires an exchange of messages, so it is used not for all the data but for small, important pieces: coordination, configuration, leader election. Bulk data is stored differently, and consensus is responsible for the order.\n\nA transaction groups operations into one: either all of them are performed, or none. The ACID properties are atomicity, consistency of constraints, isolation of concurrent transactions, and durability of what was committed. Full isolation is expensive, so databases give you something weaker by default: the read committed level allows non-repeatable reads, and repeatable read allows phantoms. The strictest level, serializability, rules out the anomalies but slows things down and causes conflicts. When choosing a level, you need to understand which anomalies are unacceptable for your service.\n\nWhat if an operation touches two databases? The classic answer is two-phase commit. In the first phase, the coordinator asks all participants whether they are ready to commit. In the second, if everyone agrees, it tells them to commit; otherwise, to roll back. The scheme gives you atomicity, but it has a price. Participants hold locks while they wait for the coordinator. If the coordinator crashes between the phases, they hang. Latency grows with the number of participants. That is why high-load systems avoid two-phase commit and replace it with lighter schemes.\n\nA saga breaks a long operation into a chain of local transactions. Each step has a compensating action that undoes its meaning. An order: reserve the item, charge the money, schedule the delivery. If the delivery fails, we run the compensations in reverse order: refund the money, release the reservation. A saga is driven either by an orchestrator that knows the order of the steps, or by the participants themselves reacting to each other's events. The price of having no locks is intermediate visibility: in the middle of a saga, others see a partial result.\n\nMessages over the network get lost and duplicated. There are three delivery guarantees. At most once: send and forget, a loss is possible. At least once: retry until acknowledged, duplicates are possible. Exactly once: sounds ideal, but in a distributed system it cannot be achieved in its pure form. In practice, people use at-least-once delivery and make the receiver idempotent, so that processing a message again breaks nothing. The effect looks like exactly once, even though there were several deliveries.\n\nAn operation is idempotent if repeating it gives the same result as performing it once. Setting the status to "paid" is idempotent; adding one dollar is not. To make an operation like charging a card idempotent, the client sends a unique idempotency key. The server remembers the result under that key and, on a repeat, returns the saved response without charging the money again. Unique constraints in the database work too, as does an insert that updates the row when the key already exists. Without idempotency, every retry is a risk of a double charge.\n\nRetries are a double-edged sword. If a service is overloaded, a retry without a pause only makes the overload worse: clients triple the load, and the service cannot get back on its feet. So you retry with a growing pause: one second, two, four, and you add random jitter so that clients don't all rush in at once. You limit the number of attempts, and you keep the overall retry budget small, for example no more than ten percent of requests. Only idempotent operations may be retried, and retrying a client error such as a four-oh-four makes no sense.\n\nIn a distributed system you cannot trust clocks: they drift apart by milliseconds or more. The order of events is established with logical clocks and version numbers. Optimistic locking uses this in a simple way: on read we remember the version, on write we check that it hasn't changed; if it has, the write is rejected and the client retries with fresh data. Where concurrent writes to different nodes are acceptable, special data structures are used that merge without conflicts, for example counters and sets; they are called conflict-free replicated data types.\n\nLet's sum up. The stronger the consistency, the more it costs in latency and availability, and you have to choose according to the type of data. In a network partition, you must sacrifice either availability or consistency. Consensus is for small, important data. Distributed transactions are replaced with sagas and compensations. At-least-once delivery plus idempotency gives the effect of exactly once. Retries are done with a growing pause and a budget. In the exercises you will stop a retry storm, turn on idempotency keys, and choose a replication mode.
