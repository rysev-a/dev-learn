#CHAPTER 22 | Project: Social Network (VKontakte)
Chapter 22. Project: a social network like VKontakte. There are many different subsystems under one roof here. Profiles and friends, the wall and the news feed, private messages and group chats, communities with millions of followers, music and video, people search and friend suggestions. Every subsystem loads the system in its own way: the friend graph is read constantly, messages are written without a break, and media is served by the gigabyte. So the main idea of the project is to split the data into domains and pick a separate storage approach for each one.\n\nHere are the numbers. A hundred million daily active users, each with about a hundred friends on average: ten billion connections. Private messages number in the billions per day, which means hundreds of thousands per second at the evening peak. The feed and the wall are read tens of billions of times a day. Media, especially video and music, produces most of the traffic. User data must be stored inside the country, and that determines where the data centers go. The peak comes in the evening and is predictable in time, so we size the headroom by it.\n\nPrinciple number one: do not put everything into one database. Profiles are sharded by user ID. The friend graph is also sharded by user, but in a special structure. The wall and posts are sharded by author. Messages are sharded by conversation. Media lives in object storage, and search lives in a separate index. Each domain picks a key that fits its own queries and grows independently. Domains are linked by IDs and events, not by table joins. IDs are generated with the time baked in, so that objects sort without a separate date field.\n\nFriendship is mutual, so the connection is written into the lists of both users, and the add-a-friend operation touches two shards, which means it runs as a saga or with guaranteed event delivery. Reading the friend list is the most frequent query, so lists are cached, and the largest ones are stored in compressed form. Mutual friends are the intersection of two sorted lists, and friends of friends are a graph walk of two steps. A limit on the number of friends protects against fake inflation and against queries that are too heavy. One-way followers are stored separately.\n\nThe wall is the posts of a single author, and they are stored where the author lives. The news feed is assembled from the posts of friends and of the communities a person follows. Here the hybrid works again. Posts from ordinary people are pushed into the ready-made feeds of their followers, while posts from communities with millions of members are mixed in at read time: pushing to millions of recipients is too expensive. The feed stores IDs, is paginated by cursor, and is capped in length. For inactive users, ready-made feeds are not maintained; they are built when the user comes back.\n\nPrivate messages are the write-heaviest domain. All messages of a conversation are stored together and sharded by conversation ID, so that the history is read with a single query. If we sharded by time ranges, all new messages would land in the last shard: a hot spot. That is why the shard is chosen by the hash of the conversation. Delivery goes over a persistent connection: the gateway receives the message, writes it to storage, publishes an event to the stream, and forwards it to the recipient. Delivery and read statuses are separate lightweight records, and unread counters accumulate in the cache.\n\nA community is a page with a huge audience. A popular post gets thousands of likes and comments per second, and the community's main page gets thousands of reads per second on a single key. Hot records are kept in the cache, counters are accumulated and written in batches, and the community profile is protected with rate limiting and versioning. Notifications to followers go out asynchronously through a queue and in portions, so as not to create a spike. Administrator rights are checked on every change by a dedicated permissions service, because a mistake here means a hijacked community.\n\nMedia accounts for the main volume and traffic. Photos, videos, and music are uploaded straight into object storage. Video is transcoded by workers from a queue into several qualities and cut into short segments, which the client loads as it watches. Music is streamed with protection against direct download through signed links. Delivery is handled by a content delivery network: popular items sit in it, and rare ones are requested from storage. For records nobody has viewed in a long time, cheap storage classes are switched on, otherwise media costs will eat the entire budget.\n\nPeople search is one of the most frequent operations: name, city, school, age. The index is built over people, filters are applied to it, and results are ranked by the number of mutual friends. Communities have a separate index. Search through your own messages is a personal index of the user; it is sharded by user and filled from the message stream. In all cases the results are filtered by privacy settings: a hidden profile must not show up in search. Indexes grow in volume by adding shards, and the query load is taken by replicas.\n\nThe People You May Know section is built from mutual acquaintances and interests. This is a heavy graph computation, so it is calculated ahead of time by batch jobs in an analytics system, from events in the stream, and the result is saved for each user and served from the cache. Personal ranking of the feed is done by a separate scoring service, which has a limited share of requests and falls back to chronological order. For new users with no history, we use popular items and overlaps in contacts. All models are tested on a held-out set and rolled out to a fraction of traffic.\n\nPrivacy in a social network is a requirement, not an option. Every record has visibility settings: everyone, friends, selected people, nobody. The permission check runs on every read in a dedicated fast service, whose answers are cached for seconds. Content goes through automatic and manual moderation via queues, spam and bots are held back by rate limits and checks, and a suspicious login requires confirmation. Anything deleted or hidden must disappear from feeds, caches, and search, so deletion is propagated as an event and is also checked at display time.\n\nOperating such a network is a big job of its own. Servers and data are placed in several availability zones inside the country, and where closeness is required, in several regions. Users can be split into cells: each cell serves its own part and limits the blast radius, and new versions roll out cell by cell. Metrics and reliability targets are kept per domain: messages, feed, media. The main costs are media storage and traffic, so we use storage classes, a high hit rate in the delivery network, and a reserved plan for the base load.\n\nLet us sum up. A social network is a set of domains with different sharding keys. The friend graph is stored in both directions and cached, the feed is built as a hybrid, and messages are sharded by conversation hash and travel over persistent connections. Communities are protected by cache and deferred writes, media goes through a queue and the delivery network, and search and recommendations are separate indexes and computations. A separate service checks permissions, and reliability comes from zones and cells. In the exercises you will build the network in stages and take the exam.
