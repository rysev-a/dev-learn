#CHAPTER 12 | News Feed
Chapter 12. News feed. When you open a social network, you see a feed: fresh posts from the people you follow. The task sounds simple, but with a hundred million users it is one of the heaviest workloads on the internet. Everyone opens the feed many times a day, and when a post is published, thousands of followers have to see it, and quickly. In this chapter we will look at two ways of building a feed, the celebrity problem, and the hybrid approach that real services use.\n\nLet's start with the requirements. Few posts are written, but the feed is opened constantly: there are two orders of magnitude more reads than writes. Opening must be fast, within a couple of hundred milliseconds. Freshness allows a small delay: a post will appear after a second or two, and nobody will notice. We need pagination: the feed is scrolled in portions. And we want the feed to be personal: the order depends on the person. These requirements decide the choice: we optimize reads, and writes are allowed to be expensive.\n\nThe first way is to build the feed at read time. The application takes the user's list of follows, picks the latest posts for each one, and merges the results by time. Writing a post is cheap this way: you just add it to storage. But reading is heavy: there may be hundreds of follows, and every open turns into dozens of queries. The load grows in proportion to the number of follows. This is called fan-out on read, or pull, and it suits small services and people with few follows.\n\nThe second way is to prepare feeds in advance. When an author publishes a post, the system puts its identifier into the feed of every follower, usually in a fast store. Reading becomes a single call: the feed is simply fetched. You pay on the write: an author with a thousand followers means a thousand writes for one post. This is called fan-out on write, or push. It is ideal when the read-to-write ratio is high, but expensive for authors with a large audience.\n\nWhat happens if an author has ten million followers? One post produces ten million writes into feeds. That is seconds or minutes of cluster work, a delay in publishing, and a load spike, while many of those followers do not even open the app. This is the celebrity problem. The opposite option is bad too: if every feed is built at read time, every open pulls in the celebrities' posts, and the load on their data is enormous. We need a compromise that takes the size of the author's audience into account.\n\nThe solution is a hybrid. For ordinary authors, you use fan-out on write: they have few followers, and the writes are cheap. For celebrities, the post is not fanned out but stored once. When the feed is opened, you take the precomputed part and mix in the fresh posts of the celebrities the person follows. A person has only a few such follows, so the addition is cheap. The threshold between ordinary and famous is chosen by audience size and adjusted as the load grows.\n\nA ready feed stores not whole posts but their identifiers: this saves memory, and editing a post's text does not require fixing thousands of feeds. The posts themselves live in a separate store, usually with a cache, and are fetched in a batch by a list of identifiers. The feed is capped in length, say the latest thousand entries: nobody scrolls deeper. For pagination you use a cursor, that is, the identifier of the last post seen, rather than a page number, so new posts do not shift the results.\n\nTo fan out and build feeds, you need a follow graph: who follows whom. It is stored in two ways at once: the list of a user's follows and the list of an author's followers. The data is sharded by user identifier so that a list query goes to one shard. For popular authors the follower list is huge and is read in portions. Frequently used lists are kept in a cache. Following and unfollowing are rare, so they may be a bit slower than reads, and updating feeds because of them can happen in the background.\n\nA chronological feed is simple, but not always the best. A ranked feed shows first what you are most likely to be interested in. There are usually three stages. First, you select hundreds of candidates from the prepared feed and the follows. Then a model scores each one by the probability of a reaction. Finally, you apply filters and diversity rules: not five posts in a row from one author, nothing prohibited. Ranking adds computation at read time, so it has to fit into the latency budget and be cached wherever possible.\n\nEvery post comes with likes, comments, and views. That is a huge stream of small writes. You cannot update a row in the main database for every like: a popular post would get thousands of updates per second on a single row. Counters are accumulated in a fast store or a queue and written to the database in batches every few seconds. They are shown with a small lag and an acceptable error: nobody needs the exact number of likes. For critical counters such as a balance, you use other, strict mechanisms.\n\nMany followers have not logged in for a long time. Maintaining ready feeds for them is a waste of memory and computation. So only the feeds of active users are updated, and for the rest the entry in storage expires. When an inactive user comes back, the feed is rebuilt using pull, which takes a bit longer but happens rarely. This trick can cut the amount of fan-out work several times over. It is a good example of the fact that you optimize not an abstract load but real user behavior.\n\nAn author deleted a post or hid it from some followers. But the identifier is already in thousands of ready feeds. Finding and removing it everywhere is expensive. So at read time you check the post's status: if it is deleted or unavailable to this reader, the post is skipped. A background cleanup removes it from the feeds later. The same goes for blocks and privacy settings: the rules are applied at display time, not only at fan-out time. This way privacy is respected immediately, and the cost of cleanup is deferred.\n\nLet's sum up. A feed is read hundreds of times more often than it is written, so we optimize reads. Pull builds the feed on open: simple, but heavy. Push prepares feeds in advance: fast reads, expensive writes. Celebrities break push, and the solution is a hybrid where their posts are mixed in at read time. The feed stores identifiers and is scrolled by cursor. Counters are aggregated, inactive users are not served, and deleted content is checked at display time. In the exercises you will choose how the feed is built and pick the cache and workers.
