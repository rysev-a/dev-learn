#CHAPTER 1 | Requirements, load, and back-of-the-envelope estimates
System design. Chapter 1. Requirements, load, and back-of-the-envelope estimates. System design is the skill of putting together ready-made building blocks into a service that can handle the load, survive failures, and stay within budget. The blocks are well known: a load balancer, servers, a cache, a database, a queue. The art is in choosing the right ones, wiring them together correctly, and explaining why. In this book, in every chapter you will build a diagram by hand in the trainer, and the model will calculate load, latency, availability, and cost. Let's start with the main thing: the question of what exactly we are building.\n\nThe first step is functional requirements: what the service can do. You need to limit them to a short list of scenarios. For a notes service, that is create a note, read a note, search by text. Everything else comes later. Greed hurts here: every extra feature drags in storage, a queue, and one more thing that can break. A good requirement is testable: you can write it in one sentence and write a test for it. And debatable questions, like whether we need search or whether a feed is enough, are best asked right away, while the design is still cheap, not after launch.\n\nThe second step is non-functional requirements: how well the service must do it. How many users and how many requests per second. What latency is acceptable. How much time per year the service may be down. Whether it is fine to show slightly stale data. How much money there is. These numbers, not the feature list, determine the architecture. The very same notes service for a hundred friends fits on one server, but for a hundred million users it needs dozens of machines, caches, and replication. So find out the numbers before you draw the first rectangle.\n\nLoad is estimated as a chain of multiplications. Take the number of daily active users, say ten million. Multiply by the number of actions per person, for example twenty. That gives two hundred million requests a day. A day has about eighty-six thousand seconds, so on average that is two to three thousand requests per second. But traffic is uneven: at peak the load is two to three times the average, and the design is sized for the peak. Round boldly: you need an order of magnitude, not precision to the unit. A napkin estimate saves you from a design that is a hundred times too big or too small.\n\nThe next question is the ratio of reads to writes. Most services are read far more often than written: in a news feed there are ten to a hundred reads per write. This matters because reads are easy to speed up with a cache and replicas, while writes always go to a single place and are harder to scale. If ninety-five percent of operations are reads, the database can handle an order of magnitude more than with equal shares. There are inverse services too: metrics and logs consist almost entirely of writes. Once you know the ratio, you immediately see what you will have to work on first.\n\nData volume is calculated like this: the number of new records per day, times the size of one record, times the retention period. A hundred million notes a day at one kilobyte each is a hundred gigabytes a day, about thirty-six terabytes a year. Add copies: data is stored in at least three instances. Media is counted separately: photos and videos take orders of magnitude more than text and live in object storage. The share of data that is read often matters too, the working set: if it fits in memory, the cache works wonderfully. The volume immediately tells you whether you need shards.\n\nLatency is measured with percentiles. The fiftieth is the median: half of the responses are faster. The ninety-ninth is the boundary beyond which only one request in a hundred is slower. The arithmetic mean hides the tail, and the tail is exactly what users see: with pages made of many requests, almost every visitor will hit a slow one at least once. The tail grows under overload: when a node's utilization approaches a hundred percent, the request queue grows and latency shoots up. That is why a healthy utilization is considered to be sixty to eighty percent, not a hundred, and why we watch the ninety-ninth percentile in particular.\n\nAvailability is measured in nines. Ninety-nine percent is about three and a half days of downtime a year. Three nines is nine hours. Four is fifty-two minutes. Each next nine is sharply more expensive. In a chain, availabilities multiply: a load balancer, a server, and a database with availability of ninety-nine point nine percent each already give about ninety-nine point seven. Redundancy fixes this: two independent servers fail at the same time far less often than one. But a spare costs money, and you have to decide how many nines the business actually pays for.\n\nThree abbreviations that people mix up. S-L-I is the indicator we measure: the share of successful requests or the ninety-ninth percentile of latency. S-L-O is the internal target for that indicator, for example ninety-nine point nine percent success over a month. S-L-A is the promise to a customer, breaking which costs money. The S-L-O is kept stricter than the S-L-A so that the margin kicks in before the penalty. The remainder between the target and a hundred percent is called the error budget: while it lasts, you can take risks and ship new things, and when it is used up, you stop and fix reliability.\n\nFor estimates, it helps to remember orders of magnitude. A memory access is about a hundred nanoseconds. A random read from a spinning disk takes milliseconds, from a solid-state drive, tens of microseconds. A round trip over the network inside a data center is half a millisecond, between continents, a hundred or more. One application server handles hundreds or thousands of requests per second, one database handles thousands of simple reads but only hundreds of writes. These numbers are rough, but they let you quickly see where the bottleneck is: if you need a hundred thousand writes per second, one database will not do.\n\nThe throughput of a design equals the capacity of its weakest link. You can triple the application servers, and the database will still hit a hundred percent, and the service will still be slow. So the design cycle goes like this: draw the diagram, calculate the utilization of every component, find the most loaded one, and strengthen exactly that. Then calculate again: the bottleneck has moved somewhere else. Keep going until every component has headroom. In the trainer you can see this cycle right on the diagram: the load bars turn red at the components that need strengthening. But headroom costs money: keeping utilization at thirty percent is safe and three times as expensive, while ninety is cheap, but any spike takes the service down.\n\nLet's put it all into a working order. First, clarify the requirements and write down the numbers. Second, estimate the load, the volume, and the peak. Third, draw the simplest diagram: users, a server, a database. Fourth, calculate the utilization and find the bottleneck. Fifth, strengthen it: add servers, a cache, replicas. Sixth, go through the failures: what happens if each component goes down. Seventh, name the trade-offs and the cost. This cycle will come in handy both in an interview and in real work. Further in the book, we will go through it again and again, making the diagram more complex.\n\nLet's sum up. System design starts with requirements: features and numbers. Load is estimated with a chain of multiplications and sized for the peak. The ratio of reads to writes shows where you need a cache and replicas. Latency is measured with percentiles, availability with nines, and promises are formalized as S-L-Os. Throughput equals the capacity of the weakest link, so you look for it and strengthen it. And reliability and headroom cost money. In the exercises you will build your first chain, pick the number of servers, find the bottleneck, survive a peak, and stay within budget.
