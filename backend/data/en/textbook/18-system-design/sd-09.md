#CHAPTER 9 | Resilience Patterns
Chapter 9. Resilience Patterns. In a big system something is always broken: a disk, the network, a dependency, somebody's deployment. So resilience is not the absence of failures, but the ability to limit the damage and recover quickly. The main danger is not the failure itself but its spread: one slow service drags down its neighbors, they pile up requests and fall over, and soon nothing works at all. The set of patterns in this chapter, timeouts, retries, the circuit breaker, rate limiting, bulkheads, and degradation, keeps the trouble from spreading.\n\nThe first rule: every network call has a timeout. Without one, a slow service holds a connection and a thread forever, and the piled-up requests exhaust the caller's resources. You pick the timeout from the latency percentiles of the dependency: slightly above the ninety-ninth. In a chain of calls the overall budget matters: if the client waits two seconds, then a service that calls another service has no right to wait three for it. So the remaining time is passed along with the request, and every participant knows how much it has left.\n\nMany failures are short-lived, so a retry often helps. But retries need to be done with care. Only idempotent operations may be retried. The pause grows exponentially, with random jitter added so that clients do not all rush in at once. The number of attempts is limited to two or three, and the overall retry budget is kept small. One more rule: only one level of the chain does the retrying. If each of three levels retries three times, the bottom service will receive twenty-seven requests for one: during a failure the load is multiplied.\n\nA circuit breaker works like a fuse. In the closed state, requests go to the dependency and failures are counted. When their share exceeds a threshold, the breaker opens: the following calls are refused immediately, without loading the sick dependency and without making anyone wait. After some time the breaker moves to the half-open state and lets a few trial requests through. If they succeed, it closes again; if not, it opens again. This way the system fails fast instead of suffering slowly, and gives the dependency time to recover.\n\nRate limiting protects a service from overload. It cuts off attacks, client bugs, runaway scripts, and simply greedy users. There are different kinds of limits: per user, per address, per API key, for the whole service. An excess request gets a four twenty-nine response with a header saying when it can try again. The limit is placed at the entrance, before the expensive operations. And it is chosen with headroom above normal load: a limit that is too strict cuts off your own users no worse than an attack.\n\nThere are several algorithms. A fixed window counts requests in each minute: simple, but at the boundary between minutes you can let through a double quota. A sliding window counts over the last sixty seconds and is more accurate. A token bucket refills at a constant rate and each request spends a token: it allows short bursts up to the size of the bucket and limits the average rate. In a cluster the counters must be shared, for example in a fast store, otherwise each server lets through its own quota and the overall limit ends up several times higher.\n\nIf the load is above capacity, it is better to serve some requests well than all of them badly. An overloaded service answers the excess with a quick refusal: a five oh three code and a wait before retrying. What to drop must be chosen sensibly: background jobs and analytics first, then optional features, and payments and login last of all. For that, requests are given priorities. A fast refusal is cheaper than a slow one: it frees resources instead of building a queue in which the requests will die from timeouts anyway.\n\nThe name comes from shipbuilding: bulkheads divide the hull into compartments so that a hole floods only one. In services these are separate resource pools. For each dependency you allocate its own pool of connections and threads: if a slow dependency exhausts its pool, the others keep working. Different clients get their own quotas: one greedy client will not crowd out the rest. The smaller the compartments, the better the isolation, but the worse the resource utilization. So bulkheads go where the risk of failure spreading is highest.\n\nA total outage is rarely necessary. A well-designed system degrades when something breaks: it returns a simplified result. Recommendations are down, so we show what is popular. The price service does not respond, so we take the last price from the cache, with a note. Search is down, so we keep the catalog navigation. To do this, features are made switchable with flags, and a fallback is worked out in advance for every dependency. A user will prefer a page without recommendations to a white screen with an error, and the business keeps earning.\n\nA typical cascade scenario. One service slows down. Its callers wait and pile up requests, exhaust their threads, and slow down too. Clients retry, and the load grows. Some servers fall over, the load balancer sends their share to the rest, and those fall over next. The nastiest part is the stable state of overload: even when the cause is gone, the system does not recover, because the accumulated queue of retries holds it on the bottom. Against a cascade you use timeouts, circuit breakers, load shedding, and no retries at several levels.\n\nIf a request touches dozens of nodes, the latency is set by the slowest one: one slow response out of a hundred spoils almost every user request. Against the tail we use hedging: if a response has not arrived by the time of the ninety-fifth percentile, send the same request to another replica and take the first answer. The load grows by just a few percent, and the tail shrinks several times over. The trick works only for idempotent reads and requires the system to have spare capacity.\n\nResilience patterns cannot be considered working until they have been tested. For that we use chaos engineering: under safe conditions you shut down servers, cut the network, add delays, fill the disk. You watch whether the timeouts and breakers kick in, whether something unexpected breaks, whether the alerts notice the failure. You start in a test environment, then move to a small share of real traffic. This is complemented by drills: the team rehearses an outage from a script. That way a failure at three in the morning stops being a surprise.\n\nLet's sum up. Failures are inevitable, so the main task is to keep them from spreading. Every call has a timeout and an overall time budget. Retries are used for idempotent operations, with a pause and at only one level. The circuit breaker fails fast instead of suffering. Limits protect the entrance, load shedding keeps what matters most, bulkheads isolate resources, and degradation returns a simplified result. All of this is verified with chaos tests. In the exercises you will set up a limiter without hurting your own users and assemble a protective circuit.
