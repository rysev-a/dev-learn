Chapter 23. Project: food delivery. The user picks a restaurant and dishes, pays for the order, the restaurant cooks it, a courier picks it up and brings it, and the customer follows the delivery on a map. The service brings together three sides: customers, restaurants, and couriers, and each works with its own app. Here we combine a catalog with search, a payment loop, courier dispatch by geolocation, and a complex order lifecycle. The main feature of the load is the lunch and evening peaks, when there are several times more orders than usual.\n\nLet us start with the numbers. Ten million orders a day is about a hundred and twenty per second on average, but eighty percent fall into two peaks, where the load is four to five times higher. The restaurant catalog and menus are opened tens of times more often than orders are placed. Two hundred thousand couriers send their position every few seconds. The price of a mistake is high: a lost or duplicated order means an unhappy customer and a loss for the restaurant. So payment and order state are designed by the rules for money, while the catalog is designed to be read-heavy and cacheable.\n\nThe customer looks for restaurants nearby: by address, cuisine, rating, delivery time. This needs a search index with a geo filter, not queries to the main database. Restaurant cards and menus are read most often, so they are cached, and images are served by a content delivery network. But the catalog changes: a dish runs out, a restaurant closes, the kitchen queue shifts. Such changes travel as a stream of events, update the index, and invalidate a cache with a short lifetime. The delivery time in the results is calculated ahead of time by zone and refreshed every few minutes.\n\nPlacing an order is the most sensitive path. The customer taps the button, and the service checks the cart: the dishes are available, the restaurant is open, the address is inside the delivery zone. The price is fixed at the moment of the order, and the payment is reserved. A second tap must not create a second order, so the request is idempotent and carries a key. After that the order moves through states: created, accepted by the restaurant, being cooked, ready, handed to the courier, delivered, canceled. Each transition is allowed only from valid states and produces an event for everyone involved in the process.\n\nPayment is part of a long process, and it consists of several steps, each of which has a reverse action. First, pre-authorization of the amount. Then confirmation by the restaurant. After delivery, the charge. If the restaurant declines, the pre-authorization is released. If the customer cancels, the money is refunded. This chain is driven by a saga with compensations, and every request to the external gateway carries an idempotency key, so that a retry does not charge twice. The gateway is external, it responds slowly and sometimes goes down, so there are several channels, retries are made with a pause, and a circuit breaker protects against an avalanche of errors.\n\nWhen the dish is almost ready, we need to find a courier. Courier positions arrive as a stream into an in-memory geo service, just like in a taxi service. The dispatcher picks candidates near the restaurant, takes into account their current load and the expected arrival time estimated by a model, and assigns the order atomically. One courier can carry several orders along the way, and that is a separate optimization problem. If a courier declines or does not respond, the offer goes to the next one. The assignment must be idempotent: a retry does not create a second courier for the same order.\n\nThe restaurant works in its own app or terminal, and the order has to reach it reliably. The order is delivered over a persistent connection, and if it drops, through a queue with retries and a phone call. The restaurant confirms receipt within a few minutes; if it does not confirm, the order is automatically handed to an operator or canceled with a refund. Dish availability and kitchen load come back as events. Integration with partners' point-of-sale systems goes through separate connectors, because those systems are unreliable and built in different ways.\n\nThe customer sees the order status and the position of the courier. Statuses arrive as events from the stream. The courier's positions are sent to the customer's screen over a persistent connection, but only while the order is on its way and no more often than once every few seconds. The estimated delivery time is recalculated by a model that takes traffic and the order state into account, and the result is cached per order. If the connection drops, the app asks for the current state when it comes back and then keeps listening for events. Inaccuracies are not critical, but a mistake in the order status is critical, so the status is taken from the main database.\n\nThe three sides need different notifications. For the customer: order accepted, courier on the way, courier nearby. For the restaurant: new order, urgent. For the courier: order offer, urgent, and reminders. All of them are produced by events from the stream and sent by a notification service with different priorities. Duplicates are cut off by event ID, and a push that was not delivered is repeated as a message through another channel. For mass promotions we use a separate queue with a rate limit, so that advertising does not get in the way of urgent messages.\n\nThe lunch and evening peaks are the main load. Autoscaling cannot keep up with them, so the fleet is prepared ahead of time on a schedule. Event writes are smoothed out by queues, and a rate limiter stands at the entrance. Priorities protect the critical path: placing the order, payment, handing it to the restaurant. If the load exceeds capacity, the secondary features are simplified: complex catalog filters are turned off, delivery time is refreshed less often, promotions are limited. Sometimes a restaurant or a district is temporarily switched off from accepting orders, so as not to pile up orders that cannot be fulfilled in time.\n\nAnalytics is built from the event stream. It calculates demand by district and time, the real cooking time at each restaurant, courier load, dynamic delivery zones, and surcharges. Models are trained on the same data: preparation and delivery time, dish and restaurant recommendations. All of this is computed outside the main order path and arrives in it as ready values from the cache. This way the models can be updated without risking the main process, and if analytics fails, the service keeps working on the last known values.\n\nThe service must always accept orders, and an order or a payment must never be lost. So the order databases are replicated across several zones with automatic failover, servers are spread across zones with spare capacity, rollouts are gradual, and alerts are tied to a target for the share of successful orders. Costs are reduced like this: a reserved plan for the base load, spot instances for analytics and mailing workers, a high hit rate in the cache and the delivery network, and moving old data to cheap storage. But protection of orders and payments is not where we save.\n\nLet us sum up. The restaurant catalog is built on a search index, a cache, and a delivery network. An order is an idempotent state machine with a payment saga and compensations. Couriers are matched through an in-memory geo service with atomic assignment. Events go to the stream and serve notifications, tracking, and analytics. Peaks are survived with queues, limiters, priorities, and degradation, and reliability comes from zones, replicas, and monitoring. In the exercises you will build the service in stages and take the exam.
