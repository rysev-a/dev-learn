#CHAPTER 17 | Cloud, Infrastructure as Code, and Cost
Chapter 17. Cloud, infrastructure as code, and cost. In the cloud you do not have to buy servers in advance: resources are taken as needed and paid for by the meter. That speeds up work, but it makes the cost variable, and without attention it grows silently. So price is a property of the design just like latency and availability, and it is designed deliberately. The practice that deals with this is called cloud financial management. In this chapter we will look at what makes up the bill, how to reduce it, and how to describe infrastructure as code.\n\nThe cloud offers several levels of readiness. Infrastructure as a service hands you virtual machines and disks: you install everything else yourself. Platform as a service takes care of the runtime and scaling: you bring the code. Functions as a service run code on an event, with payment per invocation. A ready-made product frees you from development altogether. The higher the level, the fewer the worries and the lower the cost of operating, but the less control and the greater the dependence on the vendor. Managed databases and queues free you from being on call, and usually justify the markup.\n\nA cloud bill consists of several items. Compute: servers, containers, functions, usually the biggest. Storage: disks, objects, backups. Network traffic: inbound is free, while outbound and cross-region traffic cost money. Requests to services: reads and writes to storage, function invocations. Managed services: databases, caches, queues, load balancers. Before optimizing, you need to see the structure: often it turns out that the expensive part is not what you thought, for example forgotten disks or traffic between zones.\n\nA typical waste is servers taken with a big margin and loaded at fifteen percent. Right-sizing relies on metrics: if the processor and memory are steadily idle, take smaller ones. If a large instance is twice as powerful as a small one and costs twice as much, many medium ones are often a better deal: they scale more flexibly and survive failures better. Modern processors on the ARM architecture usually give better performance per dollar. Sizes should be reviewed regularly, because the load changes and new generations of machines are more cost-effective.\n\nThe same machine can be paid for in different ways. On demand: you pay by the hour, you can walk away at any moment, and it is the highest price. Reserved instances or a savings plan: you commit for one to three years and get a discount of about forty percent; this fits a constant baseline load. Spot: spare capacity at a price three to four times lower, but the provider can take the machine back within a minute; this suits batch jobs, queue workers, and stateless servers that tolerate loss. A good setup combines them: the baseline on reserved, the peaks on demand, background work on spot.\n\nAutoscaling saves money: at night you need three times fewer servers than at the peak, and paying for idle ones makes no sense. Metrics trigger scaling by load, a schedule adds capacity ahead of known peaks like Monday morning, and a forecast learns from history. For rarely used services you can scale down to zero: while there are no requests, we pay nothing. The limits are well known: starting new instances is not instant, so you need headroom, and the minimum number of servers is set by the reliability requirement: at least one in each zone.\n\nStorage is cheap, but data piles up over the years. The cloud offers classes: hot for frequent access, cool for rare access, and archive for data that is almost never read. The colder it is, the cheaper the storage and the more expensive and slower the access. Lifecycle rules move objects between classes by age: after thirty days to cool, after a year to archive, after seven years delete. You also need to watch for forgotten resources: unattached disks, old snapshots, empty buckets. Compression and deduplication give extra savings.\n\nInbound traffic in the cloud is free, while outbound traffic to the internet costs real money, especially for video and large files. Traffic between zones and regions can also be paid: services that talk to each other a lot are better placed together. Against a traffic bill you have: a content delivery network, which is cheaper than serving directly; response compression; client-side caching; and sending only the fields that are needed. When designing multi-region setups, the cost of replicating data across regions goes into the calculation on par with the servers.\n\nFunctions as a service run code in response to an event, scale from zero to thousands of instances, and are billed for invocations and time. They are great for rare and uneven tasks: processing uploads, webhooks, schedules. But there are limitations. Cold start: the first call after idling takes hundreds of milliseconds. Limits on time and memory. And at a constantly high load the price is higher than for ordinary servers. So functions are handy for getting started and for peaks, while a steady stream is cheaper to serve with always-on servers.\n\nConfiguring the cloud by hand through the console turns into chaos: nobody remembers what was created and why. Infrastructure is described as code, for example in Terraform: a file says which networks, servers, and databases should exist, and the tool brings the cloud to that state. Changes go through review and the pipeline, like ordinary code. The environment can be recreated entirely, for example for tests or after a disaster. A divergence of the real state from the description is called drift, and it is detected and fixed. The infrastructure state is stored separately and protected.\n\nWhat cannot be attributed to an owner, nobody optimizes. So resources get tags: team, service, environment. The bill is broken down by them on dashboards, and every team sees its own spending. For development environments, automatic shutdown at night and on weekends is turned on. For budgets, alerts are set on the forecast of exceeding, not just on the fact. Regular spending reviews find anomalies: a forgotten cluster, a traffic explosion, a scaling bug. Cost becomes a metric you watch just like errors.\n\nManaged services are convenient, but they tie you to a vendor: their interfaces and behavior are unique. Full portability at the level of the lowest common denominator means giving up half the features and doing more of the work yourself. A reasonable approach is to choose standard interfaces where it is cheap, for example containers and ordinary databases, and accept lock-in consciously where the benefit is large. Multiple clouds are justified by reliability requirements, regulators, or price negotiations, but not as a goal in itself. The main thing is to understand what leaving would cost.\n\nLet's sum up. In the cloud, cost is variable, and it has to be designed. The bill consists of compute, storage, traffic, requests, and managed services. Size by the facts, combine reserved for the baseline, on-demand for the peaks, and spot for background work. Autoscaling and data lifecycle remove the excess. Outbound traffic is expensive. Infrastructure is described as code, and costs are tied to teams with tags and budgets. And vendor lock-in is accepted consciously. In the exercises you will cut the bill without breaking reliability.
