#CHAPTER 20 | Project: Photo Sharing (Instagram)
Chapter 20. Project: a photo-sharing service like Instagram. Users publish photos and short videos, follow each other, and scroll a feed. There are stories that disappear after a day, likes, comments, search for people and tags, and an explore section with recommendations. The load here is unusual: every post is heavy in size, and feed reads outnumber writes by orders of magnitude. The main themes of the project are media processing, delivery through a content delivery network, the feed for celebrities, like counters, notifications, and recommendations.\n\nLet's start with the numbers. Five hundred million daily active users, a hundred million new posts a day, which is about twelve hundred per second, and three times that at peak. Feed and image views run to tens of billions a day, which means hundreds of thousands of requests per second. The feed has to open in three hundred milliseconds, a post has to be confirmed immediately, but processing can take seconds. The volume of media grows by tens of terabytes a day, and that drives both the cost and every storage decision.\n\nPublishing works like this. The client uploads the original straight to object storage using a signed link, while the server creates a record for the post. The upload event goes into a queue, and workers pick up tasks and turn a single photo into a whole set: several resolutions, modern formats like WebP, a blurred preview, and content detection for moderation. While processing runs, the author is shown a local copy. The workers are idempotent, safe to run on cheap interruptible servers, and scale with the length of the queue.\n\nMost of the traffic is images and video, and almost all of it should be served by the content delivery network. Every file has an immutable address with a version in it, so the cache lifetime can be set to months. The client asks for the resolution that fits its screen, and the network picks a suitable format. The hit rate should be above ninety-five percent: every miss is a request to object storage and money for outgoing traffic. Hot posts are warmed up in advance, and old photos that are rarely viewed are moved to cheaper storage classes.\n\nMetadata means posts, follows, likes, and comments. There is a lot of it, and it is sharded. An author's posts and follows sit on one shard by user ID, while the likes and comments of a post go by post ID, so we don't have to search the whole cluster for them. IDs are built with a timestamp inside, as in the Snowflake scheme: they are unique without coordination and sort by time, so the feed and pagination work with a cursor. Hot posts and celebrity profiles are read from the cache.\n\nThe feed is the central load. For regular authors, when a post is published its ID is put into the feeds of their followers, which live in a fast cache, and reading comes down to a single call. Celebrities have millions of followers, so fan-out is too expensive; their posts are stored once and mixed in at read time. Users who haven't visited in a long time don't get ready-made feeds: those are built when they come back. The feed stores only IDs, and the posts and profiles themselves are fetched from the cache in one batch.\n\nStories live for twenty-four hours. A separate store with an automatic expiration time is enough for them: expired items delete themselves, with no background sweeps. Stories are watched in series, so the next frames are warmed up in the delivery network ahead of time. Every story has a list of viewers, and recording each view is a huge stream of tiny writes. It is accumulated in a fast store and flushed in batches, and the exact list of viewers is available to the author with a small delay.\n\nLikes are the most frequent kind of write, and a popular post gets thousands of likes per second on a single row. So counters are accumulated in a fast store and flushed to the database in batches, accepting a small error. The fact that this particular person liked the post is stored separately and is needed to show the heart as filled. Comments are sharded by post ID and read in pages with a cursor. For the hottest posts, deferred writes and aggregation are used, otherwise a single row becomes the bottleneck of the whole cluster.\n\nEvery action produces an event: a like, a comment, a follow. The event goes into a stream, and several consumers read it independently. The notification service decides whether a push needs to be sent and merges similar events: instead of a hundred pushes, you get one saying "and ninety-nine others liked this". Another consumer puts the events into an analytics store for reports and recommendations. This decoupling lets you add new consumers without touching the main path for publishing and reading.\n\nThe explore section works in three steps. Thousands of candidates are selected by interests and the follow graph, a model estimates the probability of a reaction for each one, and filters remove anything banned and duplicates. Scoring by the model is the most expensive operation: one instance serves hundreds of requests per second. So the share of requests going to the ranking service is limited, results are cached for minutes, and under overload the system degrades: it shows popular content instead of personalized content. That is also what is done for new users with no history. Search for people, tags, and places is a separate index, filled from the event stream.\n\nA service with open uploads has to check content. Every photo goes through an automatic check for prohibited material, borderline cases go to a queue for moderators, and confirmed violations are removed from both feeds and caches. Private accounts and blocks are checked on every response. At the entrance there is a rate limiter against scraping and bots, and suspicious actions are slowed down. All of this is handled by separate consumers of queues and streams, not as part of the main request path.\n\nA photo-sharing service has to be available all the time: servers, cache, database, gateways, and workers are spread across three zones with headroom for losing one, rollouts go one server at a time, and alerts are tied to the burn rate of the error budget. The main costs are media storage and outgoing traffic. They are reduced by a delivery network with a high hit rate, by moving old photos to cold storage, by modern formats, by a reserved plan for the base load, and by spot instances for workers that tolerate interruption.\n\nLet's sum up. Publishing goes through object storage, a queue, and idempotent workers. Delivery is handled by the content delivery network. The feed is built as a hybrid with a cache, and like counters are accumulated and written in batches. Events go into a stream and are consumed by notifications and analytics. Recommendations use an expensive ranking service with graceful degradation, search is a separate index, and moderation is handled by separate consumers. In the exercises you will build the photo-sharing service stage by stage and take the exam.
