#CHAPTER 10 | Microservices and the API Gateway
Chapter 10. Microservices and the API Gateway. While the team and the service are small, the whole application lives in one process: this is a monolith. As it grows, pains appear: every change is deployed as a whole, one heavy module drags the scaling of everything with it, and ten teams get in each other's way in one repository. Microservices offer to cut the application into small services with their own data, teams, and deployments. But this is not a free gift; it is a trade of simplicity for independence, and first you need to understand the price.\n\nThe monolith has underrated virtues. A call between modules is a function call, not a network call: fast and reliable. A single database allows transactions and joins. It is easier to debug and to deploy. For most startups and mid-sized products, the monolith is the best choice. It can be made modular: clear boundaries inside one application, with its own data and interface for every module. Then cutting it into services later is easy if you ever need to, and meanwhile you do not pay the distribution tax.\n\nThe main question is where to draw the boundaries. You cut not along technical layers but along business domains: orders, catalog, payments, users. A good service is highly cohesive inside and loosely coupled outside: most changes touch one service. It owns its data and talks to others through an interface. A bad boundary shows in the symptoms: every change requires edits in three services, and the services constantly call each other. It is better to start with big pieces and split them once the real seams show up.\n\nThe rule of microservices: each service has its own database, and other services do not reach into it directly. A shared database turns the services into a hidden monolith: a change to a table breaks everyone. What we pay is that joins across services are unavailable: the data is assembled at the application level, or the needed copies are stored. For consistency we use events and sagas. In return, each service picks a suitable store, scales it independently, and the failure of one database affects only its own service.\n\nThe simplest way to communicate is a direct network call: over HTTP with JSON, or binary gRPC. It is easy to understand and gives an answer right away. But the price is coupling in availability and latency. If a call passes through five services at ninety-nine point nine percent each, the overall availability is already lower, and the latencies add up. So chains of synchronous calls are kept short, with timeouts and circuit breakers, and optional calls are moved to asynchronous mode.\n\nThe alternative is events. The orders service publishes an order placed event, and everyone else who is interested subscribes: delivery, notifications, analytics. The source of events does not know about its subscribers and does not wait for them. A new consumer is added without changes to the source. The coupling is loose, and failures do not spread synchronously. But the behavior of the system becomes harder to trace: the chain of reactions unwinds through events with no single place where the whole scenario is visible. For complex scenarios you use an orchestrator or a saga.\n\nIt is inconvenient for a client to know the addresses of dozens of services. A single entry point is provided by the API gateway. It routes requests to the right service, checks authentication and permissions, limits the rate, terminates encryption, and collects metrics. Sometimes it aggregates the responses of several services into one, saving the client round trips over the network. For different clients, mobile and web, separate gateways are set up for the needs of each. The gateway itself becomes a critical component: it is made redundant, and we try not to load it with business logic.\n\nService instances come and go, and their addresses change. To find a neighbor, we use service discovery. A registry keeps a list of live instances, which register themselves and report their health. The client asks the registry or calls by name through internal DNS. In orchestrated platforms this is built in. The next step is a service mesh: next to every service sits a proxy that takes over load balancing, retries, encryption, and metrics, so none of this has to be written in code.\n\nServices are deployed independently, so the interface between them is a contract that must not be broken. Compatible changes: adding an optional field, a new method. Incompatible ones: removing a field, changing the meaning or the type. As long as there are clients of the old version, they are supported: the new version of the interface is published next to the old one, and clients are moved over gradually. Consumers describe what they need with contract tests, and the provider checks that a change does not break them. This is cheaper than catching breakages in production.\n\nSince every service has its own database, a single transaction across several services is impossible. Operations like place an order, reserve the goods, charge the money are implemented with sagas: a chain of local steps with compensations. Data between services is synchronized with events: the orders service keeps a copy of the catalog fields it needs and updates it from events. Consistency becomes eventual: for a short time the copies diverge. Product decisions must take this into account: what will the user see if a step in the middle of the chain did not complete.\n\nA distributed system is harder to debug: a request passes through a dozen services, and the error can be in any of them. You need end-to-end tracing: every request is given an identifier, and all services pass it along, to assemble the full picture of the path. You need unified logs and metrics, dashboards for each service, and alerts. Add to this versions, deployments, tests at the seams, and service discovery. All of this is work, and it is justified only when the benefits of independence exceed the cost of maintenance.\n\nTypical traps. The distributed monolith: services are coupled so tightly that they can only be deployed together, and you pay with the network. Excessive granularity: hundreds of services of five lines each, with all the complexity in the connections. The shared database: a hidden coupling stronger than an interface. The safe path is gradual. Start with a monolith, and carve out services where there is a real reason: different load, a separate team, different reliability requirements. The method is called the strangler fig: new functionality is written as a service, and the old is gradually moved over and switched off.\n\nLet's sum up. The monolith is simple and, for many, optimal. Microservices give independence of teams, deployments, and scaling, but trade it for complexity. Boundaries are drawn along business domains, and each service has its own database. Synchronous chains are kept short, and the rest goes through events. The gateway is an entry point with routing, authorization, and limits, and it is itself made redundant. Contracts are changed compatibly. Consistency between services is eventual, with sagas instead of transactions. In the exercises you will place a gateway, split a service, and distribute traffic.
