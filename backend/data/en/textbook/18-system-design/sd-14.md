#CHAPTER 14 | Observability and Reliability Goals
Chapter 14. Observability and reliability goals. We designed the architecture, and it works. Now comes the longest part: operations. If you can't see what is happening inside, you learn about breakages from your users, and you hunt for causes blindly. Observability is the property of a system that lets you understand its internal state from external signals, and answer what broke and why without shipping new code. It isn't free, but it is cheaper than any outage.\n\nObservability rests on three kinds of data. Metrics are numbers over time: requests per second, error rate, load. They are compact and good for dashboards and alerts. Logs are records of events with details: what the request was and what the error was. They are bulky, but they give context. Traces show the path of a single request through all the services and the time spent at each step. Metrics tell you what broke, traces tell you where, and logs tell you why. These data are linked by shared identifiers, so you can jump from one to another.\n\nWhat should you measure first? The four golden signals. Latency: how long a response takes, measured separately for successes and failures, in percentiles. Traffic: how many requests arrive. Errors: the share of unsuccessful requests. Saturation: how full the limited resources are, such as CPU, memory, disk, and queues. For services there is the acronym RED: rate, errors, duration. For resources there is USE: utilization, saturation, errors. With these five or six graphs per service you can answer most questions in the first minute of an outage.\n\nA log is not text for a human to read, it is a stream of events. It is better to write entries in a structured form, for example JSON, with fields such as time, service, level, request ID, user, and error. That way they can be filtered and aggregated. The request ID ties together the entries of all services for one operation. Log volume grows fast and costs money, so people use severity levels, avoid excess output in production, sample frequent events, and limit retention. Secrets and personal data must never end up in logs.\n\nTracing shows the path of a single request. It is made of spans: each describes the work of one service or call, with its start, duration, and result. A context with the trace ID is passed in headers from service to service so that the whole picture can be assembled. Looking at the diagram, you can see right away that thirty of a hundred milliseconds went to a database query and twenty to waiting in a queue. Storing every trace is expensive, so you take a sample and always keep the slow and failed ones. Traces are indispensable in a system of many services.\n\nA reliability goal starts with an indicator that reflects the user's experience. The share of successful responses and the share of responses faster than a threshold are good indicators. CPU load is a bad one: the user doesn't care what the processor is doing as long as the response is fast. The goal is phrased as a share of good events over a window: ninety-nine point nine percent of requests successful over thirty days. The goal must be achievable and deliberate: each extra nine costs more, and users often can't tell the difference between three nines and four.\n\nThe error budget is one minus the goal. With a goal of ninety-nine point nine percent, zero point one percent of failures is allowed, which is about forty-three minutes of downtime per month. As long as the budget isn't used up, the team freely ships new things and experiments. When it's gone, new features are frozen and everyone works on reliability. This gives the argument between development speed and stability a fair rule: no persuasion is needed, you just look at the budget numbers.\n\nAn alert wakes a person up, so it must report a problem that has to be solved right now. Good alerts are tied to symptoms for the user: errors exceed the allowed level, responses became slow. The modern approach is the burn rate of the budget: if in one hour you burned so much budget that the monthly one would run out in two days, the alarm is urgent. A CPU load of ninety percent should not wake anyone. False alarms cause fatigue, and on-call engineers stop reacting. Every alert should come with instructions on what to do.\n\nWhen an alert fires, an incident begins. Roles are assigned in advance: the incident commander, who coordinates and makes decisions, the responders, and the person in charge of communicating with users. There are playbooks for typical situations and clear escalation rules. The first goal is to restore the service, not to find the cause: roll back a release, switch traffic, turn off a feature. You'll investigate later. All actions are recorded in a shared channel, and users are updated regularly on the status. This discipline turns the chaos of a night-time failure into a predictable process.\n\nAfter every serious incident, a review is written. Its goal is to learn, not to punish. The document contains the timeline of events, the impact on users, causes at several levels, what helped, what got in the way, and concrete improvement tasks with owners and deadlines. A blameless culture matters: if people are punished for a mistake, they stop talking about mistakes, and the system loses its only source of knowledge. Good questions sound like this: why did the system allow a person to make a mistake, rather than why did the person make a mistake.\n\nCapacity planning relies on data. You look at the growth of traffic and volume over months and forecast when the load will reach a threshold. Load tests show the real limit of the design: at what number of requests latency starts to grow and which component gives out first. You keep working headroom above typical peaks and account for seasonal events, such as sales and holidays. Purchasing and setup take time, so the decision is made in advance. The forecast is compared with reality and adjusted: a model that isn't checked against reality goes stale quickly.\n\nA good dashboard answers specific questions: is everything fine, what has changed, where to look next. The top level shows the golden signals for each service, and below it are details by component. Don't try to show everything. Watch the cost of observability. Metrics get more expensive with the number of unique label combinations: if you add the user ID as a label, the number of series explodes. Logs get more expensive with volume and retention. The solution is to choose what you really need and aggregate the rest.\n\nLet's sum up. Observability is built on metrics, logs, and traces linked by shared identifiers. You measure the golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. Reliability goals are phrased from the user's experience, and the error budget gives a rule for when to take risks and when to fix things. Alerts are tied to symptoms and to the budget burn rate. Incidents are run by roles, and afterwards a blameless review is written. Capacity is planned ahead. In the exercises you will choose a design to meet goals for availability and latency.
