#CHAPTER 24 | Project: Messenger
Chapter 24. Project: a messenger. Messages must arrive instantly, in the right order, and exactly once, even if the phone has lost the network. There are private chats and groups with thousands of members, sending photos and videos, delivery and read statuses, an online status, notifications, and syncing between several devices. The load here is millions of long-lived connections and a huge stream of small messages. We will repeat much of the book: persistent connections, queues, sharding by chat, and idempotency, but in their most demanding combination.\n\nHere are the numbers. Five hundred million users a day, each with an open connection: that is hundreds of millions of simultaneous connections. Messages number in the tens of billions per day, which means hundreds of thousands per second on average. Delivery must take fractions of a second. The main requirements are to lose no message, keep the order within a chat, and never deliver one message twice. We need high availability, and chatting must work on a bad network. Storage is nearly unlimited, but fresh messages are read orders of magnitude more often than old ones.\n\nEvery client holds a connection to a gateway. One gateway holds tens of thousands of connections, so there are thousands of gateways. They contain no business logic: they only accept frames, check permissions, and forward them on. A client connects to the nearest gateway, and the presence registry knows which gateway each user is sitting on. When a gateway fails, clients reconnect with a growing pause and some jitter, and the gateways limit the rate of accepting new connections, to avoid a storm. Gateways are rolled out in batches with draining.\n\nThe path of a message goes like this. The sender sends the message to the gateway along with a client ID. The message service assigns it a number within the chat, saves it to the database, and publishes an event to the stream. The recipient's gateway, found through the presence registry, delivers the message over the connection. If the recipient is offline, the message waits in storage and a push notification goes out to them. The sender gets back a confirmation of saving, and only after that does the tick in the app change. Every step is idempotent, because resends on a bad network are unavoidable.\n\nThe order of messages in a chat is determined not by arrival time but by a number assigned sequentially in one place for each chat. That is why a chat always belongs to one shard and one number allocator. The client generates its own message ID, and a resend with the same ID does not create a duplicate. Delivery follows the at-least-once scheme: the recipient acknowledges receipt, and the server repeats whatever is unacknowledged. The client remembers the last number it received, and each of the user's devices, phone, tablet, or computer, catches up on history independently by its own cursor after a drop.\n\nThere are a great many messages, and they are written constantly. The storage must accept writes quickly and return the latest messages of a chat in one query, so the data is sharded by chat ID and ordered by number within the chat. Sharding by time ranges is not an option: all new messages would land in the last shard. Fresh messages of active chats are kept in the cache, old ones are read straight from storage and over time move to cheaper media. The metadata of chats, members, and settings is a separate, small database.\n\nIn a private chat, a message has one recipient, in a group there are dozens or hundreds, and in a channel there are millions. The message is stored once in the chat's storage and delivered to each member separately. For small groups, the fan-out goes immediately to everyone who is connected. For large groups and channels, the fan-out turns into a stream of tasks in a queue, split into portions, and members who are offline get a push and pick up the messages when they open the chat. To avoid an avalanche, the fan-out rate is limited, and the hottest chats are moved to separate resources.\n\nThe online and typing statuses are short-lived data. Presence is kept in a fast cache with a short lifetime, refreshed by a heartbeat, and it is queried at the moment it is shown. Typing is not saved at all, it is passed as a transient event. Delivery and read statuses are a huge stream of small records. They are merged: instead of a hundred read marks, one mark is sent with the number of the last message read. Such events go through the stream and update only what needs to be shown, without writing every tick to the main database.\n\nPhotos, videos, and files do not go through the message service. The client uploads the attachment straight to object storage by a signed link, and the message itself carries only the link and a small preview. Video and large images are compressed by workers from a queue. Delivery is handled by a content delivery network, and access is limited to the chat's members through signed links with a short lifetime. For conversations with end-to-end encryption, the server stores and serves only encrypted data, and the keys belong to the clients.\n\nWhen the recipient is not connected, a push notification goes out through the phone's platform. The notification service takes the settings into account: group muted, do-not-disturb mode, a mention or an ordinary message. Many messages are merged into one notification, and the sending rate is limited. With end-to-end encryption the content does not go into the push, only a signal that there is something new. Push platforms are unreliable and rate-limited, so we need queues with retries, and the push itself is just a hint. The source of truth about delivery is the state of the messages on the server.\n\nA messenger must keep working through any failure. Gateways and services are placed in several zones with spare capacity for losing one, and in different regions to be close to users. A chat is tied to a home region, where message numbers are assigned, and a copy lives in another region for disasters. Losing a gateway breaks connections, but the messages are saved, and clients catch up by cursor. Losing a region requires a switchover, and for some time messages can only be accumulated on clients. Rollouts go cell by cell, and the metrics measure delivery latency and the share of lost messages.\n\nA messenger stores the most personal data there is, so protection is built in. Connections are encrypted, and so is data on disk. For secret chats, end-to-end encryption is turned on: the server sees only encrypted blocks and a minimum of metadata. Spam and mass mailings are held back by send rate limits, checks on new accounts, and user reports. Phone numbers and contacts are protected from being scraped by limits and by access auditing. Deleted messages are deleted for all members through an event, and the retention period of backups is limited in advance.\n\nLet us sum up. A messenger rests on gateways with persistent connections, storage sharded by chat, and message numbering in a single place. Delivery is at-least-once with duplicate filtering, and the client catches up on history by cursor. Groups and channels are fanned out through queues in portions, presence and statuses live in the cache and are merged, and attachments pass through object storage and the delivery network. Pushes only hint, while the truth is kept on the server, and reliability comes from zones and regions. In the exercises you will build the messenger in stages and take the exam.
