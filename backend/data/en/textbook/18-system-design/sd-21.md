#CHAPTER 21 | Project: Ride Hailing (Uber)
Chapter 21. Project: a ride-hailing service like Uber. The passenger taps a button, the system finds the nearest free drivers, offers the order to one of them, shows the approaching car on a map, calculates the price, and takes payment. What matters here is not the data in a database but the stream: hundreds of thousands of drivers continuously send their coordinates, and from them we have to answer the question of who is nearby right now, within a fraction of a second. On top of that, the project involves money, an external payment provider, peaks like rain, and an availability requirement.\n\nLet's start with the numbers. Twenty million rides a day is about two hundred and thirty orders per second, and three to four times that at rush hour. A million drivers online send their position every four seconds: two hundred and fifty thousand updates per second, and that is the main load. Matching a driver has to fit within a second, and the price and arrival time within a couple of seconds. Availability has to be very high: a passenger standing in the rain will not wait, and an unavailable service means lost money and reputation.\n\nDriver positions cannot be written to an ordinary database: two hundred and fifty thousand overwrites per second would kill any table, and old coordinates are of no use to anyone. For this we create a separate geo service. It keeps current positions in memory, splits the map into cells, and stores a list of drivers in each cell. A write always replaces the previous value, and every record has a short lifetime, so a driver who disappears drops out of the results on their own. The route history is written separately, in batches, through an event stream.\n\nHow do we find drivers nearby? The map is divided into cells by a geohash or by a hierarchical grid. A query determines the passenger's cell, takes it together with its neighbors, gets a list of candidates, and sorts them by distance. If nobody is nearby, the radius is widened in rings. In dense areas the cells get too crowded, so they are automatically split into smaller ones, and in empty areas they are merged into larger ones. The geo service is sharded by cells, so a search in one city does not load the others, and a dense downtown can be given to dedicated nodes.\n\nMatching is the heart of the service. The dispatcher takes candidates from the geo service, uses the ranking service to estimate each one's arrival time rather than just the straight-line distance, and offers the order to the best one. The driver has ten seconds to answer; if there is no answer, the offer goes to the next one. It is critical that one driver never gets two orders: the assignment is atomic, with the condition that the driver is free, and a repeated request is idempotent. Orders in one zone are processed one after another, so we don't create competition for the same drivers.\n\nA ride has several states: looking for a driver, driver assigned, driver on the way to the passenger, passenger in the car, completed, canceled. This is a finite state machine, and each transition is allowed only from certain states, which a conditional update of the record enforces. Ride data is sharded by city, because the passenger and the driver are almost always in the same city. Every transition is published as an event to a stream, from which notifications, price calculation, payments, and analytics read it. Because of that, the main record stays short and reliable.\n\nThe passenger watches the car move on the map. To make that work, both the passenger's app and the driver's app hold a persistent connection to a gateway. Driver positions go to the geo service and to the stream, and from there the gateway forwards them to the passenger, only for the current ride. The frequency is limited: four to five seconds is quite enough, and the client draws smooth movement by predicting the path along the route. If the connection drops, the app reconnects with a pause and jitter, so that a network outage doesn't create a storm of connections.\n\nArrival time and price are calculated by separate services. For the route we use a mapping service that accounts for traffic, and models provide a correction for real conditions. The price is made up of a rate for distance and time and a demand multiplier. The multiplier is computed by stream analytics per district: how many orders and how many free drivers there were over the last few minutes, and it is updated every thirty seconds. Results are cached by cell and by route, so the expensive models are not called on every request, and if they fail, an approximate estimate is shown.\n\nMoney is the most sensitive part. The payment provider is external, answers in hundreds of milliseconds, and sometimes makes mistakes. So when the order is placed, we do a pre-authorization for the expected amount, and the actual charge is made after the ride. Every request to the provider carries an idempotency key, so that a retry doesn't charge the money twice. Retries are done with growing pauses, and in case of a long outage the ride is completed anyway and the payment is carried through by a background saga with compensations. You need several provider channels and a fallback way to pay, so the failure of one doesn't stop the service.\n\nThe passenger receives notifications: driver assigned, driver arrived, ride completed. The driver receives order offers, and for those a one-second delay matters. All of these messages are produced by events from the stream and sent through a notification service that distinguishes priorities: an order offer is more urgent than a receipt. To connect the driver and the passenger, number masking through a calling service is used. If a push didn't arrive, it is duplicated with a message over another channel, and delivery is confirmed, so the order isn't wasted.\n\nThe service naturally divides by city: the passenger and the driver are almost always in the same city, and they don't need the data of another city. So every city or region works as a cell with its own geo service, rides database, and queues. A failure or overload affects one city, and a new version is rolled out cell by cell. Shared data, such as accounts, payment methods, and rates, lives in a global directory with replicas near the cities. A trip into another city is a rare case, and it is handled by a separate procedure.\n\nRain or the end of a match increases the load several times over within minutes. Autoscaling can't keep up, so we keep spare capacity and use protective measures. A rate limiter at the entrance protects against repeated taps. Queues smooth out the writing of states. Priorities give first place to the critical path: matching and contact matter more than ride history. Under overload the system degrades: it turns off exact price recalculation, lengthens the position update interval, and shows an approximate arrival time. But it must always be able to accept an order and get the passenger there.\n\nLet's sum up. The main load on a ride-hailing service is the stream of positions, and it is taken by an in-memory geo service sharded by map cells. The dispatcher matches a driver using an arrival time estimate and assigns them atomically. Rides are a state machine in a database sharded by city, and every transition goes into the stream as an event. Payments go through an external gateway with idempotency keys and a saga. Tracking works over a persistent connection, cities are cells, and peaks are survived with spare capacity, queues, and degradation. In the exercises you will build the service stage by stage.
