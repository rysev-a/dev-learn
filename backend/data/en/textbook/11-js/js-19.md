Modern Frontend. Chapter 19. CSS: Flexbox. Not so long ago, blocks were laid out any way people could: with floats, tables and clever margins. Flexbox solved the problem in one line: you turn on flexible layout on the parent, and its direct children line up in a row or in a column. The parent is called the flex container, the children are flex items, and the grandchildren take no part in the layout. The layout is one-dimensional: you control one axis at a time, and sizes adjust to the free space. It all starts with the value flex on the display property.\n\nEvery flex container has two axes. The main axis is the one along which the items are placed, and the cross axis is perpendicular to it. By default the main axis is horizontal and runs left to right, and the cross axis runs top to bottom. The direction of the main axis is set by the flex-direction property: row, row-reverse, column and column-reverse. When the direction changes, the axes swap places, so alignment is described with the words along and across, not horizontal and vertical. In right-to-left languages the main axis flips by itself.\n\nFree space along the main axis is distributed by the justify-content property. Flex-start pushes the items to the start of the axis, flex-end to the end, and center gathers them in the middle. Three other values hand out the space. Space-between leaves gaps only between the items and pushes the outer ones to the edges. Space-around gives every item equal margins on both sides, so the gap at the edges is half the gap between neighbors. Space-evenly makes all the gaps the same, including the outer ones. If the items fill the whole line, there is nothing to divide. The classic case is a site header: the logo on the left, the menu on the right.\n\nAcross the main axis, other properties do the work. Align-items aligns all items inside a line: to the start, to the end, to the center, to the text baseline, or stretched to the full height. Stretch is the default, which is why cards in a row come out the same height. Align-self overrides the alignment for one item and leaves its neighbors alone. Align-content controls the free space between the lines themselves when there are several, and does nothing in a single-line container. It is easy to remember: justify is along, align is across, items is about each item, and content is about the group of lines.\n\nSpacing between items used to be set with margins, and there was always a catch: the last item got an extra margin that had to be reset with a separate rule. The gap property solves this right in the container: it sets the space between neighbors and adds nothing at the edges. You can give one value for both directions, or two: first between rows, then between columns. There are also separate properties, row-gap and column-gap. Gap works together with justify-content: free space is divided as usual, and the gap stays as a minimum, so items never get closer than that. Browsers have supported it in flexbox for a long time now.\n\nBy default, a flex container keeps all items in one line and shrinks them if it has to. The flex-wrap property allows wrapping: when the next item doesn't fit, it moves to a new line. Each line becomes a separate flex line with its own main axis, and justify-content distributes space in each line independently of the others. The value wrap-reverse flips the order of the lines. This is where align-content comes in handy: it hands out the vertical space between the lines. The shorthand flex-flow sets the direction and wrapping together. But columns will not line up across lines on their own: for a real table you need grid.\n\nThe size of an item along the main axis is determined by three numbers. Flex-basis is the base size before the division: a width, a height, or the word auto, meaning based on content. Flex-grow says how many shares of free space the item takes; the default is zero, so it doesn't grow. Flex-shrink says how willingly the item shrinks when there isn't enough room; the default is one, so everything shrinks. The shorthand flex sets all three numbers at once. Flex one means grow, shrink, basis zero; flex auto means grow and shrink starting from the content size; flex none means don't grow and don't shrink. Write the shorthand, not the three properties separately.\n\nLet's work through the division with numbers. A container is six hundred pixels wide, with three items whose basis is one hundred each: three hundred is taken, three hundred is free. If all of them have flex-grow one, each becomes two hundred wide. If the first has one and the second and third have two each, that makes five shares, one share is sixty pixels, and the widths will be one hundred sixty, two hundred twenty and two hundred twenty. Now the opposite case: bases of two hundred, one hundred and one hundred, in a container of three hundred sixty, so forty is missing. The shortfall is divided in proportion to shrink times basis, and the items become one hundred eighty, ninety and ninety. The big one gives up more than the small ones.\n\nThe order property changes the visual order of items without touching the order in the markup. By default everyone has zero, items are arranged by increasing number, and ties go in document order. A negative number sends an item to the front, a positive one to the back. This is handy when a sidebar sits on the left on a wide screen but must come after the main content on a phone. But there is a trap: the keyboard and the screen reader follow the markup order, not the picture, and if focus jumps around the screen at random, the user gets lost. So order and the reverse directions are only for cosmetics and must not change the meaning.\n\nThe most common flexbox bug: a long word or a wide image pushes the container apart and sticks out past the edge. The reason is that the minimum size of a flex item is by default equal to the size of its content, not zero, so it won't shrink below the long word. It is fixed in one line: min-width zero, and for columns, min-height zero. Then the item can shrink, and you can turn on text truncation with hidden overflow and an ellipsis. Other mistakes. A sidebar got squashed: someone forgot to forbid it from shrinking. An item stretched to full height: align it to the start. A nested container doesn't grow: you need flex on the parent as well.\n\nAuto margins in flexbox are a small but powerful tool. An auto margin on any side takes all the free space and pushes the item in the opposite direction. The classic case: in a navigation bar, the last item gets an auto left margin and moves to the right edge, while the others stay on the left. An auto margin on both sides puts the item in the center, and on both axes at once it centers vertically too, which ordinary blocks couldn't do for decades. Unlike justify-content, auto margins act on a specific item, not on the line, and they win: when they are present, there is nothing left to divide.\n\nLet's put together some typical patterns. A navigation bar: a row, center alignment, a gap and an auto left margin on the last item. A row of cards: wrapping, a gap, and each card gets flex with growth and a basis equal to the minimum width you want. Centering: two properties on the container, center along and across. A sticky footer: a root column with a minimum height of the full screen, the main part gets flex-grow one, and the footer drops to the bottom by itself. The holy grail: a header, a footer, and in the middle a row of three columns where the center one grows and the side ones don't shrink. Each pattern is just a few lines.\n\nWhen flexbox, and when grid? Flexbox is made for a line: a toolbar, a row of buttons, the inside of a card; the content dictates the sizes. Grid is for a two-dimensional layout: a page layout, a gallery, a form with columns; the container dictates the sizes. They work well together: lay out the page with grid, and the lines inside the cells with flexbox. Here is a checklist. You turned on flex on the parent. You know which axis is the main one. You distribute space along with justify-content and across with align-items. You set spacing with gap, not margins. You enable wrapping with flex-wrap, and text truncation with min-width zero. And if the layout won't listen, open the developer tools: they draw the axes.
