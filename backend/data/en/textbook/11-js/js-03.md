#CHAPTER 3 | Slow Evolution and a Sudden Explosion: ES5, ES6, Babel, Bundlers and Preprocessors
Modern Frontend. Chapter 3. Slow Evolution and a Sudden Explosion: ES5, ES6, Babel, bundlers and preprocessors. Ten years passed between the third and the fifth editions of the standard: ES3 came out in nineteen ninety-nine, ES5 in two thousand nine, and the language barely changed. And then everything sped up: since twenty fifteen a new edition has come out every year, and around the language grew transpilers, bundlers and preprocessors that hardly any other language had. In this chapter we will see why it was slow, how the explosion happened, and where the tools came from that no frontend can do without today.\n\nWhy did it take so long? In the early two thousands the committee started work on the fourth edition: classes, modules, static types, namespaces. It was a whole new language. Inside the committee there was a split: Adobe and Mozilla wanted a big reform, while Microsoft and Yahoo, joined by Douglas Crockford, thought it was too risky. The arguments dragged on for years. In the summer of two thousand eight the committee made a decision: cancel the fourth edition. They kept some modest improvements, called ES3.1, which became the fifth edition, and promised to bring the ambitious ideas back later under the working name Harmony.\n\nThere was also a more down-to-earth reason: old browsers. If a new feature introduced unfamiliar syntax, a browser that did not know it treated the whole file as invalid and did not run the script at all. Internet Explorer six and seven stayed on many people's computers for years. Developers could not use arrow functions or classes while such browsers made up a noticeable share of the audience. So even approved features sat in the standard for years, and you could only use them with great caution. The speed of the language was set not by the committee, but by the oldest browser on the market.\n\nThe fifth edition came out in December two thousand nine. It brought strict mode, which forbids implicit global variables and other dangerous constructs, the built-in JSON object, the array methods forEach, map, filter and reduce, object methods such as keys and create, getters and setters, and bind for functions. These were exactly the things people used to write as polyfills. Internet Explorer nine supported the fifth edition in two thousand eleven, but version eight did not, and for years the community included libraries like ES5-shim. The fifth edition became the base that almost everyone relied on until the turning point.\n\nIn the same period an idea appeared that changed everything: write in a convenient language and compile to JavaScript. In two thousand nine Jeremy Ashkenas released CoffeeScript: short syntax without curly braces, arrows, classes, string interpolation, all compiled into plain code. It showed that a language could be improved from the outside, without waiting for browsers. Many CoffeeScript ideas later made it into the standard: arrow functions, classes, destructuring and rest parameters. Google's Dart and, later, TypeScript appeared in the same way. The word transpiler came into use for such compilers from one language to another of the same level.\n\nWork on modules was going on alongside. The CommonJS format from Node did not suit the browser, because require is synchronous and the network is slow. The AMD format and the RequireJS library loaded modules asynchronously. The UMD format was written so that code worked in both systems. And in two thousand eleven Browserify made it possible to write for the browser in the Node style: it found all the require calls and glued the files into one. That is how bundlers were born. Then came automation tools: Grunt in two thousand twelve, where tasks were described in a config, and Gulp in two thousand thirteen, where tasks were written as code and passed data through streams. They concatenated, minified, checked and copied files.\n\nAt the same time another pain point was being addressed: styles. CSS for a long time had no variables, no nesting and no functions, and as a project grew, the style sheets turned into thousands of repeated lines. Preprocessors added all this. Sass appeared in two thousand six, was written in Ruby, and got the popular SCSS syntax, compatible with plain CSS. Less in two thousand nine was written in JavaScript. Stylus in two thousand ten made braces and semicolons optional. Variables, nested rules, mixins and file imports gave a huge gain. PostCSS in two thousand thirteen turned style processing into a chain of plugins, the best known of which is Autoprefixer.\n\nIn June twenty fifteen the sixth edition came out, known as ES2015 or ES6. It is the biggest update in the whole history of the language: let and const, arrow functions, classes, template strings, destructuring, default parameters, rest parameters and spread, iterators and generators, symbols, Map and Set, promises and, finally, standard modules. Everything you saw in the previous chapters of this book arrived at once, in one package. The work took six years after the fifth edition, because the committee first split the Harmony ideas into pieces and then reviewed each one. A release of this size will never happen again.\n\nTo keep that from happening again, the committee changed the way it works. Every proposal goes through stages: from stage zero, an idea, to stage one, a formal proposal, stage three, a candidate, and stage four, when the feature is implemented in at least two browsers, tested, and will go into the standard. Editions became yearly and are named after the year: ES2016 brought the exponentiation operator and the includes method, ES2017 brought async and await, ES2018 brought spread for objects, and so on. At the same time browsers began to update automatically: Chrome did so from the very start, and Edge moved to the Chromium engine in two thousand twenty. The oldest browser stopped holding the language back.\n\nBut even after ES6 came out, browsers took years to implement it. So in two thousand fourteen Sebastian McKenzie, then still a schoolboy, created 6to5, later renamed Babel. The idea is simple: parse the code into a tree, replace the new constructs with equivalent old ones, and print it back out. An arrow becomes a function, a class becomes a constructor function, let becomes var. Missing objects, such as Promise, are filled in by polyfills from the core-js library. The list of target browsers is set by the browserslist file, and sets of rules are plugged in as presets. Now you could write in tomorrow's language today. Draft proposals from the committee also went through Babel, and people tried them out before release.\n\nBig bundlers appeared too. Webpack by Tobias Koppers, started in two thousand twelve, came into mass use by twenty fifteen: everything becomes a module, whether scripts, styles, images or fonts, and loaders transform each type of file. It could split code into chunks, run a development server and update modules without a reload. Rollup by Rich Harris in twenty fifteen focused on standard modules and tree shaking, that is, removing unused code, and Parcel in twenty seventeen offered running with no configuration. Webpack configs turned into an art of their own, and the choice of tools into JavaScript fatigue. In March twenty sixteen one developer pulled an eleven-line package called left-pad from npm, and builds around the world broke.\n\nIn the mid-twenty-tens frameworks grew up around the bundlers: Backbone and AngularJS in two thousand ten, React in two thousand thirteen, Vue in two thousand fourteen. We will not go through them, but their influence is huge: components, the virtual tree, markup inside code. Microsoft's TypeScript, which appeared in two thousand twelve, added static types on top of JavaScript. Later, bundlers got faster thanks to other languages: esbuild by Evan Wallace in Go in two thousand twenty, SWC in Rust, and Vite by Evan You, which uses the browser's native modules during development and builds the product with Rollup. CSS itself gained variables, grid and nested rules, absorbing part of what preprocessors offered.\n\nLet's sum up. The language stood still for ten years because of a split in the committee and old browsers. ES5 brought strict mode, JSON and array methods, and ES6 in twenty fifteen delivered the whole modern language at once. After that the standard became yearly, and browsers began to update themselves. Babel made it possible to write in the new language for old browsers, and bundlers, package managers and preprocessors formed the frontend toolchain. The history is finished: now we study the language itself, for now without a browser, starting with the first program and values.
