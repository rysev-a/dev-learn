#CHAPTER 21 | CSS: Responsive Design, States and Animations
Modern Frontend. Chapter 21. CSS: Responsive Design, States and Animations. We close the layout section with what makes a page feel alive. People open sites on phones, tablets, laptops and huge monitors, and one page has to look good everywhere. That is responsive design. More than half of visits now come from mobile devices, so you start thinking from the phone. The first thing a page head needs is the viewport meta tag with the device width. Without it, a phone pretends to be a desktop, shows the site tiny, and shrinks the whole page to fit.\n\nThe main tool of responsive design is the media query. It is a rule with a condition: the styles inside apply only if the condition is met, most often if the window is at least or at most a given width. The widths at which the layout is rebuilt are called breakpoints. You choose them not by device models, but by the moment the content starts to look bad. The most convenient approach is mobile first: you write the base rules for a narrow screen, and then extend the layout with queries on minimum width. That way the phone gets less code and does not have to override anything extra.\n\nWidth is not the only condition. The browser tells you which color scheme the person prefers, light or dark, and it is enough to override the color variables inside a single query. There is a condition for the wish to reduce motion, and we will come back to it at the end. There is the screen orientation, portrait or landscape. And there are conditions about the input device: whether the main device can hover, and how precise the pointer is. A mouse is precise, a finger is coarse, so on touch screens the buttons should be bigger. All this lets you adapt not to sizes, but to the abilities and habits of the person.\n\nA media query looks at the whole window, but a component lives in different places: in a wide column, in a narrow sidebar, in a modal window. A card in a sidebar should not depend on the screen width. That is what container queries were invented for. You declare on the parent that it is a container, usually by its inline size, that is, its width. After that, a rule with a container condition fires when the parent itself becomes wider or narrower than a threshold. The component stops knowing about the page and decides for itself how to look, which means you can put it anywhere.\n\nStep-like breakpoints are a bit crude: the layout jumps at the boundary. Smooth values come from the clamp function. You pass it three numbers: a minimum, a preferred value and a maximum. A font size, for example, grows with the window width, but never gets smaller than what is readable and never grows to something absurd. The preferred value is most often computed as a fraction of the window width, in viewport units. Paddings and gaps are handled the same way. The result is fluid typography: one line instead of a chain of queries, and the sizes change continuously, from a phone to a wide monitor, without a single jump.\n\nImages are a frequent cause of broken responsive layouts. If a photo is wider than its column, it sticks out past the edge and a horizontal scrollbar appears. Two properties fix it: a maximum width of one hundred percent and an automatic height. Then the image shrinks to the column and keeps its proportions. For covers, you set an aspect ratio and a fill mode: the image covers the block, the extra is cropped, and nothing is distorted. Remember the chapter on images: a set of sizes through srcset gives a phone a small file and a wide screen a large one. Layout is responsible for how the image fits, and markup for which file to download.\n\nThe page reacts to what a person does, and layout describes every state. Hover highlights the element the mouse points at. Active fires while the button is held down. Focus shows where the keyboard is. For focus, it is better to use focus-visible: the outline appears when you navigate with the keyboard and does not get in the way of the mouse. You must not remove the focus outline without replacing it with a visible one. Touch screens have no hover, and a site that hides something important behind hover is inaccessible there. Hover effects are kept as decoration and turned on with a condition that checks for the ability to hover.\n\nWithout animation, states change with a jerk. The transition property asks the browser not to jump but to glide smoothly from the old value to the new one. It needs four things: which property to animate, how long it lasts, which timing function the motion has, and what delay comes before the start. Acceleration and deceleration are set by keywords: ease, linear, ease-in, ease-out. The transition is written in the normal state of the element, and then it works in both directions: when the pointer arrives and when it leaves. A tenth of a second is too fast, a full second is too slow: interface effects live in the range from two to five tenths.\n\nThe movement of an element is set by the transform property. Translate shifts it along the axes without touching its neighbors. Rotate turns it by a given angle. Scale stretches or shrinks the element. You can list several transformations in a row, and they are applied in order. The transformation happens around a reference point, transform-origin: by default it is the center, but you can take a corner or an edge. The main point is that transform does not move the neighbors and does not change the page layout, the element is simply drawn in another place. That makes it ideal for animation: nothing around it needs to be recalculated.\n\nA transition goes between two states. When you need more, for example an endless pulse or a multi-stage appearance, you use keyframes. First, the keyframes rule describes the animation by name: what happens at percentages from zero to one hundred, or between the words from and to. Then you write the animation property on the element: name, duration, timing function, delay. The number of repeats is set by iteration-count, and it can be made infinite. The fill mode decides what is visible before the start and after the finish: both keeps the starting frame and the final frame. And direction runs the animation back and forth.\n\nNot every property is equally cheap to animate. The browser builds the page in three steps: layout, paint and compositing. If you change the width, the margins, or the position through left, the layout is recalculated and everything around it is repainted, and the animation starts to stutter on weak phones. The transform and opacity properties work on the last step: the browser moves the element to a separate layer and moves it with the power of the graphics card. That is why those are the ones you animate. The will-change hint prepares a layer in advance, but layers take memory, so you turn it on selectively, right before the animation, not on all elements at once.\n\nAnimation can be harmful. Spinning elements, parallax and large shifts cause dizziness and nausea in people with vestibular disorders, and flashing is dangerous for people with epilepsy. Operating systems let you choose a reduce motion setting, and the browser passes it to the page through the prefers-reduced-motion condition. Inside such a query you turn off unnecessary animation or replace motion with a soft fade. You do not have to switch off effects completely: useful hints stay, while endless spinning and large shifts disappear. This is not a whim, but a basic part of accessibility.\n\nLet us sum up with a checklist. The viewport meta tag is in the head. The layout is built from the mobile version, and the breakpoints are chosen by content. Components react to their own container. Text sizes and spacing are fluid. Images do not stick out of their columns. Every state, including focus, has a visible look. Only transform and opacity are animated, and motion respects the user's setting. It is convenient to check all this in the browser developer tools: device mode changes the window width and emulates touch, and the animations tab slows down and breaks down every frame. Now you have everything you need for layout.
