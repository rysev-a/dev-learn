#CHAPTER 20 | CSS: Grid
Modern Frontend. Chapter 20. CSS: Grid. Flexbox lays out items along one axis, while grid works in two at once: rows and columns. The container is turned on with a single value of the display property, and its direct children become grid items. The grid is made of lines, which are numbered starting from one; the space between two neighboring lines is called a track, a column or a row, and the intersection of a row and a column is a cell. An item can live in one cell or stretch across several. Next we'll look at how to define the tracks and how to place content on them.\n\nColumns are set with the grid-template-columns property, rows with grid-template-rows: track sizes are written separated by spaces. A size can be given in pixels, in percentages, or in a special unit, fr. An fr is a share of the free space: first the fixed sizes and gaps are subtracted, and then the remainder is divided among the tracks in proportion to the numbers. A column of two hundred pixels, then one share and two shares, gives one fixed column and two flexible ones, with the second twice as wide as the first. Repeated tracks are written with the repeat function: three equal columns is repeat of three, one share.\n\nGaps between tracks are set by the gap property: one value for rows and columns together, or two separated by a space, first for rows, then for columns. Gap adds no margins at the edges, only space between neighbors. If there are more items than cells in the explicit grid, the browser builds implicit rows on its own, and by default their height is determined by the content. They are controlled by the grid-auto-rows and grid-auto-columns properties: for example, every new row is a hundred pixels tall. This way the grid grows with the data, and you don't have to count rows in advance.\n\nOften you need a track with limits: no narrower and no wider than something. For this there is the minmax function with two arguments, a minimum and a maximum. A column from two hundred pixels to one share will never shrink below two hundred, but will take all the free space. There are also keywords: auto stretches to fit the content, min-content takes the narrowest possible value, and max-content takes the widest. And there is a pitfall: an fr track is by default never smaller than its content, so a long word can push a column apart. The cure is minmax from zero to one fr: then the columns stay equal.\n\nThe most useful trick is a responsive grid without a single media query. We write repeat with the keyword auto-fit, and as the second argument minmax from two hundred pixels to one fr. The browser itself counts how many two-hundred-pixel columns fit in a row and stretches them across the full width. On a phone you get one column, on a wide monitor, five. Auto-fill differs in that it keeps the empty tracks: the columns stay in their places even when there are few items. Auto-fit collapses the empty tracks and stretches the rest.\n\nItems can be put in specific places, by lines. The grid-column property takes a start and an end line separated by a slash: from line one to line three is two columns. Instead of the end line you can write the word span and a number of tracks: the item will take that many columns, starting from its own position. Negative numbers count from the end: from line one to minus one means the whole row, however many columns there are in the explicit grid. For rows, grid-row does the same. Items can also overlap, and the order of the layers is set by z-index.\n\nFor a whole page, another approach is more convenient: give the areas names. The grid-template-areas property draws the grid in words: each row is in quotes, names are separated by spaces, and the same name several times in a row merges into one area. An empty cell is marked with a dot. Then each item gets the grid-area property with an area name, and takes its place. The layout reads like a sketch on paper: header on top, menu on the left, content on the right, footer at the bottom. Areas must be rectangles, otherwise the declaration is invalid. Just change this declaration, and the whole page is rearranged.\n\nGrid has two kinds of alignment, and they are easy to mix up. The first is content inside the cells: justify-items aligns along the row, that is horizontally, and align-items aligns along the column, that is vertically. The shorthand place-items sets both at once, and the value center puts everything in the middle of the cell. For a single item, use align-self and justify-self. The second is the grid itself inside the container, if the tracks don't fill all the space: that is handled by justify-content, align-content and the shorthand place-content.\n\nBy default, items fill the grid row by row. The grid-auto-flow property changes the direction: the value column fills columns first. The word dense turns on dense packing: if wide items have left holes, the browser takes the next item that fits and puts it into the hole. A gallery of tiles of different sizes comes out without gaps. The price is that the order on screen stops matching the order in the document, while the keyboard and the screen reader follow the document. For meaningful lists, it is better not to use dense packing.\n\nA grid item can itself be a container, including a grid. A nested grid lives inside its own cell, has its own tracks and gaps, and knows nothing about the outer grid. Because of that, the lines of the outer and inner grids don't line up. To make them follow shared lines, the value subgrid was introduced: the nested container takes the tracks of its parent. The classic example is a row of cards whose titles, texts and buttons sit on the same lines, even though the cards have different amounts of text. Subgrid works in all modern browsers.\n\nLet's put together some typical layouts. A page: two columns, a narrow fixed one for the menu and a flexible one for the content, and three rows, header, main part and footer; the header and footer span both columns. On a narrow screen, inside a media query, the columns turn into one. A gallery and cards are a responsive grid with auto-fit, minmax and a gap. Notice that there are no percentages anywhere and no clever width calculations. We describe the tracks, and the browser does the rest of the work. The whole layout fits in about ten lines of styles.\n\nGrid or flexbox? If items go in one direction and their size is determined by the content, use flexbox: a menu, a row of buttons, a toolbar. If a two-dimensional structure matters and the container sets the sizes, use grid: a page, a gallery, a form with labels and fields. The two approaches combine very well: grid builds the frame, and inside the cells flexbox lines up buttons and labels. Don't be afraid of nesting. The same task can often be solved either way, and you should pick the one that reads more easily.\n\nThe easiest way to debug a grid is in the developer tools. Next to an element whose display is grid there is a badge: click it, and the browser draws lines, line numbers and area names over the page. Track sizes are visible there too, and implicit tracks are marked separately. Now the checklist. Tracks are set with fr and minmax, gaps with gap. Responsiveness comes from auto-fit, and a media query is needed only when the grid needs a different structure. Areas are named by meaning, and the order in the document matches the order on screen.
