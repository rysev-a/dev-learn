#CHAPTER 3 | Combinatorics: permutations and combinations
Discrete mathematics. Chapter 3. Combinatorics: permutations and combinations. Every estimate of the kind "this brute force will finish in a hundred years" and every hack-resistant password starts with the same thing — the ability to count the options without listing them. Combinatorics is the arithmetic of choice: how many ways there are to do something made of many small decisions. Today we will master two rules and three counters, on which almost all engineering estimates rest.\n\nThe sum rule sounds trivial, but everything begins with it. If you are choosing exactly one thing, and the first group has three options and the second has four, and the groups do not overlap, then there are seven options in total — we add. A lunch menu: three soups or four salads — seven ways to choose a first course. In code this is branching: each branch of a condition is its own group of options, and the total number of execution paths is added up across the branches.\n\nThe product rule is noticeably more powerful. If you make several independent choices in a row, the number of options multiplies. A four-digit PIN code: ten possibilities at each position, ten to the fourth power in total — ten thousand combinations. Nested loops are a direct embodiment of the product rule: the outer loop over one list, the inner over another, and the number of iterations of the body is the product of the lengths. As soon as you see a chain of independent choices — multiply.\n\nLet's apply the product rule to passwords. Eight characters from a set of ninety-five printable symbols give ninety-five to the eighth power options — about six and a half quadrillion. Every extra character multiplies this space by ninety-five, and adding a new alphabet raises the base. From this comes a simple security conclusion: a long password of plain words is usually stronger than a short one of symbols, because the space grows faster with length. A brute-forcer is limited not by the attacker's talent but by the size of this space.\n\nFor counting orderings we need our own unit of measurement — the factorial. N factorial is the product of all numbers from one to n: four factorial equals twenty-four. It grows frighteningly: ten factorial is more than three and a half million, and twenty factorial goes past a quintillion. This is exactly why problems of brute-forcing all orderings so quickly become impossible: the factorial overtakes the exponential already at modest numbers, and no hardware will save you.\n\nA permutation is a way to arrange n objects in places, and there are exactly n factorial such ways. The running order of ten talks at a conference is ten factorial options, more than three and a half million schedules. The classic engineering illustration is the traveling salesman problem: visit all the cities and return, choosing the shortest route, and the number of routes is the factorial of the number of cities. With fifty cities the full brute force will not finish in a reasonable time, and that is exactly why approximate algorithms are invented for such problems.\n\nNow we choose not everything but a part, and see whether order matters. An arrangement is a choice of k objects out of n when order matters: gold, silver and bronze among twenty athletes are three different awards, not one company of finalists. The formula in words: n factorial divided by n minus k factorial — we mentally line everyone up in all n factorial ways and cut off the tail of those left without places. A PIN code without repeated digits is also an arrangement: ten factorial divided by six factorial, which is five thousand and forty options. The main question at this step: if you swap two chosen items, do you get a different combination? If yes — we count an arrangement.\n\nIf order does not matter, we count combinations — and this is the most widely used number in applied combinatorics. A combination of n choose k, pronounced "n choose k", is an arrangement divided by k factorial: we divide by all the permutations inside the chosen group, because now they are indistinguishable. Five cards from a deck of fifty-two — fifty-two choose five, about two and a half million hands. Choosing two reviewers from six in the pool — six choose two, fifteen options. The same selection with and without regard to order differs by a factor of k factorial.\n\nThe poker numbers deserve a separate look, because they show how combinations compress the space. There are two and a half million hands, but only about ten ranking categories: a pair, two pairs, a straight and so on. All those millions of hands are spread across the categories very unevenly, and the rarity of a category makes it higher. This is a general technique of analysis: first count the total number of outcomes, then group the outcomes by the property of interest and count the group. The ratio of the group size to the whole is the probability, but let's not get ahead of ourselves today.\n\nA special case of combinations is so useful that it deserves its own paragraph: n choose two is the number of pairs. Ten people in a room will shake hands ten choose two times — forty-five handshakes. In code this is the number of iterations of a loop in which the second index is always greater than the first — comparing all pairs without repeats and without mirror duplicates. This is also the number of edges in a complete graph: for each of a hundred servers to be able to reach each of the others, you need four thousand nine hundred and fifty channels.\n\nThe newcomer's main mistake is double counting: the same thing counted twice, or order taken into account where there is none. The cure is simple: take a tiny example and count by hand. Three friends, choose two: by hand you get three pairs, and the formula three choose two gives the same three — it matches, so the formula can be trusted on large numbers. Before any counting problem it is useful to classify it: is it the sum rule, the product rule, a permutation, an arrangement or a combination — and half the solution is already ready.\n\nNotice how the chapters link up. A combination is simply a subset of size k from a set of size n, that is, combinatorics grew straight out of the sets of the first chapter. The product rule is the Cartesian product from the second chapter, counted by the number of elements. And permutations and combinations will meet us again in the next chapter: Pascal's triangle and the binomial theorem will be built from them. Discrete mathematics is valuable precisely for these links — every new tool reuses the old ones.\n\nLet's sum up. Two rules — the sum for mutually exclusive groups and the product for independent choices — give the frame for any estimate. Then the counters: n factorial for permutations, arrangements for choosing with order, and n choose k for choosing without order, while n choose two lives separately on counting pairs. Apply them to passwords, PIN codes, poker hands and handshakes — and you will start estimating brute force before you run it. In the next chapter — Pascal's triangle, the binomial theorem and the pigeonhole principle: three tools that will teach you to see structure where before there was only crude arithmetic.
