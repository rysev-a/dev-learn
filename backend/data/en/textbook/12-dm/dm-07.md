#CHAPTER 7 | Induction and recurrences
Discrete mathematics. Chapter 7. Induction and recurrences. You write recursion almost every week: walking a tree, parsing nested JSON, descending through directories. But each time the same question stands before you: how do I know that this will work for all inputs, and not only for those I checked? The answer is given by mathematical induction — a way to prove a statement for an infinite number of cases in two steps. Today we will learn to use it, and along the way we will honestly count how much time fast sorts take.\n\nLet's start with a staircase. Imagine an infinite staircase and two facts: you can stand on the first step, and from any step you can step up to the next. What follows from this? That you will be able to get onto any step — the hundredth or the millionth: you stood on the first by the first fact, and then the second drags you higher and higher. Induction needs nothing more: these two facts are called the base and the step, and together they cover all the steps at once.\n\nMathematical induction says the same thing formally. Suppose there is a statement about natural numbers. If it is true for one — that is the base — and if from its truth for an arbitrary k follows its truth for k plus one — that is the step — then the statement is true for all natural numbers. Note that we did not check the statement for every number — there are infinitely many. We checked two facts, and the chain of steps automatically carried the truth to any number you name.\n\nLet's go through a classic example that is useful in itself. The statement: the sum of all numbers from one to n equals n times n plus one, divided by two. Base: for n equal to one, there is a one on the left, and on the right one times two divided by two — also one, it matches. Step: suppose the formula holds for k; we prove it for k plus one. Add k plus one to both sides and transform carefully: you get k plus one times k plus two divided by two — exactly the form of the formula for the next number. The circle is closed, so the formula is true for all n.\n\nIf the step seemed like a trick, here is the intuition in one picture. Write the numbers from one to a hundred, and under them the same sum in reverse order: a hundred plus ninety-nine and so on down to one. Each pair in a column gives one hundred and one, there are a hundred columns, so the sum of two copies is a hundred times one hundred and one, and of one copy — half of that. According to legend, this is exactly how the schoolboy Gauss outwitted his teacher's assignment to add the numbers up to a hundred in his head. A guess says "looks like it's so", and induction says "it is so, for all" — together they make mathematics reliable.\n\nInduction can be strong. In an ordinary step we rely on the truth of the statement for exactly k, while in strong induction we allow ourselves to rely on all the previous values at once: if the statement is true for all numbers less than k, then it is true for k as well. It sounds like a liberty, but it is a legitimate technique: the staircase simply has more supporting steps. It comes in handy wherever the step depends not on one previous value but on several — for example, in proofs about factoring numbers into primes.\n\nNow the main bridge to your everyday code: induction and recursion are twins. The base of a recursion is the base of an induction: the case solved without a recursive call. The recursive call is the step: we solve a smaller problem, assuming that its solution is already correct. If the solution is written by these rules, correctness follows from induction automatically, and there is nothing to prove separately. Hence also the main source of errors: a missing base or an unreachable base breaks the very first step, and the whole staircase collapses. A stack overflow is induction without a base, descending into eternity.\n\nA relative of induction is recurrence relations: formulas in which each term of a sequence is expressed through the previous ones. The classic is the Fibonacci numbers: the n-th number equals the sum of the n-minus-first and the n-minus-second, and the base sets the first two values. Compare with a recursive function: the base is the exit condition, the formula is the calls with smaller arguments. A recurrence is the mathematical notation of an algorithm that solves a problem through its smaller versions. And most importantly: from such a formula you can compute not only values but also the running time of an algorithm.\n\nTake merge sort. Its structure: split the array in half, sort both halves, and merge the results in linear time. So the time on an array of n elements equals the time on two halves plus n for merging — that is the recurrence. Let's lay out the levels: the array is split into two pieces of n over two, then into four of n over four, and so on down to pieces of one element — about log n levels. At each level the total work across all the pieces stays n. In total log n levels times n work — n log n, and that is proved, not measured.\n\nQuicksort lives by the same law with one caveat. If the pivot splits the array roughly in half, the time again comes out as n log n: the same levels, the same linear work per level. But if the pivot turns out to be an extreme each time, no splitting happens — an array of n elements turns into an array of n minus one, and there become n levels. The worst case is n times n, that very square of nested loops. That is why libraries choose the pivot at random: not to always be fast, but to make the failure improbable. Recurrences let you compute both scenarios before running the code.\n\nAnd what about loops, where there is no recursion at all? There is a native technique for them: the loop invariant. An invariant is a statement that is true before each iteration and after each one; it is proved exactly by induction. Before the first turn the invariant is true — the base; if it is true before a turn, then it is also true after the turn — the step. When the loop has finished, the invariant plus the exit condition give exactly that the loop solves the problem. This is how the correctness of insertion sort, binary search and many other workhorses is proved.\n\nA few practical takeaways for your collection. When you write recursion, first formulate the base and make sure each recursive call moves toward it, otherwise the staircase never ends. When you prove an algorithm, look for a statement that stays true at every step: for recursion it is the induction hypothesis, for a loop it is the invariant. When you estimate time, write down the recurrence: how much one level costs and how many levels there are in total. Three guiding questions — and "seems to work" turns into "works, and here is why".\n\nLet's sum up. Induction proves statements for all natural numbers in two steps: the base and the step — like a staircase with a first step and a rule for stepping up. Strong induction allows relying on all the previous values at once. Recursion is induction in code, and its base is a matter of life and death for the stack. Recurrences write such algorithms as formulas and give the running time: merge sort costs n log n, and an unlucky quicksort costs a square. In the next chapter we move on to graphs — structures where recursion and traversals come into their own: the browser's DOM tree, social networks and package dependencies.
