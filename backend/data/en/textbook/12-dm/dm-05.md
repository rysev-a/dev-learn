#CHAPTER 5 | Propositional logic
Discrete mathematics. Chapter 5. Propositional logic. Every time you write a condition in an if statement, you are doing propositional logic — just without the formal apparatus. And every tangled inversion of a condition, every argument about the precedence of "and" and "or", every suddenly green test — all of it is the territory of today's chapter. This lesson opens the logic part of the course and will teach you to take apart Boolean expressions like a construction kit, not like a spell.\n\nLet's begin with the proposition — the atom of the whole chapter. A proposition is a statement that is objectively either true or false: "two is greater than one" is true, "today is Wednesday" is true or false depending on the day, but there is no third option. Not propositions: the question "what time is it", the command "turn off the light", and the phrase "this sentence is false" — the last is a paradox, because it cannot be assigned either of the two values. In code the closest relative of a proposition is an expression of Boolean type: anything you can put in a condition.\n\nCompound propositions are built from simple ones with three basic operations. "Not" flips the truth. "And" is true only when both parts are true. "Or" — and here is the first catch — is true when at least one part is true, including the case of both: the mathematical "or" is inclusive, not exclusive as it sometimes is in everyday speech. Precedence is worth remembering too: "not" binds tighter than "and", and "and" tighter than "or" — which is why parentheses in complex conditions are written not for beauty but for unambiguity.\n\nA truth table is a way to write out the behavior of a formula in all possible cases. For two variables the table has four rows: true-true, true-false, false-true, false-false. For three variables — eight, and in general for n variables there are two to the power of n rows: you recognize the count from the chapter on sets — the power set has returned. A table is a full enumeration of inputs, and that is why it gives a guaranteed answer: a formula of five variables is checked with thirty-two rows, not by intuition.\n\nThe fifth operation is the most counterintuitive — implication, "if, then". The first part is called the premise, the second the consequence. An everyday example: "if it rains, then the pavement is wet". Notice the honesty of this construction: it promises only that rain makes the pavement wet, but does not promise that rain is the only cause — the pavement may be wet from a sprinkler, and the implication is not violated. In code implication hides in every early return: if the argument is empty, return an error — that is it.\n\nAnd now the catch that everyone trips over. When the premise is false, the implication is true — automatically, for any consequence. The statement "if two is greater than three, then I am a ballerina" is formally true, because two is not greater than three. It sounds wild, but there is engineering sense in it: the contract "if the input is invalid, abort" is not violated when the input is valid — the check quietly passed. From the same place comes the rule many have seen in the standard library: the statement "all elements satisfy the condition" on an empty array honestly returns true — no elements, no violators.\n\nThe sixth operation is equivalence: "if and only if". It is true when both parts coincide: both true or both false, and false when they diverge. Equivalence is a two-way implication: the second follows from the first and back. In tests it lives everywhere: the expected result equals the actual one — otherwise the test is red. And in refactoring too: when you say "the new condition is equivalent to the old one", you are asserting that their truth tables matched in every row.\n\nFormulas fall into interesting classes according to the whole truth table. A tautology is true in all rows without exception: "it is raining or it is not raining". A contradiction is false in all rows: "rain and no rain at the same time". For code these are direct diagnoses: a tautological condition is a dead check that always passes, a contradictory condition is an unreachable branch. Linters and compilers clean such places up automatically, but in architectural reasoning — in "always", "never" and "either this or that" — contradictions hide more often than it seems.\n\nAs with sets, logical operations have laws, and most of them look natural. The order of "and" does not matter, nor does the order of "or"; with the same operation, parentheses can be placed as convenient. A double negation cancels: "it is not true that it is not true" is the original statement unchanged. There is also distributivity: a common factor is taken out of parentheses and put back, as in school algebra — and it works for both "and" and "or". These laws are the algebra of conditions: with them, expressions are simplified as mechanically as numbers in a school exercise.\n\nThe most useful laws are again De Morgan's, now for logic. "It is not true that a and b" is the same as "not a or not b"; "it is not true that a or b" is the same as "not a and not b". An everyday check: if I did not take the umbrella and the keys at the same time, it means I did not take the umbrella or did not take the keys. These two formulas are literally the instruction for inverting conditions in code: "not" of an "and" gives an "or" of negations, "not" of an "or" gives an "and" of negations. Everyone who has turned a condition inside out for the sake of an early return has applied De Morgan, sometimes without knowing the name.\n\nA practical bridge into programming languages is short-circuiting. Logical "and" and "or" in most languages are evaluated lazily: if the left part already decides the outcome, the right part is not evaluated at all. The classic example is the check "the user exists and the user's name is longer than five characters", where the second part is guaranteed not to run on a null reference. Short-circuiting is not a compiler trick but a direct consequence of the truth tables: the result of "and" is already known when the left part is false. Here logic is not a metaphor but a working contract, on which both protection from null references and lazy evaluation are built.\n\nHow do you apply all this daily? First — simplify conditions with the laws before writing: fewer negations, less nesting, parentheses when mixing "and" and "or". Second — test by the truth table: each row of a condition's table is a separate test case, and branch coverage is in essence the coverage of rows. Third — read other people's conditions aloud: "at least one of the two is not satisfied" is almost always clearer than a wall of exclamation marks. Formal logic is not an academic ritual but a way to make a condition understandable before it runs, not after the bug.\n\nLet's sum up. Propositional logic has given us a vocabulary: the proposition, the operations "not", "and", "or", implication with its false premise, equivalence, truth tables, tautologies, De Morgan's laws and short-circuiting. These are rules for handling individual statements, and already at this level half of the tangled conditions get cleaned up. Where to go next? In the next chapter — predicates and quantifiers: we will learn to make statements not about one particular number but about all of them at once, or at least about one. This is exactly how properties of programs are formulated: there are no negative elements, a suitable index exists, for any request the answer arrives on time.
