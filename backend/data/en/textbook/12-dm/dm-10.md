#CHAPTER 10 | Probability and Bayes' theorem
Discrete mathematics. Chapter 10. Probability and Bayes' theorem. This is the final chapter of the course, and it is about the most honest way to think in a world where nothing is guaranteed. A cache hits or misses, a feature test wins or doesn't, an alert turns out to be real or false — every day of yours consists of random events that can be reasoned about strictly. We will go through classical probability, conditional probability and Bayes' formula, and finish with a summary of the whole journey. I promise: the spam filter at the end will be well deserved.\n\nLet's begin with the classical definition, and it is almost naive. If all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the share of favorable outcomes among all of them. A die has six faces, so the probability of rolling an even number is three out of six, that is, one half. A coin has two outcomes, the probability of tails is one half. Notice how close combinatorics stands: as soon as the outcomes become many, counting their shares without the counting rules from the first chapters will no longer be possible.\n\nAnd combinatorics here is not a guest but the hostess. We toss five coins: the number of all outcomes is two to the power of five, because each coin doubles the options. We choose a starting five from twenty developers — twenty choose five, and without this formula you would be recounting options until evening. The famous birthday paradox stands on this: in a room of twenty-three people the probability of a shared birthday is more than fifty percent, because there are almost two hundred and fifty pairs there. The moral: intuition about randomness is unreliable, while counting formulas are reliable.\n\nNext — events and the arithmetic over them. The probability that the first event or the second happens is the sum of their probabilities minus the probability that both happen: otherwise the common cases would be counted twice. The probability that both happen depends on the link between the events, and that is our next conversation. In the language of code: "a database error or a network error" is a union, "a database error and the retries exhausted" is an intersection. Write the event in words, and the formula assembles itself.\n\nEvents are called independent if knowing about one changes nothing in the estimate of the other. Two different dice are the classic: a six came up on the first, and about the second we still know only one sixth. For independent events the probability of both at once is simply the product of the probabilities. It is important not to confuse independence with incompatibility: "an even number came up" and "a three came up" are incompatible — they will not happen together — but dependent: having learned about the three, you know for certain that it is not even. Dependence is about information, not about simultaneity.\n\nAnd now the main tool of the chapter: conditional probability. It is the probability of an event recalculated taking into account that another has already happened. It is cloudy — the probability of rain is higher than on a clear day, although the climate is the same. Formally: the probability of a given b equals the probability of both at once divided by the probability of b — we squeeze the world down to the cases where b happened and look at the share of a among them. Almost everything meaningful that you say about the future is a conditional probability: it is just that usually you do not spell out the condition.\n\nBayes' formula is the rule for turning a condition around. It says: the probability of a cause given an observed effect is proportional to the probability of the effect given the cause, multiplied by how probable the cause was by itself. This formula has a human reading: we update our confidence when new data appears. There was a prior confidence — how many times the service had crashed before; evidence arrived — the monitoring fired; it became a posterior — how much we now believe the crash is real. Bayes is not a spell but honest bookkeeping of beliefs.\n\nLet's go through the famous trap with numbers. The disease is rare: one person in a thousand has it. The test is good: it catches a sick person in ninety-nine cases out of a hundred, but it also wrongly flags a healthy person in one case out of a hundred. The question: the test is positive — what is the probability of the disease? Intuition screams "ninety-nine percent", and mathematics says about nine: out of a hundred thousand people there will be about a hundred sick whom the test will catch, and about a thousand healthy whom it will falsely flag. A positive result more often means an error than a disease, because there are too many healthy people. This is not a defect of the test but the power of the base rate.\n\nThe same mechanism drives the spam filters that all email lives with. The prior probability of spam is the experience of all past mail, say every third letter. The word "free" in a letter is evidence: among spam it occurs incomparably more often than among normal letters. Bayes' formula multiplies the prior confidence by how characteristic the word is of spam and gives an updated estimate; a dozen different words — and the letter goes off to the spam folder. Notice the elegance: the filter does not "know" that it is looking at spam — it updates its confidence word by word.\n\nThe trap from the paragraph about the disease has a name: base rate neglect. People are quite good at estimating how characteristic evidence is of a cause, and fail on the question of how common the cause is overall. Hence practical rules. An alert that fires rarely, but on a rare event, is almost always false — set the thresholds taking the prior frequency into account. A perfectly accurate tool for a rare phenomenon still produces mostly false alarms, which is why rare things are searched for in a cascade: a cheap rough filter first, then an expensive accurate analysis.\n\nProbabilistic thinking shows up in engineering at every step. Evaluating a cache is the probability of a hit multiplied by the gain, minus the probability of a miss multiplied by the cost: decisions about capacity and eviction are made on these numbers. A Bloom filter answers "maybe present" with a controlled probability of error and "definitely absent" without error — and saves millions of database queries. An A/B test is Bayes in the other direction: from the accumulated data we update our confidence that a variant is better, and decide when it has passed a reasonable threshold. Even choosing the number of retries and replicas is an estimate of failure probabilities.\n\nAnd one more area where Bayes is a workhorse: diagnostics. A service went down, and five alerts lit up on the dashboard. The probability of each cause given these symptoms is a Bayes problem: the prior frequency of failures is multiplied by how characteristic the symptom is of each cause. An experienced on-call engineer does this intuitively — starts with what breaks more often and better explains the picture; formulas only make the intuition reproducible. Good incident documentation is, in essence, an accumulated table of the prior frequencies of your system.\n\nLet's sum up — and at the same time close the whole course. We began with language: sets, relations and combinatorics gave a way to describe data and count options. Then came logic: predicates and quantifiers taught us to formulate statements, and induction and recurrences taught us to prove them and estimate running time. Then graphs: vertices, edges and traversals became the frame for DOM trees, dependencies and routes. Today probability and Bayes added the most important thing — the ability to reason where there are no guarantees. This whole course is now your tool, not someone else's: next comes programming in Python and algorithms, and there all of it will come to life line by line.
