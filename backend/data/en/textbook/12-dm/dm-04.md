#CHAPTER 4 | The binomial theorem, Pascal and the pigeonhole principle
Discrete mathematics. Chapter 4. The binomial theorem, Pascal and the pigeonhole principle. At first glance these are three different things: a triangle of numbers, a formula for raising a sum to a power, and a joke about rabbits and cages. In fact they are three views of one question — how to count without enumerating. Today we will tie them together and see how the pigeonhole principle explains the collisions in hash tables that you live with every day.\n\nLet's start with Pascal's triangle, because it is built without a single formula. At the top is a one; along both edges there are ones; each number inside equals the sum of the two numbers above it. The third row is one, two, one; the fourth is one, three, three, one; and so on forever. Notice how honestly the triangle grows from a single rule of addition: no multiplication, no division, and in the end you get a table of all the combinatorics of choice.\n\nThe link with the previous chapter is direct: row number n is all the combinations n choose k, written out in order. Why this happens is easy to see through movement: going down from the top to a number, you make n steps, each to the left or to the right, and the number in the table counts the routes to that cell. And a route is exactly a choice of which of the n steps to turn left on, that is, a combination. Programmers know a grid with obstacles: the number of paths from corner to corner moving only right and down is the same n choose k.\n\nFrom the triangle you can instantly read off properties we met in the chapter on sets. The sum of all the numbers in row n equals two to the power of n — and a row is all subsets of an n-element set, grouped by size. The row is symmetric: n choose k equals n choose n minus k, because choosing whom to take is the same as choosing whom to leave. And Pascal's rule is in plain sight too: n choose k equals the sum of two neighboring numbers in the previous row — take or do not take a particular element. The triangle is a visual reference book in which these equalities are seen, not memorized.\n\nNow the binomial theorem, and in words it does not sound scary at all. To raise a sum of two terms to the power n means to go through all the ways in which the first term appeared some number of times and the second some number of times, and to add up the results. If the first term appeared n minus k times and the second k times, the coefficient of such a term is exactly n choose k, the number of ways to place them in their positions. Therefore the coefficients of the expansion are a row of Pascal's triangle, entire: raising a sum to the fourth power means expanding it along the row one, four, six, four, one.\n\nThe practical strength of the binomial is quick estimates without a calculator. Take one plus a small number and raise it to a large power: the main role is played by the term in which the small number appears to the first power — and the estimate is ready in seconds. This is how compound interest is estimated, load growth under a small daily deterioration, approximations in the analysis of algorithms. The general skill is one: expand the expression by the binomial, see which terms dominate, and drop the tail. Discrete mathematics loves such honest simplifications — they keep the essence and remove the noise.\n\nWe move on to inclusion and exclusion — honest counting over overlapping groups. For two sets the rule is this: the cardinality of the union equals the sum of the cardinalities minus the cardinality of the intersection. Forty people started the audio course, thirty watched the webinar, and ten did both: sixty people were reached in total, not seventy — we counted ten twice and subtracted them a second time. This is exactly what a union in a database does: it does not return duplicates, that is, it quietly performs inclusion and exclusion for you.\n\nWith three sets a beautiful pulsation begins: add, subtract, add. The cardinality of the union of three sets equals the sum of the cardinalities, minus the sums of the pairwise intersections, plus the intersection of all three. A problem about languages: out of a hundred people, fifty-five know English, forty-five know German, twenty-five know French; English and German together — eighteen, English and French — twelve, German and French — eight; all three languages — five. We count: fifty-five plus forty-five plus twenty-five, minus eighteen, minus twelve, minus eight, plus five — that comes to ninety-two. So eight people know none of the three languages, and the question is closed without a single enumeration.\n\nAnd finally the pigeonhole principle — the simplest and the trickiest tool of the course. The statement: if there are more rabbits than cages, then at least one cage holds at least two rabbits. That's all, with no conditions on how the rabbits are seated: the conclusion is guaranteed by the numbers alone. The strength of the principle lies elsewhere — it proves existence without showing where exactly: we learn that a coincidence exists, but we don't learn which cage to look in.\n\nFor a programmer this principle is a sentence and a consolation at once. A hash table has a finite number of cells, while the elements can be any number, so when it is filled beyond the number of cells a collision is guaranteed — not "possible" but guaranteed. From this comes all the machinery you are used to: chains inside cells, open addressing, growing the table with rehashing. A hash function cannot remove collisions — it can only make them rare and irregular. Fighting the very possibility of collisions is pointless; designing the table so that collisions are cheap is sensible.\n\nThe same principle gives the famous birthday surprise: in a group of twenty-three people it is more likely than not that two share a birthday, although there are three hundred and sixty-six days in a year. Intuition fails because it counts people, whereas you have to count candidate pairs: there are almost two hundred and fifty of them, and each pair only needs to match. And the classic existence proof: in a city of a million there are two people with exactly the same number of hairs on their heads, because a person has fewer than four hundred thousand hairs, and there are a million people. There is no need to look for either pair — Dirichlet has already said everything.\n\nEven cryptography lives under this law, just with huge numbers. Any hash has a finite space of outputs, so with enough files a collision must happen — the only question is how large that space is and how expensive it is to find a pair. A good hash function makes the space practically unimaginable, and digital signatures and integrity checks rest on that. A thought for any design in the future: finiteness is structure, not a detail, and the pigeonhole principle will sooner or later remind you of itself.\n\nLet's sum up. Pascal's triangle is a table of combinations built by a single addition; its rows sum to two to the power of n and read as all subsets. The binomial theorem expands a power of a sum along Pascal's row and gives quick estimates. Inclusion and exclusion correct double counting on overlapping sets, and the pigeonhole principle guarantees collisions wherever there are more rabbits than cages — from hash tables to birthdays.
