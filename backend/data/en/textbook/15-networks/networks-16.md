#CHAPTER 16 | Firewalls, Proxies, and NAT in Practice
Chapter 16. Firewalls, proxies, and NAT in practice. A network connects everyone with everyone, and security means not connecting what should not be connected. A firewall decides which packets to let through, NAT rewrites addresses at the border, and a proxy acts as a middleman for requests to outside sites. In this chapter we will put this together into a practical toolkit: how the filter in Linux works, how to write rules, why we need zones and the demilitarized zone, how the kinds of NAT differ, and how port forwarding combines with filtering.\n\nLet's start with the idea. A firewall is a set of rules placed on the path of traffic. The first principle is default deny. Everything that is not explicitly allowed is dropped, and exceptions are listed one by one. That way the list of what is allowed stays short and easy to audit, and anything forgotten stays closed. The opposite scheme, where everything is allowed except what is listed, goes stale with every new service. The second principle is to keep rules as narrow as possible. Not the whole world to the web server, but port four hundred forty-three; not the whole office to the database, but only the application server.\n\nIn the Linux kernel this is handled by netfilter. On its way, a packet passes through several checkpoints called chains. Prerouting fires before the routing decision. Input applies to packets destined for the host itself, output to packets originating on the host, forward to transit packets, and postrouting fires after the routing decision. Rules are grouped into tables by purpose: filter handles admission, nat handles address rewriting, and mangle handles header changes. The tools iptables, nftables, ufw, and firewalld are just different front ends on top of the same mechanism.\n\nNow about the rules themselves. Each rule describes a condition, made of interfaces, addresses, protocol, ports, and connection state, and an action: accept, drop, or reject. The chain is read from top to bottom, the first matching rule fires, and if nothing matches, the chain's policy applies. That is why order matters. The most important condition is the connection state. A stateful firewall remembers connections, and the rule that accepts already established traffic lets replies in without opening the door to new connections. This allows clients inside the network to go out freely while letting nobody in from outside.\n\nNAT comes in two kinds. Source NAT, also known as masquerade: packets from the internal network leave with the gateway's public address, and replies come back through the translation table. Destination NAT, or DNAT: packets that arrive at the public address and port are redirected to an internal server. This is port forwarding. One important subtlety: DNAT fires before the filter, so the forward rule already sees the internal address and port. A forgotten permission for the internal address is the most common reason for the complaint that forwarding is configured but does not work.\n\nTo avoid leaving internal systems exposed, the network is divided into zones. The internal network is trusted, the internet is untrusted, and servers that must be reachable from outside are moved into the demilitarized zone, the DMZ. The rules between zones are simple. From the internet you may go into the DMZ, and only to the necessary ports. From the internal network you may go to the zone and to the internet. From the zone, nowhere into the internal network. Then, if a server in the zone is compromised, the intruder stays in an isolated segment and does not get free access to workstations and databases.\n\nA classic problem of its own is the loop through the gateway. An internal client contacts a server by its public address, the gateway redirects the packet to the internal server, and the server sees a source from its own network and replies to it directly, bypassing the gateway. The client was waiting for a reply from the public address, receives one from the internal address, and discards it. The fix is source NAT for such traffic on the gateway, or split DNS, where inside the network the name points straight to the internal address.\n\nA proxy is a middleman at the application layer. The client talks to the proxy, and the proxy goes to the site on its own behalf. This gives you a point of control: allow lists and block lists, authentication, logs, and a shared cache. Direct internet access for workstations is then closed on the firewall, and the proxy remains the only path. There is also a transparent proxy, which intercepts traffic without any client configuration. A reverse proxy works the other way around: it stands in front of the servers and accepts requests from outside clients. We will talk about it in the next chapter.\n\nHow do you avoid breaking things? Write rules from top to bottom, from specific to general, and always test them from both sides: from inside to outside and from outside to inside. Turn on logging of dropped packets, at least in the important places: without it, finding the rule that blocks something takes hours. Before saving a configuration, leave yourself a way to roll back, otherwise one wrong rule will cut you off from your own server. In the cloud, the role of the firewall is played by security groups and network access lists, but the principles are the same. In the exercises you will set up port forwarding, zones, the loop through the gateway, and a proxy.\n\nLet's sum up. Default deny, narrow rules, and connection tracking are the foundation of a firewall. Netfilter applies chains and tables in a strictly defined order, and DNAT fires before the filter. Zones and the demilitarized zone limit the damage from a break-in. A proxy provides a controlled path outward, and source NAT solves the problem of the loop through the gateway.
