#CHAPTER 19 | Network Attacks and Defense: Spoofing, DoS, MITM
Chapter nineteen. Network attacks and defense: spoofing, DoS, and MITM. Every protocol we have studied was designed with honest participants in mind, and almost every one has a flip side. We will look at the typical attacks through the eyes of a defender: how reconnaissance works, what address spoofing and interception are, what a denial of service looks like and what stops it, and then we will put together the principles of layered defense. You need to know attacks not in order to launch them, but to understand why firewall rules, encryption, and monitoring exist.\n\nEvery attack begins with reconnaissance. A port scanner walks through addresses and ports and watches who answers: an open port replies with a handshake acknowledgment, a closed one replies with a reset, and a filtered one stays silent. From the replies and from program banners, attackers work out which services and which versions are running on the machine, and therefore which known vulnerabilities they have. Scanning by itself is not a break-in, but it shows that you are on somebody's target list. The defense here is simple: expose only the ports you really need, do not reveal too much in banners, and notice scanning in your logs and packet captures.\n\nOn a local network the classic is ARP spoofing. ARP has no authentication, and a node that receives a reply simply believes it. An attacker on the same network sends the victim replies like "the gateway's address is my MAC," and tells the gateway "the victim's address is my MAC." Now the victim's traffic flows through the attacker's computer, and the attacker reads it, changes it, or just drops the packets. This is a man-in-the-middle attack, or MITM. The signs: the gateway's entry in the ARP table has a foreign MAC, replies are duplicated, and latency grows. The defense: static entries for critical addresses, switch port security, ARP inspection, and above all, encryption on top.\n\nA similar spoof is possible higher up. If a client gets its address over DHCP, a rogue server on the network can hand out its own gateway and its own DNS. If a resolver trusts replies, they can be forged and written into the cache: a victim typing the name of a bank ends up at someone else's address. Switch ports that are allowed to answer protect against rogue DHCP servers. Against DNS spoofing, you have DNSSEC signatures, encrypted queries, and, once again, encrypted connections with certificate checks: even at a foreign address, the right certificate will not be there.\n\nNow, denial of service. The goal is to make a service unavailable by exhausting some resource. A SYN flood overwhelms the server with half-open connections: the queue fills up, and real clients cannot connect. Volumetric attacks clog the link itself with a stream of packets. Then there is amplification: the sender fires a short request in the victim's name at an open DNS or NTP server, and that server sends the victim a long reply, so the stream grows many times over. And if there are thousands of sources, it is a distributed attack, a DDoS, and you cannot filter out a single node.\n\nWhat helps? Against a SYN flood there are SYN cookies: the server keeps no state until the handshake is confirmed, and instead encodes it in the sequence number of its reply. Rate limiting on the firewall and the load balancer cuts off simple floods. Closing open resolvers means they do not amplify other people's attacks. Against volumetric attacks your own link is no longer enough: the traffic is diverted to a content delivery network or to a scrubbing provider, where it is filtered on enormous capacity. And plan for headroom: a link and servers that already run at their limit will fall over at any spike.\n\nInterception is dangerous only while the traffic can be read. That is why the main defense against MITM, at any layer, is encryption with authentication. When a browser sees a certificate that a trusted authority has not vouched for, it warns the user; HTTP Strict Transport Security forbids opening a site over a plain channel at all, and certificate pinning keeps the authority from being swapped out. Fake Wi-Fi hotspots with an attractive name are dangerous too, and so is the habit of clicking "continue" on a warning. Any browser warning is not just an "error"; it is a signal that someone is standing in the middle, or that an administrator forgot to renew a certificate.\n\nAnother common vector is password guessing against services: an open SSH server or a mail server is constantly under brute force, and login and password pairs leaked from other services are tried against yours. The defense: keys instead of passwords, two-factor authentication, lockout after several failures, a limit on the rate of attempts, and tools like Fail2ban that automatically add the sources to the firewall. Minimal privileges and a separate account for each service limit the damage if one of them is broken into after all.\n\nAll these measures add up to layered defense. First: expose as little as possible to the outside, and keep everything else closed by the firewall and a deny-by-default policy. Second: segment the network into zones, so that breaking into one part does not give access to everything. Third: encryption everywhere, including the internal network. Fourth: timely updates, because most successful attacks use long-known vulnerabilities. Fifth: monitoring, meaning firewall and server logs, network statistics, intrusion detection systems, and the habit of looking at normal behavior so that you can spot an anomaly.\n\nLet us sum up. An attack begins with reconnaissance, and closing unneeded ports and keeping logs give you the first line of defense. Spoofing of ARP, DHCP, and DNS puts the attacker in the middle, and encryption with authentication protects against it. Denial of service exhausts a resource, and it is fought with cookies, limits, and diverting traffic. Passwords are replaced with keys and a second factor. The foundation of security is layering: little exposed, segments, encryption, updates, and monitoring. The last chapter is about networks in the cloud and in containers.
