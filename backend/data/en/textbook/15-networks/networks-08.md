#CHAPTER 8 | Reliability and Congestion Control: Windows, Loss, Slow Start
Chapter 8. Reliability and congestion control: windows, loss, slow start. We have seen how TCP delivers bytes without loss. Now the question of speed: how fast should we send? Too slowly, and the link sits idle. Too fast, and the router buffers overflow, losses grow, and the useful speed drops. TCP knows neither the capacity of the link nor the number of competitors, so it finds the speed as it goes. In this chapter: the receive window, the congestion window, slow start, the sawtooth, and modern algorithms.\n\nLet's start with the receiver. It has a limited buffer, and in every acknowledgment it reports the receive window size: how many bytes it is ready to accept beyond those already acknowledged. The sender must not have more unacknowledged data than this window. This is flow control. A simple formula follows from it: the speed is no higher than the window size divided by the round-trip time. A window of sixty-four kilobytes with a delay of eighty milliseconds gives only a little over six megabits per second, even if the link is hundreds of megabits.\n\nTo fill the link, you need to keep in flight as many bytes as it can hold, that is, the product of bandwidth and delay. For this, TCP uses a sliding window and pipelining. Instead of sending a segment and waiting for the acknowledgment, the sender immediately sends many. Each acknowledgment moves the window forward and allows the next portion to be sent. Acknowledgments arrive at the same pace at which the data left, and they "clock" the sending themselves. If the window is smaller than the capacity of the link, the link sits idle, and this is exactly what is called a long fat network.\n\nBut the receiver is not the only one that may fail to cope. The network itself can be congested: somewhere on the path there is a bottleneck whose queue overflows. Back in nineteen eighty-six, the internet went through a collapse, when everyone resent what was lost and the useful speed fell by hundreds of times. That is why the sender has a second window, the congestion window. It grows while all is well and shrinks on losses. In practice, you may send no more than the minimum of the two windows: the receive window and the congestion window.\n\nHow does TCP begin? With a small window, about ten segments, because it does not know the link. Then comes slow start, although in fact it is fast: for every acknowledgment the window grows by one segment, which means it doubles in one round trip. Ten, twenty, forty, eighty: exponential growth. This goes on until a threshold is reached or a loss happens. For a short transfer, for example a web page of a few kilobytes, this is enough, and the whole transfer takes place in the start phase.\n\nNext comes congestion avoidance. Near the threshold the window grows carefully: by one segment per round trip. The loss of a segment is a sign of congestion: the window is cut in half, and slow growth starts again. This gives the famous sawtooth: a smooth climb and a sharp drop. This scheme is called AIMD, additive increase, multiplicative decrease, and it is what makes the sharing of a link fair: when several connections compete, their windows even out on average.\n\nHow does TCP learn about a loss? There are two signals. A timeout is complete silence for the RTO time: the situation is serious, the window drops to one segment, and a new slow start begins. Three identical acknowledgments mean that one segment is lost while the rest arrive: the sender quickly resends it and only halves the window. The selective acknowledgment extension lets the receiver say exactly which blocks it has received, so that only the needed ones are resent. In practice, duplicate acknowledgments save the speed, while timeouts kill it.\n\nThe classic sawtooth is not ideal on fast and long links, and other algorithms appeared. CUBIC, the default in Linux, grows along a cubic curve and after a loss cuts the window not by half but to seventy percent. BBR from Google works differently: it measures the bandwidth and delay of the link and keeps the speed near their product, hardly reacting to individual losses. This gives good speed where losses are not caused by congestion, for example on Wi-Fi or over a satellite. And explicit congestion notification, ECN, lets a router report congestion without dropping packets.\n\nA last touch: buffers. A big buffer at a bottleneck seems to save you from losses, but it fills to the brim, and every packet waits in the queue for hundreds of milliseconds. This is called bufferbloat. The speed is high, but a call or a game turns into a slideshow. What helps is shorter queues and smart disciplines such as fq_codel, which drop packets before the queue grows. So a good answer on a loaded network is not the biggest queue, but the shortest queue that still keeps the link full.\n\nHere is the summary. The receive window protects the receiver, the congestion window protects the network, and you may send no more than the smaller of the two. Slow start quickly grows the window, and congestion avoidance carefully searches for the limit and backs off on loss. CUBIC and BBR react to losses in different ways, and huge buffers hurt latency. In the exercises you will enlarge the window, switch the algorithm, and shorten the queue. Next we move from theory to code: sockets in a program.
