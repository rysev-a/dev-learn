#CHAPTER 1 | How a Network Works: Nodes, Links, and Packets
Computer Networks. Chapter 1. How a network works: nodes, links, and packets. Almost every program today talks to other programs: a browser asks for a page, a phone checks the mail, one service calls the service next door. All of it runs on the same few simple ideas. In this book we will follow a byte from the network card all the way to a web request, and understand why the internet is built the way it is. In the first chapter we introduce the vocabulary: nodes, links, packets, delays, losses, and protocols, and see how they add up to a network of networks.\n\nLet's start with the parts. A network node, or host, is any device that sends and receives data: a computer, a phone, a server, a camera. Nodes are connected by links: a wire, an optical fiber, the radio air. Between them sit intermediate devices. A switch connects the nodes of one local network, and a router passes data between networks. Every link has a speed, its capacity in bits per second, and a length, which determines how long a signal takes to travel. We usually draw the whole picture as a graph: nodes are vertices, links are edges, and the path of the data is a chain of edges.\n\nHow do we send data across such a graph? Old telephone networks used circuit switching: for the length of your call, a path with guaranteed bandwidth was reserved for you. Reliable, but expensive: while you are silent, the link sits idle, and nobody else can use it. The internet chose packet switching. A message is cut into pieces, each piece travels on its own, and shares links with other people's data. While one node is silent, another uses the freed capacity. This trick is called statistical multiplexing, and it is exactly what let thousands of users share one wire and pay little for it.\n\nWhat is a packet? It is a piece of data with a header stuck on the front. The payload is a part of your message. The header is service information: where the packet comes from and where it goes, how big it is, and how to put the message back together. At every layer of the network another header is added, like an envelope inside an envelope; we will talk about that in the next chapter. The size of a packet is limited: on ordinary Ethernet, the maximum payload of a frame is fifteen hundred bytes, a value called the MTU. A big message has to be cut up, and the receiver has to glue it back together. Tiny packets are expensive because of their headers, and huge ones hold up their neighbors.\n\nNow, about time. The delay of a packet on one hop is made of four parts. The first is processing: the router reads the header and chooses a path. The second is waiting in a queue, if the link is busy. The third is transmission: to push L bits onto a link running at R bits per second, you need L divided by R seconds. The fourth is propagation: the signal travels along the cable at about two thirds of the speed of light, roughly two hundred thousand kilometers per second, which is five microseconds per kilometer. Add these terms up over all the hops of the path, and you get the delivery time of the packet.\n\nDon't confuse speed with delay. Throughput tells you how many bits the link pushes through per second; delay tells you how much time passes between sending and receiving. A wide pipe with a long path can deliver a lot, but slowly: that is how satellite links work. The product of throughput and one-way delay is called the BDP, the bandwidth-delay product, or the capacity of the link: that many bits are in flight at the same moment. If the sender sends less than that, the link sits partly idle. One more quantity is jitter, the variation of delay from packet to packet: it does not matter for downloading a file, but it matters a lot for voice and video.\n\nWhere do queues and losses come from? A router receives packets on several links and sends them out one at a time. As long as the arrivals do not exceed the departures, packets pass straight through. When at some moment more arrive than can leave, the extra ones wait in a buffer. The buffer is finite: once it is full, new packets are simply dropped. A loss in such a network is not a disaster, but a normal way of telling the sender: slow down. Buffers that are too large are harmful too: packets wait for seconds, the delay grows, and yet nothing is lost. This phenomenon is called bufferbloat, and home routers suffer from it.\n\nFor all these different devices to understand each other, we need protocols. A protocol is an agreement about the format of messages and the rules of exchange: who speaks first, what kinds of messages exist, what to do on an error. You can compare it to the etiquette of a phone call. Protocols are described in public documents called RFCs, and they are developed by an organization called the IETF, where anyone can take part in discussing changes. Thanks to this, equipment from different vendors works together. Each protocol solves one task: carry a frame over a wire, find a route, deliver bytes reliably, request a web page. A stack is built out of them.\n\nThe internet is a network of networks. It consists of tens of thousands of autonomous systems: the networks of providers, universities, clouds, and companies, each with its own administrator. Providers connect to each other: they pay bigger ones for transit, while equals exchange traffic directly, which is called peering. The exchange happens at internet exchange points. Networks inside a building are called LANs, and networks between cities are called WANs. Nobody has a map or a single control panel, and the network still works, because everyone follows the same protocols.\n\nLet's sum up. A network is nodes and links; data is cut into packets with headers, which share links with other people's packets. Delivery time is made of processing, queueing, transmission, and propagation. Throughput and delay are different things, and overloaded routers drop packets, and that is normal. The order of communication is set by protocols described in RFCs, and the internet unites thousands of independent networks. Next we will see how protocols line up into layers, and how a single packet is wrapped in several headers. To do that, we will meet the OSI and TCP/IP models.
