#CHAPTER 18 | SSH, VPN, and Tunnels
Chapter 18. SSH, VPN, and tunnels. Servers and networks have to be administered remotely, and offices and employees have to be connected to each other across the unreliable internet. There are two sets of techniques for this. SSH gives you a protected remote shell and port forwarding. A VPN builds an encrypted tunnel, and ordinary packets of private networks travel inside it. In this chapter we will look at how they work, how to establish trust the first time, how to log in with keys, how to go through a bastion, how to build a tunnel between offices, and how not to send extra traffic into it.\n\nLet's start with SSH. It is a protocol for secure access to the command line, on port twenty-two. It replaced the now-forgotten telnet and rlogin, where the password went in clear text. SSH encrypts the entire session, verifies the identity of the server, and lets you log in with a password or with a key. Besides the shell, it can transfer files and also set up tunnels for other protocols. That makes it the main tool of a system administrator, and it is exactly why an open port twenty-two is constantly scanned and hit with password guessing.\n\nHow does the client make sure it has connected to the real server? The server has a host key. On the first connection the client shows its fingerprint and asks whether to trust it: this is trust on first use. The accepted key is written to the known hosts file. On the next connection the client compares the key, and if it has changed, it shows a loud warning: either the administrator changed the key, or you are talking to a fake server. You must not silence that warning by reflex. First confirm with the administrator that the change is legitimate, and only then delete the old entry.\n\nNow, about logging in. Passwords get guessed by bots, so serious servers accept only keys. A key pair is created with the ssh-keygen command: the private part stays with you, and the public part is copied to the server, into the user's list of authorized keys, usually with the ssh-copy-id command. Logging in with a key does not send a secret over the network: the client proves it owns the private key with a signature. The private key is protected with a passphrase and restricted file permissions. After you have checked that it works, you turn off password login on the server, and password guessing becomes pointless.\n\nOften servers are hidden in an internal network, and only one node is open to the outside, a bastion. Then you connect through it: the jump option tells the client to log in to the bastion first, and from there open a connection to the target, without exposing internal servers to the internet. SSH can also forward ports: local forwarding makes a remote service available on a local port, remote forwarding does the opposite, and dynamic forwarding turns the connection into a proxy. This is a handy but potentially dangerous mechanism: a user who is allowed to log in can reach any service on the network through the server.\n\nA VPN, a virtual private network, solves a different problem: joining networks and devices as if they were on one private network. There are two typical scenarios: remote access for an employee into the corporate network, and linking offices to each other. The essence is the tunnel: the original packet, addressed to a private network, is packed whole into a new packet that travels across the internet between two public addresses, and at the other end it is unpacked and continues on its way. An outside observer sees only the exchange between two gateways, and the contents are encrypted.\n\nThere are several implementations. IPsec is a standard built into the network layer, popular for linking offices. OpenVPN builds a tunnel on top of TLS. WireGuard, the newest, is very compact: each node has a key pair, each peer has a known public key and address, and the tunnel runs over UDP and stays silent when there is no traffic. Setup everywhere consists of the same steps: define the peer and the key, give the tunnel interface an address, and add routes that direct the needed networks into the tunnel. If the key does not match, you get silence, with no complaint sent back.\n\nTwo practical questions come with tunnels. The first is packet size: packing adds headers, so the MTU inside the tunnel is smaller than outside, and you need to lower it, otherwise large packets vanish, as we saw in the chapter on diagnostics. The second is which traffic to send into the tunnel. A full tunnel sends everything into it, including ordinary internet traffic, which gives you control but loads the corporate gateway. A split tunnel sends only the corporate networks into it, and the rest goes directly. The tunnel's own packets to the peer must always go around the tunnel.\n\nWhat about security? VPN and SSH keys are the main secret: they must be stored carefully, restricted to specific nodes, and rotated regularly. Access inside the network is restricted the same way as from outside: someone connected by VPN should not see everything; they need firewall rules. The modern approach, zero trust, assumes that merely being on the network guarantees nothing: every request is checked against the identity and the state of the device. And logs: who connected, when, and from where, must be read, not just collected.\n\nLet's sum up. SSH encrypts the remote shell, verifies the server's key, logs in with keys, and can go through a bastion. A VPN packs packets of private networks into protected packets between public addresses; it needs a peer, a key, a tunnel address, and routes. The MTU inside the tunnel is smaller, and a split tunnel sends into it only what is needed. In the exercises you will hand out keys, go through a bastion, connect offices, and reconfigure a laptop's routes. Next come network attacks and defense.
