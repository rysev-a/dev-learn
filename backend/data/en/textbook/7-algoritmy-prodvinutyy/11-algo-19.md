Graphs and strategies. Chapter 19. Greedy algorithms and heuristics. We have reached the last chapter of the course, and the final technique is the most tempting one: a greedy algorithm is one where at every step we simply take the best option available right now, without looking back and without thinking about the future. No tables, no recursion, just one pass and a sort. When it works, it is the most elegant code in the world. When it doesn't, it is the most insidious kind of bug, because the solution looks correct, passes half of the tests, and then fails in production on rare cases. Today we learn to write greedy algorithms, to prove them right, and to notice when greed lies to us.\n\nLet us start with one of the few areas where greed doesn't just work but is provably optimal: intervals. Here is the scheduling problem. There is a set of events, each with a start and an end, there is only one hall, and we need to pick the maximum number of events without overlaps. The temptation is to be greedy by start time and take whatever starts earliest, but the counterexample is right there: a long event that starts before everything else blocks the whole day, while the right answer skips it and takes a bunch of short ones. The correct greedy rule is to sort by end time and take the event that finishes earliest, freeing the hall as soon as possible. This problem, known as activity selection, is a template for dozens of others: the maximum number of meetings per room, the minimum number of points that cover a set of segments, the most breaks you can fit into a schedule.\n\nLet us look at the favorite problem for testing greed: making change. We need to pay out an amount using the minimum number of coins. With the familiar set of one, two, five, and ten, the greedy rule of taking the largest coin that doesn't exceed the remainder works perfectly. But replace the set with one, three, and four, and ask for six: greedy hands out four plus one plus one, which is three coins, while the right answer is three plus three, which is two. A single new denomination broke our intuition. The moral is twofold: greed depends not only on the problem but also on the specific data, and arbitrary denominations turn making change into dynamic programming with a table over amounts.\n\nAnother honestly greedy structure is Huffman coding, the heart of lossless compression. The idea is that frequent symbols get short codes and rare symbols get long ones. The algorithm uses the priority queue from the chapter on heaps: we pull out the two rarest symbols, merge them into a node with the combined frequency, and put the node back into the queue. We repeat until a tree has grown, and the paths from the root to the leaves are the codes. Every step is locally the best one, merging the two rarest, and altogether we get an optimal prefix code, in which no code is the beginning of another. You will probably never write an archiver yourself, but everyone should see the connection between the heap, greed, and file compression.\n\nHere is one more honestly greedy problem: minimizing the total waiting time. There is one server and a queue of jobs with known durations. In what order should we run them so that the clients wait the least in total? Sort by increasing duration and run the short jobs first: a short job placed at the front reduces the waiting time for everyone behind it. This strategy provably minimizes the sum of waiting times, and its echoes are everywhere: express checkout lanes in stores, priority for short queries in databases. Notice the unfairness to long jobs: where starvation is unacceptable, protection against aging is added to the greedy rule, but that is a choice of compromise, not pure mathematics.\n\nWe have already met two provably greedy algorithms, and it is useful to look at them in hindsight. Dijkstra's algorithm is greedy: at each step, taking the vertex with the smallest estimate is optimal precisely because the weights are non-negative and the estimates cannot improve any further. Kruskal's algorithm is greedy: taking the cheapest edge that doesn't create a cycle is optimal because any other edge can be exchanged for it without making things worse. Notice the common pattern: greed is optimal when the problem has the greedy choice property, meaning there is a first step that is always part of some optimal solution, and optimal substructure, meaning that after the first step the rest of the problem is solved optimally by the same method.\n\nLet us look at greed through the eyes of the patterns from the chapters on pointers and windows. Here is the problem. Children have appetites, cookies have sizes, and each child is happy with one cookie that is at least as big as their appetite. Maximize the number of happy children. The solution is to sort both lists and walk through them with two pointers: we give the most demanding child the largest available cookie, and if there is no suitable one, that child goes away unhappy and the cookie waits for the next one. It takes a single pass after sorting. This is the typical shape of greedy solutions: sorting creates an order in which the greedy choice becomes obvious, and two pointers carry it out in linear time. Most greedy problems in interviews are exactly this combination.\n\nHow do you check a greedy solution yourself when you don't feel like writing a proof? There is an engineering discipline made of two tricks. The first is to look for a counterexample: come up with a small case where your greedy strategy gives a non-optimal answer. Ten minutes of brute force on paper save weeks of analyzing a production incident. The second is to have a fallback plan: if a counterexample turns up and the problem looks like a knapsack, you are almost certainly looking at a problem that needs the dynamic programming from the previous chapter. Greed and dynamic programming are two sides of the same coin: greed works where a local choice doesn't ruin the global optimum; dynamic programming works wherever the answer is built from subproblems, but it pays with a table. Start with greed, and switch to dynamic programming at the first counterexample.\n\nCompare two brother knapsacks. If items can be cut, that is the fractional version, and greed by value per kilogram is provably optimal: we take the most valuable stuff while it fits, and split the last item. If items cannot be cut, that is the very same knapsack from the previous chapter, and the same greed already lies; only dynamic programming works. One switch, the divisibility of items, decides the fate of the method, and this lesson scales: before choosing an algorithm, ask what freedoms the data gives you. The ability to split a resource, the independence of items, the monotonicity of the objective function: each property opens or closes the door to simple solutions.\n\nNow let us talk about cases where neither greed nor exact dynamic programming is affordable. Imagine a traveling salesman with a hundred cities: the exact solution is a factorial, honest dynamic programming needs memory exponential in the number of states, and you need the answer today. This is where heuristics and approximation algorithms step onto the stage: strategies that give good, though not guaranteed to be the best, answers in acceptable time. The simplest heuristic is a greedy start: always drive to the nearest unvisited city. On real maps the route comes out tens of percent worse than the ideal one, but it is built instantly. An improvement on top of it is local search: swap pairs of cities in the route until the improvements stop. This is how real logistics systems work.\n\nIt is important to distinguish heuristics without guarantees from approximation algorithms with a proven bound. The latter promise that our answer is never worse than the optimum by more than, say, a factor of two, and that is a mathematical guarantee, not a hope. The classic example is the minimum spanning tree walk for the traveling salesman: it gives at most twice the optimum, and we build the spanning tree in n log n time. When the requirements say that the answer must be predictably good on any data, look for an approximation algorithm with a proven bound; when they say it is usually good and we can live with rare failures, a heuristic with local search is enough.\n\nLet us also talk about heuristics in everyday engineering, where nobody calls them algorithms. A pruning rule in a database search, such as first using the index on the selective field, is a heuristic that sometimes lets you down. Ordering validations from cheap to expensive is greed that is almost always right. Warming up a cache with popular content, rounding the scheduler's time slice, picking the first free slot that comes along: all of these are greedy and heuristic decisions, and awareness changes everything here. An engineer who knows that their slot allocator is greedy understands why it degrades under fragmentation, and plans defragmentation in advance. Name your strategies out loud: that is halfway to controlling them.\n\nTo choose a strategy for a new problem, let us put everything together. Ask: is there an order in which the locally best choice is obvious and doesn't spoil the future? Then try greed and look for a counterexample. If there is a counterexample but the states can be described compactly, build dynamic programming. If even the table is unmanageable, use a heuristic or an approximation algorithm with a bound. And always check the edge cases: empty input, a single element, all elements equal, sorted in reverse. This is exactly where greedy algorithms break most often.\n\nAnd the final skill is coming up with a greedy criterion through an invariant. Ask: what property does my strategy preserve after every step? In activity selection the invariant sounds like this: the hall becomes free no later than with any other choice among the remaining ones. In Kruskal's algorithm, the forest built so far is always contained in some minimum spanning tree. In Huffman's algorithm, the current forest can still be grown into an optimal tree. If you can state such an invariant in words and it survives the first step, greed is almost certainly correct; if the invariant breaks at the second step, you have found a counterexample before it found you. This way of thinking is what separates a proven greedy algorithm from luck on the tests.\n\nPractice for today. Solve the problem of the maximum number of non-overlapping intervals with greed by end time, and find its counterexample for greed by start time, with your own hands on paper. Write Huffman coding on a small text: count the frequencies, build the tree with a heap, write out the codes, and compare the length of the encoded text with a fixed length per symbol. Beat the greedy salesman route with local search using pair swaps, and you will see how the improvement heuristic betters the greedy start. And work through the coin problem: in one system of denominations greed is optimal, in another it isn't, so find both. And check the activity selection invariant in words: one sentence about why an early end never hurts is worth ten tests.\n\nLet us sum up the course. We have walked a path from an honest comparison of algorithms, through structures, namely arrays, lists, stacks and queues, hash tables, trees, and graphs, to strategies: sorting and searching, pointer and window patterns, divide and conquer, dynamic programming, and greed. The main things to take away are these. Complexity is the shape of growth and a conversation about the future of your data. A structure is an answer to the question of in what order you are obliged to hand out the data. A strategy is an honest trade between time, memory, and guarantees. The problem itself suggests the tool if you listen to how it is phrased. Solve problems by hand, measure on real volumes, look for counterexamples to your own solutions, and algorithms will stop being an exam and become your everyday language. Thank you for listening to this course to the very end, and good luck in practice.
