Graphs and Strategies. Chapter 18. Dynamic Programming. This is the trickiest technique in the whole course, and also the most reusable. Behind the scary name hides a simple idea: don't solve the same subproblem twice. Today we'll look at when this idea turns an exponential brute-force search into a single linear pass, how to formulate subproblems, how memoization differs from tabulation, and we'll solve the classics, from Fibonacci numbers to the knapsack and the edit distance. After this lesson you'll start to see dynamic programming in problems that used to look like brute-force monsters.\n\nLet's begin with an honest demonstration of a disaster. Fibonacci numbers: each number is the sum of the two before it, and the sequence starts with one and one. Naive recursion takes a single line: the function calls itself for the two previous numbers. It's beautiful, short, and monstrously slow: for the fortieth number the function makes billions of calls. Why? Because the subproblems overlap. To compute the fortieth number we compute the thirty-eighth twice and the thirty-seventh three times, and every number gets recomputed again and again. The call tree grows exponentially, even though there are only forty distinct subproblems. Overlapping subproblems, plus the ability to assemble an answer from the answers to smaller ones, are the hallmarks of problems that dynamic programming cures.\n\nThe first cure is memoization: wrap the same recursion with a dictionary. Before computing anything, we look in the dictionary. If the answer is already there, we return it instantly; if not, we compute it and store it. Three extra lines turn an exponential into a linear: now every subproblem is solved exactly once, and repeated requests hit the cache. This style is called top-down: we start from the big problem, and the recursion itself reaches the base cases. It's natural, it makes you think only about the transition, and it's lazy: it computes only the subproblems that are really needed.\n\nThe second cure is tabulation, also known as bottom-up. Instead of recursion, we fill a table from the base cases up to the answer we need. For Fibonacci, that's an array whose first two elements are ones, and each next element is the sum of the two before it. The result is just as linear, but there's no recursion and none of its stack overhead, the traversal order is predictable, and often you can save memory. Choosing between the styles is a matter of taste and circumstance: top-down is quicker to write and harder to get the order wrong, while bottom-up runs faster and uses less memory. Both styles solve the same problems, and it's worth mastering both.\n\nThe simplest one-dimensional classic for a warm-up is the staircase. You stand at the bottom, and each step takes you up one or two stairs. How many ways are there to reach the top of a staircase with n steps? The last move was made either from the previous step or from two steps below, so the answer for the top is the sum of the answers for the two steps below it. Recognize it? These are Fibonacci numbers in disguise, and all the machinery of memoization and tabulation works unchanged. Remember this feeling: dozens of different problems turn out to be the same table once the wording is changed, and recognizing the disguises is half of the craft.\n\nLet's take our first substantial problem: the robot on a grid. The robot stands in the top-left corner of a table, it can move only right or down, and we need to count the distinct paths to the bottom-right corner. Brute force over all paths is exponential, but look: the number of paths to any cell equals the number of paths to the cell above plus the number of paths to the cell on the left, because those are the only places we could have come from. That is the state with its transition. We fill the table row by row, each row relying on the previous one and on the already filled cells of the current one: quadratic time, and linear memory if we keep a single row. If you add walls or traps, forbidden cells, to the grid, the solution doesn't change: in a forbidden cell the number of paths is simply zero.\n\nDynamic programming has already shown up in our course, and it's useful to recognize it in hindsight. Bellman-Ford from the chapter on graphs is dynamic programming in its pure form: the state is a vertex and the number of edges in the path, the value is the shortest distance, and the transition is a minimum over the last edge. Breadth-first search is the simplest dynamic programming over layers, where a queue serves the layers. And the whole idea of memoization was already waiting for us in the discussion of hash tables: a dictionary whose key is the arguments. The more of these recognitions you have, the fewer algorithms you need to keep in your head: what remains is a handful of principles and an endless supply of disguises.\n\nThe maximum sum of consecutive elements is a pearl of one-dimensional dynamic programming. Given an array of positive and negative numbers, find a contiguous piece with the maximum sum. Checking all pieces is quadratic. The dynamic programming idea: keep the best piece that ends exactly at the current position. It either extends the best piece ending at the previous position, or starts over from the current element, and we take whichever is larger. The answer is the maximum over all positions. One pass, constant memory, and a solution that looks like a magic trick until you have the idea of a state. This problem, known as Kadane's algorithm, comes up in time series analysis more often than any other.\n\nNow the classic that half of all job interviews rest on: the knapsack problem. There is a knapsack of a given capacity and items with weights and values, and you must pack the maximum value without tearing the bag. Greed, which means taking the most valuable item per kilogram, doesn't always work: one heavy ingot may be worth more than a handful of rings, yet not fit. Honest dynamic programming: the state is how many items we've considered and how much capacity is used, and the value is the best total worth. The transition is simple: for each item, we either take it, adding its value and weight, or skip it. The table has items times capacity cells, and each answer picks the better of two options. This is the first example where the naive greedy answer is wrong, so remember it as a vaccine against careless greed, which we'll return to in the next chapter.\n\nTwo-dimensional dynamic programming on strings gives us two more classic tools. The longest common subsequence of two strings: the state is a pair of prefix lengths, n and m, and the transition is this. If the last characters are equal, take the diagonal plus one; otherwise take the maximum of the left and the top. The answer is the length of the longest chain of characters in the same order. This is how file versions are compared and similar records are found. The Levenshtein edit distance is the minimum number of insertions, deletions, and substitutions that turn one string into another; the table is the same, and the transition accounts for the cost of a substitution. The difference between a document diff and finding a similar name in a database is a couple of lines in the transition of the same table.\n\nAnother important family is dynamic programming over segments of a string. The longest palindromic substring is solved with a table: the state is a pair, start and end, and the value says whether that piece is a palindrome. The transition looks at the outer characters and at the inner piece, and the table is filled from short segments to long ones. Notice the traversal order: from the diagonal outward, from small pieces to large ones. This is a standard trick for segment problems, and it differs from filling grids row by row. The same method solves splitting a string into dictionary words and placing parentheses in an expression to get the maximum value.\n\nHow do you recognize dynamic programming in the wild? By the wording. How many ways: counting dynamic programming. Minimum or maximum: optimization dynamic programming. Is it possible: reachability dynamic programming. The key questions to ask yourself: can the state be described briefly, does the big problem reduce to smaller ones, and does the order of the small solutions within a state not matter? The hardest creative step is choosing the state: what exactly are we computing for each prefix, each cell, each pair? A good state is small and contains exactly the information that is enough for the transition. Extra information bloats the table, too little information breaks the transitions, and this balance is a real skill that comes with a hundred solved problems.\n\nNow a counterexample: a state that looks reasonable but doesn't work. A robot on a grid collects coins, moving right and down. The wrong state is the position plus the number of coins already collected. The second quantity is not known at the moment we enter a cell, so you can't build a table over it. The right state is just the position, and the value is the maximum number of coins on a path to that cell. The accumulation lives in the value, not in the state. The rule: include in the state only what affects the future and is known at the moment of entry. The question, what must the robot remember as it leaves the cell, is the best test for any state.\n\nMemory optimization is the second skill after choosing the state. If the transition looks only at the previous row, store two rows instead of the whole table, and a square becomes linear memory. If the transition looks to the left in the current row, a single row is enough with careful updating. If the state is the knapsack capacity, walk through the capacities in the right direction so that you don't use an item twice. The direction of traversal is itself part of the solution, and a mistake in direction is the most popular mistake in the knapsack.\n\nAn honest warning about the price. Dynamic programming isn't free: its table is memory, and the time is the number of states multiplied by the cost of a transition. Before building a table, estimate its size. A state with three parameters of a thousand values each is a billion cells, and no production system will digest that. Sometimes it is precisely the realization of the table's size that sends you toward greedy methods, approximations, or a reformulation. And the other way around: if brute force is chewing through an exponential while the state fits into a million cells, you are almost certainly looking at dynamic programming, you just haven't noticed yet.\n\nToday's practice. Write Fibonacci three ways: naive recursion, memoization, and tabulation, and time them on the fortieth number; the difference will convince you. Solve the robot on a grid, then add a wall and check that the solution holds up. Implement Kadane's algorithm on an array with negative numbers. And finish off the knapsack: a table of items by capacity, then recover the chosen set with a backward pass. Recovering the answer from the table is a valuable skill of its own. And finally, the longest palindrome with a table over segments: short pieces first, then long ones, and you'll see how the traversal order becomes part of the solution.\n\nLet's sum up. Dynamic programming kicks in when subproblems overlap and the answer is built from the answers to smaller ones. Memoization works top-down and is lazy in a smart way; tabulation goes bottom-up and saves memory and stack. State and transition are the whole essence of the method: a table cell is a short answer to a subproblem, and the transition is an honest relation between neighboring cells. The knapsack, grids, and two strings are three families of problems that cover most of the practice. And remember the price: the size of the table is the memory you pay for speed. The last chapter of the course is about greedy algorithms: when you don't even need to build a table, because a locally best step provably leads to the global optimum.
