#CHAPTER 13 | Red-black trees
Trees and heaps. Chapter 13. Red-black trees. A search tree promises logarithmic time, but only if it doesn't degenerate: on sorted data it turns into a chain. We need a structure that guarantees logarithmic height for any order of insertions and deletions. Such structures are called self-balancing trees. They sit under the ordered maps and sets of almost every language: TreeMap in Java, map and set in C plus plus, BTreeMap in Rust, the process scheduler in the Linux kernel. The best known are AVL trees and red-black trees, and this chapter is about them. We'll go through rotations, the coloring rules, the three cases of insertion and an overview of deletion, and in the exercises we'll build our own trees. Nine exercises are waiting for you at the end.\n\nLet's start with the tool, rotations, which you may already know. A left rotation lifts the right child up, and the old root becomes its left child; the subtree that sat between them moves over to the node that went down. A right rotation is the mirror image. A rotation is just five or six pointer assignments, it never breaks the order of the keys, and it changes the heights of subtrees. The whole art of self-balancing trees is deciding when to rotate and which way, so that the height stays logarithmic. Here two schools part ways: track the heights of subtrees directly, as AVL trees do, or encode the balance in the colors of the nodes, as red-black trees do.\n\nThe AVL tree was the first self-balancing tree, invented in nineteen sixty-two. The rule: for every node, the heights of the left and right subtrees differ by at most one. This difference is called the balance. After an insertion or a deletion we walk up from the changed spot and recompute heights; if some node ends up with balance plus or minus two, we fix it with rotations. There are four cases. Left-left: the left subtree of the left child is too heavy, and one right rotation fixes it. Right-right is the mirror image. Left-right: first a left rotation of the child, then a right rotation of the node; right-left is the mirror image. The height of an AVL tree is at most about one and a half times the binary logarithm of the number of nodes.\n\nRed-black trees work differently. Every node is painted red or black, and four rules must hold. First: the root is black. Second: a red node has two black children, so there are no two reds in a row. Third: empty links count as black leaves. Fourth: on every path from a node down to an empty link there is the same number of black nodes; this number is called the black height. The color is a single bit per node, and there is no need to store heights. The rules look odd, but they are designed so that insertion and deletion can be repaired with a few recolorings and no more than two or three rotations.\n\nWhy do these rules give logarithmic height? Take the shortest path from the root to an empty link: it consists of black nodes only, say b of them. Any other path also contains exactly b black nodes, but red ones may sit between them, only one at a time, because two reds in a row are forbidden. So the longest path is at most twice as long as the shortest. On the other hand, a tree with black height b has at least two to the power b, minus one, nodes. From this, the height is at most twice the binary logarithm of the number of nodes plus one. The balance is worse than in an AVL tree, but it is cheaper to maintain: there are fewer restructurings.\n\nNow insertion into a red-black tree. We insert the new node as in an ordinary search tree and paint it red: this doesn't change any black heights and breaks at most one rule. If the parent is black, everything is fine and we're done. If the parent is red, the rule about no two reds in a row is broken, and the grandparent of the new node is necessarily black. What happens next is decided by the uncle, that is, the sibling of the parent. There are three cases: the uncle is red; the uncle is black and the new node is in a kink relative to the grandparent; the uncle is black and the new node is on one line with its parent and grandparent. Let's go through each.\n\nThe first case: the uncle is red. We repaint the parent and the uncle black, and the grandparent red. The black heights haven't changed: the paths under the grandparent still have the same number of blacks. But the problem may have moved up two levels: the grandparent became red, and its own parent may be red too. Then we repeat everything with the grandparent as the new node. If we reach the root, we paint it black, and the black height of the whole tree grows by one. This is the only case that moves up the tree, and it has no rotations at all, only recoloring.\n\nThe second and third cases: the uncle is black. If the new node is inside a kink, that is, it is the right child of a left parent or the left child of a right parent, we rotate the parent, and the kink turns into a line. Now the node and the parent are on one line with the grandparent. Then we rotate the grandparent in the opposite direction and swap the colors: the former parent, which is now the root of the subtree, becomes black, and the grandparent becomes red. The black height of the subtree is the same as before, there are no two reds in a row, and the insertion ends here. So an insertion makes at most two rotations. This is why red-black trees are popular wherever there are many insertions.\n\nDeletion is harder, so we'll just give an overview. A node with two children is replaced by its successor, as in an ordinary tree, which reduces the problem to deleting a node with at most one child. If the deleted node is red, all is well. If it's black and its replacement is black too, one black disappears from the path, and a so-called double black appears, which has to be resolved. We act according to the color of the sibling. A red sibling: a rotation turns this into the black sibling case. A black sibling with black children: we recolor the sibling and push the problem up. A black sibling with a red nephew: one or two rotations and a recoloring settle everything. In total, no more than three rotations. In practice, deletion is often written from a ready-made table of cases and checked with invariant tests.\n\nRed-black trees have many relatives. Sedgewick's left-leaning red-black trees require red links to go only to the left; the code is shorter, though subtle. AA trees simplify the cases even further. Treaps, or Cartesian trees, keep balance through random priorities in the nodes and behave like heaps. Skip lists get by without trees: levels of linked lists with a randomly chosen height. Trees with a large branching factor, B-trees, are used by databases and file systems. The choice depends on the task, but the principle is the same: a small local restructuring in constant time every time the balance is broken.\n\nLet's compare AVL and red-black trees. AVL keeps the tree stricter, with height around one and a half logarithms, so search is faster. A red-black tree allows height up to two logarithms, but insertion restructures fewer nodes: at most two rotations, and deletion at most three, whereas in AVL a deletion can trigger a chain of rotations all the way to the root. Hence the practical rule: for read-heavy workloads, AVL more often; for frequent changes, red-black. Libraries more often use red-black trees precisely because of the cheap insertions. In any case, all operations cost order log n in the worst case, which a search tree without balancing doesn't guarantee.\n\nHow do you debug self-balancing trees? The only reliable way is to check the invariants. Write a checking function that walks the tree and makes sure of the following: the key order is correct, the root is black, there are no two reds in a row, every path has the same black height, and, for AVL, the balance of every node is within bounds. Then insert and delete random and sorted sequences, and call the check after every operation. A mistake in a rotation or recoloring case is found in seconds, whereas without the check it shows up many operations later. This is a practice that works for any structure with invariants.\n\nLet's sum up. Self-balancing trees keep the height logarithmic for any order of operations, thanks to rotations. The AVL tree watches the difference of subtree heights, the red-black tree watches the colors and the black height. A red-black tree has four rules, and from them follows a height of at most two logarithms. Insertion is a red node and three cases by the uncle: recoloring, rotating a kink, rotating a line; no more than two rotations. Deletion repairs the double black according to the sibling's color. AVL searches faster, red-black changes faster. In the nine exercises you will write rotations, a rule checker, an AVL tree and a red-black tree with insertion.
