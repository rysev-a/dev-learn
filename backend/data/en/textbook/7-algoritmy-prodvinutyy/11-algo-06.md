#CHAPTER 6 | Two Pointers
Patterns and structures. Chapter 6. Two pointers. This is one of the most common techniques in interviews and in real work. The idea is simple: instead of one index running over an array, we keep two, and every move of either one guarantees something. A quadratic search over pairs turns into a single pass. This chapter covers three forms of the technique: pointers moving toward each other from both ends; pointers moving in the same direction with different roles, a reader and a writer, or a slow one and a fast one; and pointers walking over two different sequences, as in a merge. We will see why all of this is correct, where the technique breaks, and solve twenty problems, half of which are classics.\n\nLet's start with the most famous form, moving toward each other. You are given a sorted array and a number, and you need to find a pair of elements with a given sum. Trying all pairs costs a square. Now use two pointers: the left one on the smallest element, the right one on the largest. Compute the sum. If it is too big, move the right pointer left, which is the only way to make the sum smaller. If it is too small, move the left pointer right. If it matches, you are done. The pointers get closer, each step moves one of them, and there are at most as many steps as the length of the array: linear time and constant memory. The sorting is no accident here: it is the ordering that lets us reason about what happens after a move.\n\nWhy is the move correct? Suppose the sum of the left and right elements is too big. Then the right element, paired with any element to the right of the left pointer, gives a sum no smaller than the current one, which is even further above the target. So the right element cannot take part in any answer, and we can discard it forever. We discard one candidate per step without looking at all the pairs, and we lose nothing. This argument, discarding a candidate that is certainly no better, is the main tool of the technique, and you should use it as a check: if you cannot explain why the discarded element is not needed, the pointers are moving the wrong way.\n\nThe second example on the same principle is the container with water. You are given a set of vertical walls of different heights, and you pick two so that the most water fits between them. The area is the distance between the walls times the smaller of the two heights. Put the pointers on the outermost walls: the distance is the largest possible. Which wall should we move? Only the shorter one. If we move the taller one, the distance shrinks while the smaller height stays the same or drops, so the area cannot grow. The shorter wall paired with any other wall inside the span gives no improvement either, so it can be discarded. One pass, linear time. The same technique solves the problem of rain water trapped between walls.\n\nPalindromes are natural territory for pointers moving toward each other. A string reads the same in both directions if the first character equals the last, the second equals the second to last, and so on. Put the pointers at the ends, compare, and bring them closer. There are variations. To skip punctuation and ignore case, move a pointer past the unwanted characters before comparing. To allow deleting one character, at the first mismatch try skipping the character on the left or on the right and check the rest. The same technique reverses an array in place: swap the elements under the pointers and bring them closer. Remember that the stopping condition is that the left pointer is strictly less than the right one.\n\nThe second form moves in one direction, with different roles: a reader and a writer. The reader pointer looks at every element, and the writer pointer marks where to put the next element we need. This is how you remove duplicates from a sorted array in place: the reader goes over everything, and when it meets a value different from the last one written, the writer writes it and moves on. The same works for removing a given value and for moving zeros to the end. The advantage of two pointers over inserting and deleting in the middle is linear time instead of quadratic and no extra memory. It is compressing the array on top of itself: we read ahead and write behind, and the writer never overtakes the reader.\n\nSlow and fast pointers are familiar to us from linked lists, but they work on arrays too. Here is a classic problem. An array holds n plus one numbers with values from one to n, so one number is repeated. Find it without changing the array and without extra memory. The trick is to look at the array as a linked list, where from position i an arrow leads to the position equal to the value stored at i. A duplicate means that two arrows lead into the same position, so the graph has a cycle, and the entry point of the cycle is exactly the repeated number. We find the cycle with the tortoise and hare algorithm, and then its entry point on a second run, exactly as in the chapter on lists.\n\nThe third form walks over two sequences. Given two sorted arrays, we need to find their intersection. Put a pointer at the start of each and compare the elements. If they are equal, add the value to the answer and move both. If the left one is smaller, move the left one, otherwise move the right one. Every step moves at least one pointer, so the total number of steps is at most the sum of the lengths. This is the same merge we saw in merge sort and in merging lists. Variations include union, difference, the intersection of lists of intervals, and checking whether one string is a subsequence of another: one pointer walks over the short string and moves only when it has found the next character.\n\nLet's make it harder: triples. Find all distinct triples in an array that add up to zero. Brute force is cubic. But if we sort the array, it is enough to fix the first element and look for a pair with the needed sum among the rest using pointers moving toward each other: a square instead of a cube. Be careful with duplicates. To keep triples from repeating, after finding a triple and when moving on to the next first element we skip equal values. The same technique solves the problem of the triple sum closest to a target: move the pointers according to the sign of the difference. The price of the technique is the sort, which costs n log n, but next to a square it is barely noticeable, and it is an example of a general pattern: sorting before using pointers is a common step.\n\nAnother classic is partitioning into three groups, the Dutch national flag problem. The array holds zeros, ones, and twos, and we need to order them in a single pass, in place. There are three pointers: everything before the first holds zeros, everything after the last holds twos, and the middle one examines the unknown part. If the middle pointer sees a zero, swap it with the left boundary and move both. If it sees a two, swap it with the right boundary and move only the right one, because the element that arrived has not been examined yet. If it sees a one, just move on. The same technique underlies partitioning in quicksort, and it helps separate even from odd, negative from positive, any two classes in one pass.\n\nPointers are also useful for counting pairs. How many pairs of elements have a sum no greater than a given number? After sorting, put the pointers at the ends. If the sum of the outer elements is within the limit, then the left element pairs up with every element between the pointers, so we add the whole length of the gap at once and move the left pointer. If the sum is greater, the right element is too big, so we move it. Quadratic pair counting becomes linear after sorting. And pairs with a given difference are found by pointers moving in the same direction: if the difference is too small, move the right one forward, if it is too large, move the left one. The pattern is always the same: moving one pointer changes the quantity we are watching in a monotonic way.\n\nNow, where the technique does not work. The first condition is monotonicity: moving a pointer must change the quantity under consideration in a known direction. For a sum, sorting provides this. On an unsorted array the reasoning, too much so move the right one, proves nothing. The second: sorting destroys the original positions of the elements. If you need the actual indices of the pair, sort pairs of value and index, or switch to a hash table. The third: a window with a sum when there are negative numbers, where expanding the window does not guarantee that the sum grows. There the pointers lose monotonicity, and you need prefix sums with a table. Always ask yourself: what does each move guarantee?\n\nMistakes with pointers are almost always the same. The first is the boundary: strictly less, or less than or equal. For pointers moving toward each other, the usual condition is that the left one is strictly less than the right one. If both stand on the same element, a pair made of that element with itself is usually not allowed. The second is a pointer that does not move in any branch: the loop will hang. Check that on every step at least one pointer advances. The third is duplicates: skip them right after processing, but with a bounds check. The best way to keep it all in your head is an invariant: a condition that is true at the start of every step. For example: everything to the left of the left pointer and everything to the right of the right pointer has already been discarded, and the answer, if there is one, lies between them.\n\nLet's sum up. Two pointers turn a search over pairs into a single pass when moving a pointer changes the needed quantity monotonically and the discarded candidate is certainly not needed. There are three forms: toward each other, for a pair with a given sum, the container, and the palindrome; reader and writer, for compressing in place; and over two sequences, for merging, intersection, and subsequences. Add triples by fixing one element, partitioning into three groups, and counting pairs. If the conditions do not hold, first sort, or switch to a hash table. Twenty exercises are waiting for you, and half of them are classic problems asked in interviews.
