Graphs and Strategies. Chapter 16. Shortest Paths and Spanning Trees. In the last chapter all the edges were equal, and breadth-first search honestly found the fewest steps. But the real world is weighted: roads have kilometers, links have delays, deals have fees, tasks have durations. Today we solve the central problem of routing: find a path between two vertices with the smallest sum of weights. We'll cover Dijkstra's algorithm, the workhorse of navigators and routers; its sibling Bellman-Ford for graphs with negative weights; A-star, the heuristic search from the game industry; and spanning trees, which answer how to connect everything at minimum cost. These are the algorithms you will recognize in every navigator and every delivery system.\n\nDijkstra starts from a simple greedy idea. For every vertex we keep a current estimate of the distance from the start: at first it's infinity for everyone and zero for the start. At each step we take the unprocessed vertex with the smallest estimate and declare its distance final: nothing can be closer, because any detour would go through a vertex with an even larger estimate. Then comes relaxation: we walk along every edge out of that vertex and try to improve the neighbor's estimate. If going through us is shorter, we update the estimate and remember where we came from. We repeat until everything is processed. To restore the path, we follow the came-from marks backward from the goal to the start.\n\nLet's run Dijkstra by hand on a tiny example, because the mechanics matter more than the formula. Say a direct street from home to work takes forty minutes, the route through the bank takes twenty plus fifteen, and the detour through the cafe and the bank costs ten plus five plus fifteen. At the start, home has zero and everything else has infinity. We pop home and relax its neighbors: cafe ten, bank twenty, work forty. We pop the cafe with ten, since it's cheaper than the rest; through it the bank improves to fifteen. We pop the bank with fifteen, and work improves to thirty. We pop work: thirty minutes, along home, cafe, bank, work. Notice how the direct road, which looked best on the first estimate, turned out to be the worst, and it was the gradual improvement of estimates that fixed the choice.\n\nAn efficient implementation of Dijkstra keeps the vertices with their estimates in a heap, the priority queue from the chapter on heaps. We pop the minimum in logarithmic time, relax the neighbors, and update the estimates in the heap. The total complexity is vertices plus edges, times a logarithm. On a graph with a million vertices that's a few million operations instead of hundreds of billions for brute-force enumeration of all paths. That's exactly why Dijkstra lives inside navigators, packet routing systems, and delivery planners. Remember the formula: vertices plus edges, times the logarithm of the vertices. It comes up in interviews all the time.\n\nDijkstra has one fundamental restriction: weights must be non-negative. The greed at its heart relies on monotonicity: passing through a vertex with a larger current estimate can't improve the path. With negative weights that breaks. A long path with a large negative part can turn out shorter, but Dijkstra has already closed its vertices and never revisits its decisions. Allow negative edges, and you need a different tool. The practical takeaway: before applying Dijkstra, ask whether your system has negative contributions, such as discounts, refunds, or negative delays.\n\nBellman-Ford is a calm hard worker with no greed at all. It simply goes over all the edges n times, and on each pass tries to improve the estimates through every edge. After n passes the estimates are guaranteed to converge to the true shortest paths. The cost is n times the number of edges, which is slower than Dijkstra, but negative edges don't scare it. And here's the bonus that makes the algorithm indispensable: if the estimates keep improving even after n passes, the graph has a negative cycle reachable from the start. The sum around the loop is negative, and a shortest path simply doesn't exist. The classic application is finding arbitrage chains in currency exchange rates: logarithms of the rates become the weights, and a negative cycle means guaranteed profit going around the loop.\n\nA-star is Dijkstra with foresight. While Dijkstra spreads out in every direction equally, A-star adds a heuristic to each vertex's estimate: a lower bound on the distance to the goal, for example the straight-line distance on a map. Vertices are processed in order of the distance already traveled plus the heuristic, so the search is pulled toward the goal instead of exploring everything. If the heuristic never overestimates, the path found is guaranteed to be the shortest. This is how game bots find routes for units, and how routing services save computation on huge maps. The idea matters more than the formula: steer greed with a good estimate, and it gets many times faster without breaking.\n\nThere's a third story in shortest paths, all pairs at once: you need the distance between every pair of vertices. The Floyd-Warshall algorithm solves it with cubic dynamic programming in three lines: try to improve the path between every pair by going through each intermediate vertex. It's slow on large graphs, but extremely simple, and indispensable on small dense graphs, for instance all pairs of metro stations or all pairs of rooms in a game. For an engineer's toolbox it's enough to remember three things: this problem exists, it has a simple cubic solution, and at large scale nobody computes all pairs. There, repeated runs of Dijkstra do the work.\n\nFinally, a word about the variety of weights. A weight can live not only on an edge but on a vertex: the fee for entering a city turns into the weight of all edges leading into it. Weights can depend on time: a road takes twenty minutes, but forty at rush hour, and the right algorithm accounts for the start time, working with a graph that changes on the fly. Weights can be link reliability probabilities, and then multiplying probabilities turns into adding logarithms, a trick you already met when we talked about currency arbitrage. Every time you reduce a new problem to a familiar algorithm by choosing the weights carefully, you've done half of the solution.\n\nAn important variation is many sources at once. A delivery service looks for a path from any of twenty pizzerias to an address; a dispatcher computes the arrival time of the nearest car. The solution is elegant: put all the sources into the heap with a zero estimate and run the same algorithm. The wave goes out from all sources simultaneously, and every vertex learns its nearest one. The same trick with breadth-first search from many sources answers the question of the nearest object for every point on the map in a single pass. A one-line modification makes the algorithm flexible, so always ask yourself: is my single source really a set?\n\nThe second half of the chapter asks a different question: not the shortest path between two vertices, but the cheapest way to connect everyone. A telecom company needs to lay cables between cities, paying for every kilometer, but the network must be connected. A minimum spanning tree is a subset of edges that connects all vertices, has no cycles, and has the smallest sum of weights. Notice the beauty of the setup: cycles in a network are a waste of cable, so the answer is always a tree. There are many such trees, the minimum one is unique, and it's found by a greedy method that, surprisingly, is provably optimal.\n\nKruskal's algorithm: sort all edges by weight and go from the cheapest, adding an edge if it connects two different components and skipping it if both vertices are already connected. Checking whether two vertices are in the same component, and merging components, is the job of a structure called disjoint sets. Each set has a representative, find walks up to the root with path compression, and union hangs the smaller tree under the larger one. With these two tricks every operation costs almost a constant, amortized, which is one more meeting with amortization from the very first chapter. Kruskal is pure greed, and in the last chapter of the course we'll return to it as a proven greedy algorithm.\n\nPrim's algorithm solves the same problem from the other side: we grow a tree from a single vertex, and at each step add the cheapest edge leaving the tree that's already built. An implementation with a heap makes it almost a twin of Dijkstra: the same structure, a different comparison key. For Dijkstra it's the sum from the start, for Prim it's just the edge weight. It's useful to see the pair side by side: one idea, greedily reaching for the best local step, serves both shortest paths and the minimum network, and a difference in a single line changes the meaning of the whole algorithm.\n\nWhere do spanning trees live outside textbooks? Clustering: build a minimum spanning tree over data objects and cut the longest edges, and you get natural clusters. Designing networks and power grids: minimum cost of connecting nodes with redundancy, where spare links are added on top of the tree. Image analysis: pixels as vertices, similar colors as cheap edges. And approximate solutions of the traveling salesman problem: the tree comes in linearithmic time, and a tour at most twice as long as the optimal one is built on it. It's a classic example of how a fast skeleton makes an approximate answer possible where an exact one is out of reach in reasonable time.\n\nPractice for today. Implement Dijkstra with a heap on a graph given as a list of edges, and find a path from the entrance to the exit of a hand-drawn maze where obstacle cells have a large weight. Run Bellman-Ford on a graph with one negative edge and compare its answers with Dijkstra's, and you'll see where the greedy method goes wrong. Write a disjoint-set structure with path compression: forty lines that will serve you many more times. And finish with Kruskal: the minimum network between cities of your region, with made-up cable prices. Then go back to the maze from the previous chapter's practice and add weights to the cells: grass, swamp, highway. You'll see that now only Dijkstra finds the honest shortest path, while breadth-first search, forgetting about weights, heroically hauls you through the swamp.\n\nLet's sum up. A weighted graph changes the question: not the fewest steps, but the smallest sum. Dijkstra is greed with a heap, n log n, non-negative weights only. Bellman-Ford is patient passes over all the edges; it allows negatives and catches negative cycles. A-star steers the search with a heuristic that never overestimates the goal. For all pairs, there's cubic Floyd on small graphs. The minimum spanning tree connects everyone more cheaply than anything else: Kruskal through edge sorting and disjoint sets, Prim through a heap, just like Dijkstra. In the next chapter we'll return to the disjoint sets you've just written inside Kruskal, and study backtracking, an honest traversal of all the options. And then we'll generalize the idea that flashed twice today: greed works when you can prove that a locally best step doesn't spoil the global optimum. And if you can't, you'll have to remember the results of subproblems. That is dynamic programming, the trickiest and most beautiful chapter of the course.
