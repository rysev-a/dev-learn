#CHAPTER 9 | Number algorithms and bits
Strings and numbers. Chapter 9. Number algorithms and bits. Numbers in programming live by their own laws: integers are not infinite, division rounds, and remainders behave like the hands of a clock. This chapter is about techniques that show up everywhere: in encryption, hash tables, random number generators, and in problems where the answer is requested modulo some number. We will cover Euclid's algorithm and its extended version, remainder arithmetic, fast exponentiation, the sieve of Eratosthenes and factorization into primes, binomial coefficients modulo a prime, and then bitwise operations and iterating over subsets with masks. At the end you will find twenty exercises.\n\nLet us start with the greatest common divisor. Trying numbers downward from the smaller one takes too long, and Euclid noticed that the common divisors of the numbers a and b are the same as those of b and the remainder of a divided by b. So the pair can be replaced with a smaller pair until the remainder becomes zero, and the last nonzero number is the answer. For forty-eight and eighteen the chain goes like this: forty-eight and eighteen, eighteen and twelve, twelve and six, six and zero. The answer is six. Every two steps the number shrinks at least by half, so the number of steps is on the order of a logarithm. The least common multiple is then the product divided by the greatest common divisor.\n\nThe extended Euclidean algorithm gives more: it finds integers x and y such that a times x plus b times y equals the greatest common divisor. These are the Bézout coefficients. They are obtained by walking backwards through the same equalities: we express the last nonzero remainder through the previous two, then substitute the expression for the previous remainder, and so on to the start. For thirty and eighteen we get that six equals two times eighteen minus thirty. The main application is the inverse element modulo a number: if a and the modulus are coprime, the coefficient at a is the number you can multiply by instead of dividing.\n\nRemainder arithmetic works like the hands of a clock. Addition, subtraction and multiplication can be done without waiting for the end: the remainder of a sum equals the remainder of the sum of remainders, and the same goes for a product. That is why huge numbers are kept within the bounds of a modulus, for example ten to the ninth power plus seven. Division works differently: to divide by a number means to multiply by its inverse, and the inverse exists only if the number and the modulus are coprime. For a prime modulus, the inverse of a equals a to the power of the modulus minus two; this follows from Fermat's little theorem. Remember the sign as well: the remainder of a negative number is computed differently in different languages, and in Python it is always nonnegative.\n\nTo raise a number to the power n, you do not need n multiplications. Let us write the exponent in binary: thirteen is one, one, zero, one. We square the base again and again and get the powers one, two, four and eight. We multiply those of them whose places in the exponent hold a one: three to the eighth, three to the fourth and three to the first give three to the thirteenth. In total there are about a logarithm's worth of multiplications, and when working modulo a number we take the remainder after each multiplication, so the numbers do not grow. This technique is called fast exponentiation, and it works for any associative operation.\n\nFast exponentiation has a striking application. Fibonacci numbers come from multiplying a two-by-two matrix with three ones and one zero: its n-th power contains the Fibonacci number with index n in a corner. We square the matrix, then again and again: after three squarings we get the eighth power and the value twenty-one. The result is a Fibonacci number in logarithmic instead of linear time. The same approach solves any linear recurrence relations: you write the step as a multiplication by a matrix, and then fast exponentiation does all the work. The size of the matrix equals the order of the recurrence, so the cost is moderate.\n\nNow prime numbers. A number is prime if it has exactly two divisors: one and itself. Checking divisibility by all numbers up to n takes too long. It is enough to go up to the square root of n: if a number has a divisor larger than the root, then its paired divisor is smaller than the root. For example, for ninety-one we look for divisors up to nine and find seven, and thirteen is its paired divisor. We check two, then only odd numbers, and that gives about the square root of n operations. For numbers up to ten to the twelfth this is enough, and for real cryptographic numbers people use probabilistic tests such as the Miller–Rabin test.\n\nIf we need all prime numbers up to n, trial division is wasteful. The sieve of Eratosthenes works the other way around: we write the numbers in a row, take the first unmarked one, which is prime, and cross out all its multiples. Then we take the next unmarked one and repeat. It is enough to start crossing out multiples from the square of the prime: the smaller multiples have already been crossed out by smaller primes. We stop when the square of the prime exceeds the bound. The time is n times the logarithm of the logarithm of n, almost linear, and the memory is one byte per number. Up to ten million such a sieve runs in Python in seconds, if you cross out multiples with slices rather than one by one.\n\nThe sieve can be strengthened: instead of the mark composite, store the smallest prime divisor of each number. Then factoring any number up to the bound takes as many steps as it has factors: we divide by the found divisor, look in the table, and divide again. For eighty-four we get two, two, three and seven. Everything else follows from the factorization. The number of divisors is the product of the exponents, each increased by one: three times two times two, twelve in total. The sum of divisors and Euler's function are computed by similar formulas. For a single huge number a table is of no use, and the factorization is found by trial division up to the root.\n\nThe binomial coefficient, the number of ways to choose m elements out of n, equals the factorial of n divided by the product of the factorials of m and n minus m. The factorials themselves are huge, so the answer is requested modulo a prime. We cannot divide, but we can multiply by inverses: let us compute in advance all factorials modulo the prime and their inverses, find the inverse of the last one with the Fermat power, and get the others by walking backwards. After that each coefficient is computed in constant time. For five choose two modulo seven we get one times four times six, that is twenty-four, which is three; the real value ten also gives three.\n\nNow bits. An integer in memory is a set of zeros and ones, and bitwise operations work on all the digits at once: and, or, exclusive or, negation and shifts. A left shift by k multiplies by two to the power k, and a right shift divides. To check the bit with number i, shift the number and take the bitwise and with one. The expression x and x minus one clears the lowest one: for forty-four, that is one, zero, one, one, zero, zero, we get forty. This gives the check for a power of two, where the result equals zero, and counting ones in the number of steps equal to the number of ones, not the number of digits. The expression x and minus x leaves only the lowest one.\n\nA mask is a number in which each bit shows whether an element belongs to a set. A set of n elements is represented by numbers from zero to two to the power n minus one, so iterating over all subsets is a single loop over masks. Union, intersection and difference of sets are or, and, and and-not over the masks. To add an element, turn a bit on; to remove it, clear it. Subsets of a given mask are iterated with a trick: the next one equals the current one minus one, and the mask. Dynamic programming over masks stores the answer for every subset: the traveling salesman problem on fifteen cities is solved in two to the fifteenth times the square of the number of cities. Exclusive or also finds the single unpaired number.\n\nLet us sum up. Euclid's algorithm finds the greatest common divisor in logarithmic time, and its extended version gives inverse elements modulo a number. Remainders let us keep numbers within bounds, fast exponentiation takes a power in a logarithm's worth of multiplications, and matrices carry this technique over to recurrences. Trial division up to the root checks a single number, the sieve finds all primes up to a bound, and the table of smallest divisors factors numbers. Factorials with their inverses give binomial coefficients, and bits and masks speed up the iteration over subsets. In the exercises you will meet each of these techniques.
