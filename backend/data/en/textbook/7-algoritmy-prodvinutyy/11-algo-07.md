#CHAPTER 7 | Sliding window and divide and conquer
Structures and patterns. Chapter 7. Sliding window and divide and conquer. Two pointers taught us to walk through an array in a single pass. In this chapter there are two related patterns. The sliding window solves problems about consecutive chunks: substrings, subarrays, stretches of a log. Divide and conquer cuts a problem into independent parts, solves them separately and stitches the answer together; it stands behind merge sort, fast exponentiation and segment trees. We will see how to recognize these problems by their wording, how to build solutions in linear time and in n log n, and why sometimes they do not work. At the end, fourteen exercises are waiting for you.\n\nA sliding window is a continuous chunk of an array or a string, bounded by a left and a right boundary, that travels over the data like the frame of a camera lens. Window problems all sound alike: find the longest or the shortest chunk with some property, count chunks, maximize the sum of consecutive elements. The naive approach tries all pairs of boundaries, which is quadratic, and with a check of the property it is even cubic. The window makes one pass: at each step the right boundary adds a new element, and the left boundary moves when the window stops fitting. Every element enters the window once and leaves it once, so there are two n moves in total, which is linear time.\n\nLet us start with a window of fixed length. We are given an array and a number k, and we need the largest sum of k consecutive elements. We compute the sum of the first k. Then the window travels: the new element on the right is added, the element that left the window on the left is subtracted, and this costs a constant. There is no need to recompute the sum from scratch. Such an update of the state on every shift is called incremental, and it is the whole essence of the technique: the state of the window, whether a sum, a count or a set of characters, must be updated with one or two operations per boundary move. If answering a question about a chunk requires recomputing everything from zero, the window degenerates into a quadratic algorithm, so make sure the state is updated cheaply.\n\nNow the window of variable length. The classic problem is the longest substring without repeating characters. The right boundary walks along the string and adds a character. If that character is already in the window, the window is invalid, and the left boundary tightens, throwing out characters from the left until the repeat disappears. We remember every valid window as a candidate for the answer. The state of the window is a hash table or a set of the characters inside. Each character enters the window once and leaves it at most once, so the work is linear, even though there is another loop inside the loop. Do not be frightened by the nesting: count the total number of boundary moves, not the depth of the loops.\n\nFor the window to work, we need the property of monotonicity: expanding the window must change its validity in a predictable way. If a window already violates the condition, then any window that contains it violates it too, and then tightening the left boundary is correct: a bigger chunk will not save us. In the problem about the minimum length of a chunk with a sum of at least the target, with positive numbers, everything works: the sum grows when we expand and decreases when we shrink. The algorithm goes like this: expand the right boundary while the sum is less than the target; as soon as we reach the target, remember the length and tighten the left boundary while the condition still holds. Each element is passed twice, so again it is linear.\n\nThe window works wonderfully with character frequencies. The task is to find all the places in a string where an anagram of a given word starts. The window has the width of the word; inside we keep character counters. On each shift one letter is added and another is removed, and if the counters of the window match the counters of the word, an anagram is found. The update takes constant time, and the comparison can also be made cheap by counting how many letters already match. A more general problem is the minimum window of a text that contains all the letters of a pattern with their multiplicities. We expand the right boundary until we have collected everything needed, then tighten the left boundary until we lose a required letter. A counter of what is still needed saves us from recomputation.\n\nAn advanced technique is the maximum in every window of fixed length. Recomputing the maximum for each window is again quadratic. A beautiful solution keeps a double-ended queue of indices in which the values decrease. A new element arrives: we throw out from the end everyone who is smaller than it, since they will expire sooner and will never be maxima again. From the front we throw out the indices that have left the window. Then the maximum of the current window always sits at the head of the queue. Each index enters the queue once and leaves once, which is linear time. The trick of throwing out those who will certainly not become the answer is called a monotonic queue, and it is useful far beyond windows.\n\nWhen does the window not work? First: the property is not monotonic. If the array contains negative numbers, the sum can fall as we expand, tightening the left boundary makes no sense, and the window gives wrong answers. For sums with negative numbers, take prefix sums and a hash table, as in the chapter about hash tables. Second: you are looking not for consecutive chunks but for arbitrary subsets, and the window is powerless here. Third: you need not the best chunk but all of them, and there are quadratically many; then writing down the answer itself costs more than a linear pass. Ask yourself: are the chunks consecutive? Is the sum or the condition monotonic under expansion? Is the state updated in constant time?\n\nNow on to divide and conquer. The scheme has three steps: divide the problem into independent subproblems of the same kind, solve them recursively, and combine the solutions. We have already met it twice. Binary search splits the array in half and solves only one half. Merge sort splits in half, solves both halves and merges them. Quicksort, fast exponentiation and much more belong to the same family. The key word is independent: the subproblems must not share data or recompute the same thing. If they do overlap, you need to remember the results, and that is already dynamic programming, which we will come to later.\n\nHow do we estimate the time of such an algorithm? Draw the recursion tree. If the problem splits into two halves and the combining costs linear time, then on every level of the tree the total work is n, and there are a logarithm of levels: n log n in total. If there is one subproblem, as in binary search, and the combining is a constant, we get a logarithm. If there are two subproblems of half the size and the combining is a constant, then the tree has about n nodes, and everything costs linear time. This is a simplified form of the master theorem for recurrence relations; in practice the picture is enough: how many subproblems, how many times smaller, and how much the stitching costs.\n\nA classic example is the number of inversions in an array, that is, pairs where the left element is greater than the right one. Going through all pairs costs a square. But in merge sort, when merging two sorted halves, we know this: if an element of the right half goes into the result before the remaining elements of the left half, then it forms an inversion with each of them, and their number equals the count of elements still left on the left. We add that and move on. Counting along with sorting gives n log n instead of a square. The same template solves the maximum subarray sum problem: we split in half, and the best subarray lies on the left, on the right, or crosses the middle, and the crossing one is found by a linear pass from the middle in both directions.\n\nFast exponentiation is divide and conquer in miniature. To raise a number to the power n, raise it to the power of n halves and multiply the result by itself; if n is odd, add one more factor. The depth of the recursion is a logarithm, and instead of n multiplications we make about two logarithms. A thousand multiplications for the power of a thousand are replaced by a decade and a half. This is the basis of public-key cryptography, where the powers have hundreds of digits, and also of fast algorithms for Fibonacci numbers through the power of a matrix. The idea is the same everywhere: if you can compute a half and use it twice, you get a logarithm.\n\nThe extreme expression of divide and conquer is the segment tree. The array is split in half, the halves are split in half again, and every node stores an aggregate of its chunk: a sum, a minimum, a maximum. The answer to a query on any range is stitched together from a few ready nodes in logarithmic time, and updating an element also costs a logarithm, because only the nodes on the path from the leaf to the root are rebuilt. There may be thousands of queries per second and millions of elements, while the time per query is dozens of operations. The segment tree and its relatives live inside databases, game engines and analytics systems. We will build one in the exercises, and binary trees as such we will study in the following chapters.\n\nLearn to recognize the pattern by its wording. Words like substring, subarray and consecutive most likely mean a window. The longest or the shortest chunk with a condition means a variable-length window. The maximum in every window means a monotonic queue. Split into two parts and process them, or count the pairs that break at the merge, means divide and conquer. Many queries on ranges with updates means a segment tree. And if the subproblems overlap, it is dynamic programming. The ability to tell these cases apart saves hours, and it is useful to keep extending your own table of wordings as you solve problems.\n\nLet us sum up. The sliding window turns trying all chunks into a single pass, if the state is updated in constant time and expanding changes validity predictably; for negative numbers you need prefix sums. The monotonic queue gives the maximum of a window in linear time. Divide and conquer cuts a problem into independent parts; the time is estimated with the recursion tree: two halves and a linear merge give n log n. From this grow inversions, the segment tree and fast exponentiation. And when the subproblems overlap, you need memoization and dynamic programming. Fourteen exercises are waiting for you.
