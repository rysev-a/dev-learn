#CHAPTER 1 | The first program
Python as a first language. Chapter 1. The first program. Python was not chosen by accident: it reads almost like English, and the first programs in it look not like a secret code but like ordinary phrases. People write everything in it, from small scripts that rename files to neural networks and services used by millions. If you listened to the discrete mathematics course, that was the logic of reasoning: sets, relations, rigorous proofs. Here begins the language of commands: the thing that turns reasoning into the actions of a machine. The promise of this course is simple: every idea is reinforced right away by an exercise with a real code check. You won't just hear how a command works. You will write it yourself and find out at once whether it worked. And don't try to memorize everything: follow the logic, and memory will come with practice. By the end of the chapter you will have your own first program, even if it is the shortest one of your life.\n\nThe first command in any Python course is print. It does a simple thing: it prints on the screen whatever you give it. The name comes from the word for printing, but there is no paper here: the print head long ago turned into a line on the screen. The command has parentheses, just like a mathematical function. And inside those parentheses is what you want to show: a word, a number, a whole sentence. The parentheses tell Python where the thing to print begins and where it ends. It's like asking a colleague to show you a document: the request is the command, and the document is what stands inside the parentheses. While you have only one command, the program seems boring, but everything else starts with it.\n\nNow let's talk about what lies inside the parentheses: text in quotation marks is called a string. Both single and double quotes will do, and they are completely equal. The only rule is that the opening and the closing quote must be of the same kind, identical on both sides. Quotes are a fence: everything inside it, Python considers to be just text. Even if there are digits inside, it won't calculate anything. It will show them as they are, like a sign. If you write a two in quotes, it is no longer a number but a picture of a number, a symbol. To the eye there's no difference, but arithmetic won't work with such a picture. The habit of telling text from numbers is one of the most useful in programming, and it begins right here.\n\nWhat happens if you write several print commands in a row? The program carries them out one after another: from top to bottom, one line at a time. And everything is printed in the same order: first the first line, under it the second, then the third. No magic: the machine doesn't jump around or swap lines. This is a program in its purest form, a list of commands that the machine reads like a recipe. In a recipe, order matters too: first the dough, then the oven. Swap the steps and you get a different dish. Swap the commands and you get different text on the screen. Huge systems are built from simple steps like these.\n\nProgrammers have a special device, comments, and a hash sign makes them. Everything that comes after the hash sign on a line, Python ignores: it doesn't read it, doesn't run it, doesn't complain. These notes are addressed not to the machine but to people: to you a month from now, and to a colleague who opens the code today. In a month you will certainly forget why you wrote a particular line, and a comment will bring the thought back. A good comment explains why something is done this way, rather than retelling what is already visible. Writing here we print a line next to a print command is wasting your own time. But printing a hint so the user doesn't get lost is already a thought. The habit of commenting is the habit of respecting the reader, and most often that reader is you.\n\nPrint can do more than show ready-made text: it can calculate. Write two plus two inside the parentheses, and four appears on the screen. Python first does the arithmetic, and then prints the finished answer. The arithmetic signs are similar to the ones from school, with a couple of surprises. Plus and minus are the same, but multiplication is an asterisk, because there is no cross on the keyboard. Division is a slash, the slanted line that lives on the same key as the question mark. Parentheses work as they did in school: they change the order of operations, and what is inside is calculated first. Two plus two, times two, is six, while two times the sum of two and two is already eight.\n\nDivide seven by two, and Python answers: three and five tenths. It writes it in its own way: fractional numbers are written with a dot, not with a comma as in math class. Three point five is the ordinary three and a half, just with English habits. Behind this is something important: for Python, whole numbers and fractional numbers are two different kinds. It stores and calculates whole numbers one way and fractional numbers another way. And Python is honest to the end: divide six by two and you get three point zero, not just three. The result of division is always a fraction, even when mathematically it comes out as a round whole number. Remember this honesty: it will come in handy when we get to why two seemingly equal numbers suddenly turn out to be unequal.\n\nYou can give print several things at once: just list them inside the parentheses, separated by commas. It will print them all on one line, separated by spaces. This is handy when a value and an explanation should stand side by side: first the word sum, then the number itself. The screen immediately answers two questions: what it is and how much. This kind of output with an explanation is used constantly in development. When debugging a program, programmers print something like here is such and such on almost every line. The clearer the label, the easier it is later to make sense of your own printouts. One command, and you get a small report.\n\nAnd what if you need a quotation mark inside the text itself? We want to print the phrase Don't give up in quotes, and the outer quotes take the inner one for the end of the string. The solution is elegant: use quotes of a different kind on the outside. If there's a single quote inside, put double quotes outside, and Python will understand everything calmly. The fences must differ: then the inner quote won't be confused with the boundary of the text. It works the other way around too: double quotes inside, single quotes outside. There is one rule, and you can apply it in either direction. There are many small agreements like this in programming, and each one removes a whole family of problems.\n\nStrings can also do arithmetic, but their own kind. The asterisk repeats a string: three times la, and la la la appears on the screen. Need a divider line of thirty dashes? Multiply one dash by thirty, and the line is ready. Plus glues two strings into one: a word and a word become a single word. This is enough for dozens of small tasks: putting together a greeting with a name, making a repeating pattern. But remember the boundary: for strings, only plus and the asterisk work. You can't subtract strings, and you can't divide them either; Python will answer with an error. Every kind of thing in programming has its own set of actions, and for strings it is small but useful.\n\nPrint has one more trick: call it with nothing at all, with empty parentheses. An empty line appears on the screen: nothing is printed, but the line moves down. It sounds useless, but in fact it is a way to lay text out nicely. This is how you make gaps between blocks: a header, an empty line, the main message, another empty line, a signature. Letters are split into paragraphs in just the same way: the eye needs air, otherwise everything merges into a monolith. One empty line is a light pause, two are a noticeable break. Emptiness here is not an absence of meaning but a part of the design. Good output, like a good page, knows how to stay silent in the right places.\n\nNow about the fear of every beginner: errors. Forget a quote or lose a closing parenthesis, and Python will stop and honestly say: syntax error. It will show the number of the line where it stumbled, and often name the very spot. You want everything to work the first time, but an error is not a failure, it is a pointer. It doesn't say that you are a bad programmer; it says where to look. Experienced developers make mistakes dozens of times a day; they have just learned to read the messages quickly. The rule is simple: read the last message in the stack, because that is where the point is, and the rest is history. Read it, find the line, fix it, and run again. This is what real development looks like: not a flight without a scratch, but careful repairs on the go.\n\nLet's sum up. We have the print command, which shows on the screen what it is given. Strings live in quotes, which must be the same on both sides. Commands run from top to bottom, in the order they are written. The hash sign hides comments, notes for people. Arithmetic calculates numbers, and strings have their own small arithmetic: repeating and gluing. And we met errors for the first time, and saw that an error message is a hint, not a sentence. For now, every command has a short memory: it runs and immediately forgets everything it saw. In the next chapter we will teach the program to remember. Variables are ahead.
