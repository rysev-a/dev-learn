Databases. Chapter 22. Replication: copies, leaders and lag. A single database server is a single point of failure: the disk burns out and the data is gone; the queries outgrow what the server can handle and the service goes down. Replication solves both problems: data is copied to several machines. Copies give fault tolerance, because if one is lost the others keep working; they let you spread reads across machines; and they bring data closer to users in different countries. But copies have a price: they do not have to match at every moment. This whole chapter is about how copies are synchronized and what oddities appear until the synchronization is finished.\n\nThe most common scheme is a leader and followers. All writes go to one machine, the leader, which applies them to itself and records them in a replication log. The followers, also called replicas, read this log and apply the changes in the same order. Since the order is the same, the states of the replicas converge. You can read from any of them, but write only to the leader. This is how Postgres, MySQL and many other databases work. The scheme is simple and clear, and its main property is that every write has a single source of ordering: the leader.\n\nWhen does the leader confirm a write to the client? If it waits for confirmation from a follower, the replication is synchronous: the data is certainly on at least two machines, but every write is slower, and a failed follower blocks writing. If it does not wait, the replication is asynchronous: fast and tolerant to replica failures, but a confirmed write can vanish along with a leader that had no time to pass it on. The compromise is called semi-synchronous replication: one replica is synchronous, the rest are asynchronous. For critical data you need synchrony, and for everything else asynchronous replication is usually enough.\n\nWhat exactly is sent in the replication log? You can send the queries themselves, which is statement-level replication, but it breaks on nondeterministic functions: the current time and random numbers will give different results on each replica. You can send physical page changes from the write-ahead log: exact, but tied to the version and format of the storage. You can send logical changes: in this table, the row with this key received these values. Logical replication is more flexible, because it works between different versions and even between different systems, and it is the basis for streaming change data capture.\n\nNow the unpleasant side of asynchrony: replication lag. Time passes between a write on the leader and its appearance on the replica: usually milliseconds, but under load seconds or more. A user changed their name and immediately opened their profile; the request went to a lagging replica, and they see the old name. This violates the read-your-writes guarantee. Another example is monotonic reads: first a request hit a fresh replica, and the next one a lagging replica, and the data seems to roll back in time. And then there is ordering: the reply to a comment can appear before the comment itself, if they sit on different replicas.\n\nThere are practical techniques against these anomalies. To read your own writes, after a change you read from the leader, or you remember the log position the client has reached and read only from replicas that have already applied it. Monotonic reads are ensured by pinning a user to one replica: for example, choosing the replica by a hash of the user identifier. Consistent ordering is ensured by sending related writes to the same log or the same partition. These guarantees are weaker than full serializability, but they are enough for most interfaces, and they are cheap.\n\nA separate topic is leader failure. Replicas watch the leader, usually through periodic signals, and if no signals arrive for longer than a timeout, a switchover happens, called failover: a new leader is chosen, most often the freshest replica, and clients are directed to it. There are many pitfalls. The new leader may have lagged, and some confirmed writes are lost. The former leader, which has merely hung, may wake up and keep accepting writes: you get two leaders, a split brain. Against this, epoch numbers and fencing are introduced: every write carries an epoch, and the storage rejects writes from past epochs.\n\nThat is why automatic failover is both loved and feared. A timeout that is too short causes false switchovers on a brief load spike, and a false failover can itself cause downtime. One that is too long drags out the unavailability. Good practice is consensus: choosing a new leader through an algorithm like Raft, where the majority of nodes make the decision, and two leaders in the same epoch are impossible. Such a role is played by the internal mechanisms of many databases and by external coordination services. Later we will return to consensus in the chapter on consistency.\n\nWhat if you need to write in several places at once, for example in data centers on different continents? Here multi-leader replication is used: each data center accepts writes locally and forwards them to the others. The gain: write latency is minimal, and the centers survive losing contact with each other. But conflicts arise: two users changed the same record in different centers at the same time. They can be resolved by the last write wins principle, by time, which loses some changes; by merging the values, for example by taking the union of sets; or by handing the choice to the application. Data structures that merge without conflicts by construction are called CRDTs.\n\nThe third scheme is leaderless, in the spirit of Dynamo. The client writes to several replicas at once and reads from several, and consistency rests on quorums. Say there are N copies in total, a write is confirmed by W replicas, and a read queries R replicas. If W plus R is greater than N, any read will overlap with at least one replica that has the latest write, and we get a fresh value. Lagging replicas catch up in two ways: read repair, when a client that notices a stale copy writes the new value into it, and background reconciliation. And if a replica is unavailable, the write is temporarily stored on a neighbor and handed over later.\n\nHow do we tell which of two versions is newer if the clocks on the machines disagree? Vector clocks were invented for this: each version stores a counter for each node. Version A precedes version B if all of its counters are no greater and at least one is smaller; then B simply replaces A. If one counter is greater and another is smaller, the versions are concurrent: they arose independently, and this is a real conflict that has to be resolved by merging. This mechanism is more honest than wall-clock time, because clocks always lie a little, and it is the basis of many multi-leader systems.\n\nOperational practice. A new replica is created from a snapshot of the leader and then replays the log from the moment of the snapshot. Lag is measured in log bytes or seconds, and an alert is set on it. A special replica with a deliberate delay of an hour or two protects against human error: if someone dropped a table on the leader, the copy has not yet applied it. It is important to understand that replication does not replace backups: deletion or corruption of data instantly goes to all replicas, and only a backup with a log gives you a copy of a past state.\n\nLet us sum up. Replication gives fault tolerance, read scaling and closeness to users. A leader with followers is the simplest, but it requires a choice between synchronous and asynchronous transfer and produces lag, which is treated with guarantees such as reading your own writes. Failover requires care: epochs, fencing, consensus. Multiple leaders and leaderless schemes give write availability at the price of conflicts, and resolving them takes vector clocks, merging or CRDTs. Replication is not a replacement for backup.
