#CHAPTER 6 | Joining tables: JOIN
Databases. Chapter 6. Joining tables: join. We already know that the data in a database is spread across tables: customers separately, orders separately. But the questions are usually about several of them at once: how much Anna has bought, who has never ordered. To answer, you need to glue together rows from different tables. That is exactly what join does: it takes the rows of two tables and combines into one those pairs that are linked by a condition. Most often the condition is the equality of a foreign key and a primary key, that is, the customer number in the order and the number in the customers table.\n\nTo understand joins, let us start with the simplest case. If you join two tables with no condition at all, you get the Cartesian product: every row of the first table paired with every row of the second. Four customers and three orders give twelve rows, of which only three make sense. Sometimes you want such a join on purpose, for example to get all combinations of sizes and colors. But more often it happens by accident, when you forgot the condition, and then the query spits out a mountain of garbage. The join condition filters out the meaningful pairs.\n\nThe inner join is written like this: after from comes the first table, then the words inner join, the second table, the word on, and the condition. The word inner can be omitted. The condition links a foreign key to a primary key: the customer number in the order equals the customer number in the customers table. Only the pairs for which the condition is true get into the result. A customer without orders, and an order without a customer, which cannot happen because of the foreign key, will not appear in the result at all. This is the most common kind of join, and it is worth learning by heart.\n\nTwo tables can have columns with the same name: both the customer and the order have an id. If you just write id, the database will not understand which one you mean, and will raise an ambiguity error. To point at a column exactly, you put the table name and a dot in front of it. Long names are inconvenient to repeat, so tables are given short aliases: a single letter or an abbreviation. The alias is set after the table name in from, and from then on it works throughout the whole query. Good style is to always name the table for every column when joining.\n\nFirst the join, then the filter. To keep only the paid orders, you add a where clause with the status to the join. For an inner join it does not matter where the condition sits: in on or in where, the result is the same. The habit is this: in on you write the condition linking the tables, and in where the conditions on the rows of the result. That way the query reads more easily. But for outer joins the difference is huge, and we will see it very soon. So it is best to get used right away to a clear division of roles between on and where.\n\nThe inner join loses the customers who have no orders. If you need them, use the left join. It keeps all the rows of the left table, and if a row has no pair on the right, the columns of the right table are filled with nulls. The customers Vera and Gleb, who never ordered, stay in the result, and in the order columns they have blanks. A left join is a way of saying: show me all the customers and, if they have any, their orders. The order of the tables is essential here: the one whose rows must be kept goes on the left.\n\nThe left join has a classic use: finding rows with no match. Customers who never ordered; products that were never sold. To do this you make a left join and keep only the rows where the key of the right table is null. Since there is no pair, nothing was found on the right. The same result comes from the condition not exists with a nested query, and in many databases it works faster. But as a join, the query is often easier to read and easier to turn into a more general report.\n\nHere is a mistake that everybody makes. You want to show all customers and only their paid orders. You write a left join, and check the order status in where. But for a customer with no orders the status is null, the condition fails, and the customer disappears. The left join has quietly turned into an inner one. To keep the customers, the condition on the right table has to be moved into on: then it takes part in choosing the pair, but does not throw away the rows of the left table. Remember: a filter on the left table goes in where, and on the right table in on.\n\nThere are other kinds of outer joins too. Right join keeps all the rows of the right table: it is a mirror image of the left one, and it can always be replaced by swapping the tables. Full join keeps the rows of both tables: pairs exist where the condition held, and everything else is padded with nulls on both sides. In SQLite, right and full appeared only in version three thirty-nine, and before that they had to be imitated. Cross join, as we saw, gives all the pairs. The other kinds are met in real life less often than inner and left.\n\nTo find out who bought what, you need four tables: customers, orders, order lines and products. You join them in a chain: customers with orders, orders with lines, lines with products. Each next join adds its own link through on. The order of writing usually does not matter for the result, and the optimizer will choose the best execution order itself. The main thing is that every table has a link to the ones already connected, otherwise you get a Cartesian product. For readability, write each join on a new line.\n\nA one-to-many join multiplies rows. An order with three lines turns into three rows after the join, and the order total is repeated in each of them. If you now add up the order totals, you get three times too much. This is one of the most treacherous mistakes in reports. To avoid it, aggregate at the right level: first compute the totals in one table, then join. If you only need the number of distinct orders, use count distinct. Always check how many rows you got after the join, and compare with what you expected.\n\nA table can be joined to itself. A typical example is employees and bosses: every employee has the number of a boss, and the boss is also a record in the same table. To get the pairs employee and his boss, you take the table twice under different aliases. Without aliases the database would not understand which copy you mean. If an employee has no boss and we want to keep him, we use a left join. Category trees and any links inside a single entity are built the same way.\n\nLet us sum up. Join glues the rows of tables together by the condition in on. The inner join keeps only the pairs, the left join keeps all the rows of the left table and fills in what is missing with nulls. With a left join, put the filter on the right table in on, not in where. A null check on the right finds the rows with no pair. Tables are connected in a chain, and row multiplication has to be kept under control. You may come across the word using, which shortens the condition for columns with the same name, and natural join, which is best avoided: it joins on all matching names and breaks easily when the schema changes.
