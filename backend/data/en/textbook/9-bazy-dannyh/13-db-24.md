#CHAPTER 24 | Consistency: the CAP theorem, latency, and honest promises
Databases. Chapter 24. Consistency: the CAP theorem, latency, and honest promises. When data lives on several machines, a question arises: what does a client see when it reads it? Always the latest write, or sometimes an old one? What happens if part of the network falls away? Answers to such questions are called consistency models, and the model you choose determines speed, reliability, and the complexity of the application. In this chapter we will look at why there is no perfect model, what each of them guarantees, how clocks and the ordering of events relate to consistency, and how machines come to an agreement with each other, that is, consensus.\n\nWe have to start with the fact that the network is unreliable. Messages get lost, delayed, duplicated, and arrive out of order. A machine may not answer, and you cannot tell whether it crashed, is overloaded, or the network to it simply broke. Failures are partial: part of the system works, part does not, and different participants see different pictures. So in a distributed system you cannot assume that an operation either definitely happened or definitely did not: there is a third outcome, unknown. All the hard things in this chapter come from this ignorance.\n\nThe CAP theorem states the basic trade-off. Consistency means everyone sees the same data, as if there were a single copy. Availability means every request to a live node gets a response. Partition tolerance means the system keeps working even when the network splits into pieces. A network partition is unavoidable, so in practice you choose between the other two: while the network is partitioned, the system either refuses to serve some requests for the sake of consistency, or answers, possibly with stale data, for the sake of availability. When there is no partition, the choice is different: latency or consistency. This refinement is called PACELC.\n\nThe strongest model is linearizability. The system behaves as if there were a single copy of the data, and each operation takes effect instantly at some moment between the start and the end of its execution. If a write has completed, any read that started later must see it. This is intuitive and convenient for the programmer: you can reason as if about a single variable. But such a guarantee has a price: operations have to be coordinated between nodes, and that costs time and causes unavailability during a partition. Linearizability is needed where who goes first matters: unique names, locks, stock balances.\n\nWeaker models are cheaper. Sequential consistency requires a single global order of operations that respects the order within each client, but does not require any link to real time: a read may see the past, as long as the order stays single. Causal consistency requires only that a cause be visible before its effect: if a reply was written in response to a message, nobody should see the reply without the question. Independent events can be seen in any order. Causal consistency is the strongest model that remains available during a network partition, which is why it is valued in distributed systems.\n\nThe weakest guarantee is eventual consistency. If new writes stop, then over time all replicas arrive at the same value. While writes continue, readers may see anything from the past, and even different values. A familiar example is the domain name system: a change spreads across the world not instantly. For likes and view counters this is enough; for a bank balance it is not. It is important that eventual is only a promise of convergence, not of speed and not of order; without additional merge rules, conflicts get resolved arbitrarily.\n\nQuorums, which we discussed in the chapter on replication, give only partial guarantees. Even when writes and reads overlap, concurrent operations can lead to ambiguity, and sloppy quorums, where a write is accepted by any available neighbor, sacrifice consistency for availability. That is why systems like Dynamo are described as eventually consistent, and for strict guarantees they need additional mechanisms: versions, merges, and sometimes consensus for individual operations. The phrase we use quorums does not mean linearizability.\n\nNone of this can be discussed without time. Computer clocks drift apart: a synchronization protocol brings the difference down to milliseconds, but not to zero, and clocks sometimes jump backwards. So timestamps cannot reliably tell which write is newer. The way out is logical clocks. A Lamport clock assigns a number to each event: when sending, we increment the counter; when receiving, we take the maximum of our own and the incoming one and add one. This way, if event A influenced B, A has the smaller number. Hybrid clocks combine physical time and a logical counter. And Spanner uses physical clocks with a known error bound and waits out that error.\n\nNow the main thing: consensus. Several nodes need to agree on a single value, for example who the leader is now, or which entry is next in the log, despite failures and lost messages. It is impossible to make this both fast and failure-proof under arbitrary delays, but practical algorithms, Paxos and Raft, guarantee safety always, and progress when the network works well enough. The decision is made by a majority: to survive f failures, you need two f plus one nodes. Any two majorities overlap, so two different decisions are impossible.\n\nRaft is simpler than the others. Time is divided into terms, with at most one leader in each. A node that has not heard from a leader becomes a candidate and collects votes from a majority; votes are given only to those whose log is not behind. The leader accepts writes, appends them to its log, and sends them to the followers; an entry is considered committed once it is written on a majority of nodes. After that it can be applied and the client can be answered, and it will not be lost even if the leader changes. Raft powers the coordinators of many systems and databases such as CockroachDB itself.\n\nWhat should an application do if the network can lose a response? Repeat the request, but then the operation may run twice. That is why people talk about delivery guarantees: at most once loses messages, at least once duplicates them, and exactly once is in practice assembled from at-least-once delivery and idempotent processing: every operation has a unique identifier, and a repeat with the same identifier does not change the result. Another technique is the outbox pattern: write to an events table in the same transaction as the change, and a separate process reliably sends the events.\n\nHow do you check that a system keeps its promises? Testing by hand is impossible: bugs appear on rare interleavings of events. For this, people use tools like Jepsen, which run a workload, break the network and nodes, and check the history of operations against a model, for example linearizability. Chaos engineering in production systems deliberately switches off machines and partitions the network. Observability shows replica lag, leader elections, and errors. Reports of consistency violations in real databases are a reason to read the guarantees documentation, not only the marketing.\n\nLet's sum up. The network is unreliable, and in a distributed system the outcome unknown is possible. The CAP theorem says that during a partition you have to choose between consistency and availability, and PACELC adds the choice between latency and consistency. The models run from linearizability to causal and eventual: the weaker, the faster and more available, but the more complex the application. Time should be handled with logical clocks, and agreement reached through majority consensus. Idempotency and the outbox turn unreliable delivery into an exactly-once effect. Next come two practical patterns: the cache, and separating transactional from analytical load.
