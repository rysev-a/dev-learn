#CHAPTER 2 | Filtering Data: WHERE
Databases. Chapter two. Filtering data: WHERE. Usually we are interested not in the whole table, but only in a part of it: expensive products, orders for May, customers from Kazan. The condition is written after the word FROM, introduced by the word WHERE. The database checks the condition for every row and keeps only those for which it is true. The rest simply do not make it into the result. The data itself does not change: filtering is a question, not a deletion. In this chapter we will look at what conditions are built from: comparisons, logical connectives, lists, ranges, and patterns.\n\nThe simplest conditions are comparisons. Equality in SQL is written with one equals sign, not two as in many programming languages. Inequality is two angle brackets facing each other, or an exclamation mark followed by an equals sign. There are also less than, greater than, less than or equal, and greater than or equal. Numbers are compared by value, strings alphabetically, and a date written as text is compared as a string. The result of a comparison for each row is true or false, and the database uses it to decide whether to keep the row.\n\nSimple conditions can be combined. The word AND requires both conditions to hold at once. With the word OR, it is enough for at least one to hold. The word NOT flips the meaning: what was true becomes false. For example, products from the Books category that cost less than fifteen hundred, or all the orders that are not cancelled. There can be as many conditions in a chain as you like. When there are many, it is better to write each on a new line: that way the query reads like a list of requirements rather than a puzzle.\n\nLogical operations have precedence, just like arithmetic. AND binds tighter than OR: the condition A or B and C reads as A, or both B and C at the same time. This is what breaks queries most often. Say you want paid or shipped orders worth more than ten thousand. If you write three conditions in a row without parentheses, only the shipped orders will have to be expensive, while the paid ones pass with any amount. The fix is simple: put parentheses around the OR. They cost nothing, and the reader immediately sees what you meant.\n\nWhen you need to check whether a value equals one of several, the word IN with a list in parentheses is more convenient. The condition city in Moscow, Kazan, or Tver replaces a chain of three comparisons joined by OR. It works for numbers too. The opposite is NOT IN: the value is not in the list. The list can be as long as you like, and it can even be the result of a nested query, but we will talk about that in chapter seven. There is one subtlety with empty values, and we will sort it out in chapter four.\n\nFor ranges there is the word BETWEEN. The condition price between one thousand and five thousand is equivalent to a pair of comparisons: greater than or equal to one thousand, and less than or equal to five thousand. Both bounds are included in the range, and this is worth remembering: a product costing exactly one thousand will appear in the result. The lower bound must come first, otherwise the range turns out empty. You can also write NOT BETWEEN, and then the values outside the range remain. BETWEEN works not only with numbers but also with strings and dates.\n\nSQLite has no separate type for dates: they are stored as text in the format year, month, day, separated by hyphens. This format is convenient because the alphabetical order of the strings matches the chronological order. So dates can be compared with ordinary operators and used in BETWEEN. You only need to keep to the format: months and days with a leading zero, otherwise the comparison breaks. Orders after the first of May are those with a date greater than the fifth month, first day, written as a string in quotes. As for time and time zones, those have to be agreed on separately.\n\nFor pattern search there is the word LIKE. In a pattern, the percent sign stands for any sequence of characters, including an empty one, and the underscore stands for exactly one character. So K followed by percent will find everything that starts with the letter K, and percent followed by O-V will find everything that ends in O-V. A percent sign on both sides of a word looks for it anywhere in the string. The underscore is handy when the format is known: five arbitrary characters, or a letter and then a digit. A query whose pattern starts with a percent sign usually has to read the whole table, and that is worth remembering.\n\nThere are a few subtleties. In SQLite, LIKE by default ignores the case of Latin letters, but for Cyrillic, without extra settings, it does distinguish case, and that is easy to forget. If you need a strict case-sensitive comparison, there is the GLOB operator with different wildcard characters. Negation is written NOT LIKE. And if you need to find a percent sign or an underscore itself, you put a special character in front of it and name it with the word ESCAPE. These cases are rare, but when they come up, it is hard to guess the answer yourself.\n\nIn a condition you can use not only columns and numbers but also expressions. For example, the value of the stock, price times quantity on hand, is greater than one hundred thousand. Or the remainder of dividing the ID by two equals zero, and then we get the even IDs. You can compare two columns of the same row: the quantity sold is greater than the stock in the warehouse. The only requirement is that the expression can be computed for every row. Remember that such a condition is evaluated anew for each row, and the more complex it is, the more work the database has to do.\n\nIt is important to understand the order in which the database executes a query. First it takes the table from FROM, then it selects rows with the condition, and only after that does it compute the expressions from SELECT. A practical consequence follows: an alias defined in the column list does not yet exist in the condition. If you need a condition on a computed value, repeat the whole expression, or wrap the query in an outer one. This is one of the most common misunderstandings among beginners, and the error message saying there is no such column usually appears because of it.\n\nComplex conditions are not written all at once. First you formulate them in words: electronics or accessories, more than a thousand, and the product is in stock. Then you write and test each requirement in turn, as a separate query. After that you join them with AND and OR and place the parentheses. At every step, look at how many rows are left: if it suddenly became zero or everything, the mistake is in the condition you added last. This habit saves a lot of time and is especially useful when the table is big and you cannot check it by eye.\n\nLet us sum up. WHERE keeps the rows for which the condition is true. It is built from comparisons, joined with the words AND, OR, and NOT, and for lists, ranges, and patterns there are IN, BETWEEN, and LIKE. AND is stronger than OR, so parentheses are your friends. Dates in SQLite are compared as strings in ISO format. The condition runs before SELECT, so aliases are not visible to it. In the next chapter we will learn to order the result and take only the number of rows we need, and then we will move on to the trickiest topic, empty values.
