#CHAPTER 27 | Schema design: keys, normal forms and denormalization
Databases. Chapter 27. Schema design: keys, normal forms and denormalization. A bad query can be rewritten in an evening, but a bad schema with a billion rows takes months to redo. The schema decides what data you can store at all, which errors the database will catch on its own, and how expensive future changes will be. In this chapter we will go from the problem domain to tables: entities and relationships, keys, types, normal forms and constraints. Then we will look at when it is worth breaking normalization on purpose, and how to handle typical tasks such as trees, history and many tenants.\n\nYou start with entities: the concepts of the problem domain that you need to remember. In a shop these are the customer, the product and the order. Every entity has attributes and an identifier. Then you describe the relationships. One-to-one is rare: a user profile kept apart from the account. One-to-many is expressed by a foreign key on the side of the many: the order holds the number of the customer. Many-to-many needs a junction table, where each row connects two objects and can hold attributes of the relationship itself, for example a quantity or a date. A good sign: every fact has exactly one natural place in the schema.\n\nA key is a column, or a set of columns, that uniquely identifies a row. A natural key comes from the problem domain: a passport number, a country code. A surrogate key is created by the database: an auto-increment number or a universally unique identifier. Natural keys break when the real world changes: a number is reissued, a country is renamed. That is why the primary key is usually a surrogate one, and the natural key is secured with a uniqueness constraint. Random identifiers are handy in distributed systems, but they get along badly with the B-tree, because inserts are scattered all over the index; time-ordered variants do not have this drawback.\n\nData types are also part of the schema. Money must not be stored in floating-point numbers: three times ten cents does not equal exactly thirty. Use an integer number of cents or an exact decimal type. Time is stored in a single time zone, in a format that includes the zone, and shown to the user in their own zone. For strings, set a sensible limit. For small sets of values, a check constraint or a lookup table works better than free text. A document column, such as JSON, is fine for rare unstructured attributes, but not for anything you search and join on every day.\n\nNow let us see why normalization is needed. Take one flat table of orders, where each row holds the order number, the customer, the customer's city, the product and the product price. The data is duplicated: the customer's city repeats in every row of their orders. This gives three anomalies. The update anomaly: the customer moved, and you have to fix hundreds of rows, and a single missed row creates a contradiction. The insert anomaly: you cannot record a new customer until they have an order. The delete anomaly: you delete the last order, and the information about the customer disappears with it. Normalization is a way to get rid of these anomalies by decomposing the table.\n\nThe formal basis is the functional dependency: the values of one set of columns uniquely determine the values of another set. The customer number determines the city, the product number determines the price. The first normal form requires atomic values: a cell must not hold a comma-separated list, or repeating groups of columns like product one, product two. The second form concerns composite keys: every non-key column must depend on the whole key, not on a part of it. If the key is the pair of order and product, then the product price depends only on the product and must move to a separate table.\n\nThe third normal form forbids transitive dependencies: a non-key column must not depend on another non-key column. If an order holds a customer and the customer's city, then the city depends on the customer, not on the order, and belongs in the customers table. A short formula: every column depends on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key. The stronger Boyce-Codd form requires that the determinant of every dependency be a candidate key. The differences are subtle and show up only with several overlapping keys, so in practice people stop at the third form.\n\nDecomposition must be lossless: after joining the parts you get exactly the original table, with no extra rows and no lost rows. This is guaranteed when the shared columns of the parts form a key of at least one of them. There is one more requirement: dependencies should be preserved, so that they can be enforced with constraints. The higher forms, the fourth and the fifth, remove independent multivalued dependencies: for example, an employee has several skills and several languages, and keeping them in one table means multiplying them together. The solution is the same: independent facts go into separate tables.\n\nA schema is needed not only for storage but also for protection. Constraints move the rules into the database, where they cannot be bypassed. Not-null, uniqueness, a condition check, and a foreign key with well-defined behavior on delete: forbid it, cascade the delete, or set the reference to null. Some databases can defer the check until commit, and then you can insert mutually linked rows in any order. The rule is simple: any invariant that can be expressed as a constraint is better expressed as a constraint. Applications change and multiply, while the database remains the only shared guardian of integrity.\n\nNormalization is not a dogma. Reading a highly normalized schema requires joins, and sometimes duplication is justified. People store a precomputed order total, a comment counter, a copy of the product name at the time of purchase. But every copy is an obligation to keep it consistent: with a trigger, an application transaction or a background recalculation. Denormalize deliberately, based on measurements, when there is a proven speed problem, and write down where the truth comes from. By the way, a copy of the product name in an order is not a violation but a fact: the price and the name at the time of purchase must not change retroactively.\n\nThere are typical tasks. Soft delete marks a row as deleted instead of really deleting it, but then uniqueness and queries have to take that into account. The history of changes is stored as versions with a validity period, or in a separate audit table. Trees are stored in different ways: an adjacency list, where a row has a reference to its parent, is good for inserts; a materialized path is convenient for selecting a subtree; a closure table stores all ancestor and descendant pairs. Tags are solved with a junction table. The entity-attribute-value antipattern, which keeps everything in three columns, deprives the database of types, constraints and speed.\n\nMany products serve many customers, called tenants, in one database. The simplest option is a tenant column in all tables, included in keys, indexes and foreign keys, so that rows of different tenants never mix. For strict isolation you use a separate schema or a separate database per tenant, but the price is the complexity of migrations and the number of connections. A schema must be able to evolve: so it is better to add columns than to change the meaning of existing ones, and not to hide extra obligations in names and types. We will talk about changing a schema without stopping the service in the chapter on migrations.\n\nLet us sum up. A schema starts with entities and relationships; a surrogate key plus a natural key under a uniqueness constraint is a reliable choice. Money is stored as integers or exact decimals, time in a single zone. Normal forms remove update, insert and delete anomalies: every fact is stored once. Decomposition must be lossless, and constraints must move the rules into the database. Denormalize deliberately and with an owner of the truth.
